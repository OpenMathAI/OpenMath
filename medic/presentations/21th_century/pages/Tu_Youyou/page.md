**Tu Youyou** ([Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_language "Chinese language"): 屠呦呦; [pinyin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pinyin "Pinyin"): *Tú Yōuyōu*; born 30 December 1930) is a [Nobel Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physiology_or_Medicine "Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine")-winning Chinese [pharmaceutical chemist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medicinal_chemistry "Medicinal chemistry"). She discovered [artemisinin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artemisinin "Artemisinin") (also known as *qīnghāosù*, 青蒿素) and [dihydroartemisinin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dihydroartemisinin "Dihydroartemisinin"), used to treat [malaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaria "Malaria"); these discoveries were a breakthrough in twentieth-century [tropical medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropical_medicine "Tropical medicine"), saving millions of lives in [South China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_China "South China"), [Southeast Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southeast_Asia "Southeast Asia"), Africa, and South America. Tu was born, educated and carried out her research exclusively in China.

For her work, Tu received the 2011 [Lasker Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Lasker_Award_for_Clinical_Medical_Research "Albert Lasker Award for Clinical Medical Research") in clinical medicine and the 2015 [Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physiology_or_Medicine "Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine") jointly with [William C. Campbell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_C._Campbell_(scientist) "William C. Campbell (scientist)") and [Satoshi Ōmura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satoshi_Ōmura "Satoshi Ōmura"). Tu is the first Chinese [Nobel laureate in Physiology or Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physiology_or_Medicine "Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine") and the first female citizen of the [People's Republic of China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/China "China") to receive a Nobel Prize in any category. She is also the first Chinese person to receive the [Lasker Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lasker_Award "Lasker Award").

Tu was bestowed the [Medal of the Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medal_of_the_Republic_(China) "Medal of the Republic (China)"), the highest honorary medal of the People's Republic of China, in September 2019.

## Early life

Tu was born in [Ningbo, Zhejiang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ningbo,_Zhejiang "Ningbo, Zhejiang"), China, on 30 December 1930.

> My [first] name, Youyou, was given by my father, who adapted it from the sentence 呦呦鹿鳴, 食野之蒿 translated as "Deer bleat *youyou* while eating wild [Hao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artemisia_(genus) "Artemisia (genus)")" in the [Chinese *Book of Odes*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classic_of_Poetry "Classic of Poetry"). How this links my whole life with [qinghao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qinghao "Qinghao") will probably remain an interesting [coincidence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aptronym "Aptronym") forever.

— Tu Youyou, when interviewed in 2011 after being awarded the 2011 [Lasker-DeBakey Clinical Medical Research Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lasker-DeBakey_Clinical_Medical_Research_Award "Lasker-DeBakey Clinical Medical Research Award")

She attended [Xiaoshi Middle School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xiaoshi_Middle_School "Xiaoshi Middle School") for junior high school and the first year of high school, before transferring to [Ningbo Middle School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ningbo_Middle_School "Ningbo Middle School") in 1948. A tuberculosis infection interrupted her high-school education, but inspired her to go into medical research.

From 1951 to 1955, she attended Beijing Medical College (now [Peking University Health Science Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peking_University_Health_Science_Center "Peking University Health Science Center")). In 1955, Youyou Tu graduated from Beijing Medical University School of Pharmacy and continued her research on Chinese herbal medicine in the China Academy of Chinese Medical Sciences. Tu studied at the Department of Pharmaceutical Sciences, and graduated in 1955. Later Tu was trained for two and a half years in [traditional Chinese medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_Chinese_medicine "Traditional Chinese medicine").

After graduation, Tu worked at the Academy of Traditional Chinese Medicine (now the China Academy of Traditional Chinese Medical Sciences) in Beijing.

## Research career

Tu carried on her work in the 1960s and 70s, including during China's [Cultural Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultural_Revolution "Cultural Revolution").

### Schistosomiasis

During her early years in research, Tu studied *[Lobelia chinensis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lobelia_chinensis "Lobelia chinensis")*, a traditional Chinese medicine believed to be useful for treating [schistosomiasis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schistosomiasis "Schistosomiasis"), caused by [trematodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trematoda "Trematoda") which infect the [urinary tract](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urinary_tract "Urinary tract") or the [intestines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intestines "Intestines"), which was widespread in the first half of the 20th century in South China.

### Malaria

In 1967, during the [Vietnam War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnam_War "Vietnam War"), President [Ho Chi Minh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ho_Chi_Minh "Ho Chi Minh") of [North Vietnam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Vietnam "North Vietnam") asked Chinese Premier [Zhou Enlai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zhou_Enlai "Zhou Enlai") for help in developing a malaria treatment for his soldiers trooping down the [Ho Chi Minh trail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ho_Chi_Minh_trail "Ho Chi Minh trail"), where a majority came down with a form of malaria which is resistant to [chloroquine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloroquine "Chloroquine"). Because malaria was also a major cause of death in China's southern provinces, especially [Guangdong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guangdong "Guangdong") and [Guangxi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guangxi "Guangxi"), Zhou Enlai convinced [Mao Zedong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mao_Zedong "Mao Zedong") to set up a secret drug discovery project named [Project 523](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Project_523 "Project 523") after its starting date, 23 May 1967.

In early 1969, Tu was appointed head of the [Project 523](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Project_523 "Project 523") research group at her institute. Tu was initially sent to Hainan, where she studied patients who had been infected with the disease.

Scientists worldwide had screened over 240,000 compounds without success. In 1969, Tu, then 38 years old, had an idea of screening Chinese herbs. She first investigated the Chinese medical classics in history, visiting practitioners of [traditional Chinese medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_Chinese_medicine "Traditional Chinese medicine") all over the country on her own. She gathered her findings in a notebook called *A Collection of Single Practical Prescriptions for Anti-Malaria*. Her notebook summarized 640 prescriptions. By 1971, her team had screened over 2,000 traditional Chinese recipes and made 380 herbal [extracts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extract "Extract"), from some 200 herbs, which were tested on mice.

One compound was particularly effective, sweet wormwood (*[Artemisia annua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artemisia_annua "Artemisia annua")*), which was used for "intermittent fevers," a hallmark of malaria. As Tu also presented at the project seminar, its preparation was described in a recipe from a 1,600-year-old [traditional Chinese herbal medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_herbology "Chinese herbology") text titled *Emergency Prescriptions Kept Up One's Sleeve*. At first, it was ineffective because they extracted it with the traditional technique using hot water. Tu discovered that a low-temperature extraction process could be used to isolate an effective antimalarial substance from the plant; Tu says she was influenced by the source, written in 340 by [Ge Hong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ge_Hong "Ge Hong"), a scholar from the [Jin dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jin_dynasty_(266–420) "Jin dynasty (266–420)"), which states that this herb should be steeped in cold water. This book instructed the reader to immerse a handful of *qinghao* in water, wring out the juice, and drink it all. Since hot water damages the active ingredient in the plant, she proposed a method using low temperature ether to extract the effective compound instead. [Animal tests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animal_testing "Animal testing") showed it was effective in mice and monkeys.

In 1972, she and her colleagues obtained the pure substance and named it *qinghaosu* ([青蒿素](https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/青蒿素 "zh:青蒿素")), or [artemisinin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artemisinin "Artemisinin") in English. This substance has now saved millions of lives, especially in the [developing world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Developing_world "Developing world"). Tu also studied the [chemical structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_structure "Chemical structure") and pharmacology of artemisinin. Tu's group first determined the chemical structure of artemisinin. In 1973, Tu was attempting to confirm the [carbonyl group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbonyl_group "Carbonyl group") in the artemisinin molecule when she accidentally synthesized [dihydroartemisinin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dihydroartemisinin "Dihydroartemisinin").

Tu volunteered to be the first human test subject. "As head of this research group, I had the responsibility," she said. She had no bad reactions, so she conducted successful clinical trials with human patients. Her work was published anonymously in 1977. In 1981, she presented the findings related to artemisinin at a meeting with the [World Health Organization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Health_Organization "World Health Organization").

Tu was awarded the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine on 5 October 2015 "for her discoveries concerning a novel therapy against Malaria."

## Later career

Tu Youyou was promoted to Researcher (研究员, the highest researcher rank in mainland China equivalent to the [academic rank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_academic_ranks "List of academic ranks") of a [full professor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Professor_(highest_academic_rank) "Professor (highest academic rank)")) in 1980, shortly after the beginning of the [reform and opening up](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reform_and_opening_up "Reform and opening up") in 1978. In 2001, she was promoted to academic advisor for doctoral candidates. As of 2023, she is the chief scientist of the China Academy of Chinese Medical Sciences under the [National Administration of Traditional Chinese Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Administration_of_Traditional_Chinese_Medicine "National Administration of Traditional Chinese Medicine").

As of 2007, her office is in an old [apartment building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apartment_building "Apartment building") in [Dongcheng District](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dongcheng_District,_Beijing "Dongcheng District, Beijing"), Beijing.

Tu is regarded as the "Three-Without Scientist" – no postgraduate degree (there was no postgraduate education then in China), no study or research experience abroad, and not a member of either of the Chinese national academies, the [Chinese Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_Academy_of_Sciences "Chinese Academy of Sciences") and [Chinese Academy of Engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_Academy_of_Engineering "Chinese Academy of Engineering"). Tu is now regarded as a representative figure of the first generation of Chinese doctors since the [establishment of the People's Republic of China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proclamation_of_the_People's_Republic_of_China "Proclamation of the People's Republic of China") in 1949.
