**Paul Christian Lauterbur** (May 6, 1929 – March 27, 2007) was an American [chemist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemist "Chemist") who shared the [Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physiology_or_Medicine "Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine") in 2003 with [Peter Mansfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Mansfield "Peter Mansfield") for his work which made the development of [magnetic resonance imaging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_resonance_imaging "Magnetic resonance imaging") (MRI) possible.

Lauterbur was a professor at [Stony Brook University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stony_Brook_University "Stony Brook University") from 1963 until 1985, where he conducted his research for the development of the [MRI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MRI "MRI"). In 1985 he became a professor along with his wife Joan Dawson at the [University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Illinois_at_Urbana-Champaign "University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign") for 22 years until his death in [Urbana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urbana,_Illinois "Urbana, Illinois"). He and Dawson established the Biomedical Magnetic Resonance Laboratory (BMRL) there. He never stopped working with undergraduates on research, and he served as a professor of chemistry, with appointments in bioengineering, biophysics, the College of Medicine at Urbana-Champaign and computational biology at the Center for Advanced Study.

## Early life

Lauterbur was of [Luxembourgish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luxembourgers "Luxembourgers") ancestry. Born and raised in [Sidney, Ohio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidney,_Ohio "Sidney, Ohio"), Lauterbur graduated from [Sidney High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidney_High_School_(Ohio) "Sidney High School (Ohio)"), where a new Chemistry, Physics, and Biology wing was dedicated in his honor. As a [teenager](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teenager "Teenager"), he built his own [laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laboratory "Laboratory") in the basement of his parents' house. His [chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemistry "Chemistry") teacher at school understood that he enjoyed experimenting on his own, so the teacher allowed him to do his own experiments at the back of class.

When he was drafted into the [United States Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Army "United States Army") in the 1950s, his superiors allowed him to spend his time working on an early [nuclear magnetic resonance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_magnetic_resonance "Nuclear magnetic resonance") (NMR) machine; he had published four scientific papers by the time he left the Army. Paul became an [atheist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atheist "Atheist") later on.

## Education and career

Lauterbur received a BS in chemistry from the Case Institute of Technology, now part of [Case Western Reserve University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Case_Western_Reserve_University "Case Western Reserve University") in [Cleveland, Ohio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cleveland,_Ohio "Cleveland, Ohio") where he became a Brother of the Alpha Delta chapter of [Phi Kappa Tau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phi_Kappa_Tau "Phi Kappa Tau") fraternity. He then went to work at the Mellon Institute laboratories of the [Dow Corning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dow_Corning "Dow Corning") Corporation, with a 2-year break to serve at the Army Chemical Center in [Edgewood, Maryland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edgewood,_Maryland "Edgewood, Maryland"). While working at Mellon Institute he pursued graduate studies in chemistry at the [University of Pittsburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Pittsburgh "University of Pittsburgh"). Earning his PhD in 1962, the following year Lauterbur accepted a position as associate professor at [Stony Brook University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stony_Brook_University "Stony Brook University"). As a visiting faculty in chemistry at [Stanford University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford_University "Stanford University") during the 1969–1970 academic year, he undertook NMR-related research with the help of local businesses [Syntex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syntex "Syntex") and [Varian Associates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Varian_Associates "Varian Associates"). Lauterbur returned to Stony Brook, continuing there until 1985 when he moved to the [University of Illinois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Illinois "University of Illinois").

## The development of the MRI

Lauterbur credits the idea of the MRI to a brainstorm one day at a suburban [Pittsburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pittsburgh,_Pennsylvania "Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania") [Eat'n Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eat'n_Park "Eat'n Park") [Big Boy Restaurant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Boy_Restaurants "Big Boy Restaurants"), with the MRI's first model scribbled on a table napkin while he was a student and researcher at both the [University of Pittsburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Pittsburgh "University of Pittsburgh") and the [Mellon Institute of Industrial Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mellon_Institute_of_Industrial_Research "Mellon Institute of Industrial Research"). The further research that led to the Nobel Prize was performed at [Stony Brook University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stony_Brook_University "Stony Brook University") in the 1970s.

The [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics") in 1952, which went to [Felix Bloch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Felix_Bloch "Felix Bloch") and [Edward Purcell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Mills_Purcell "Edward Mills Purcell"), was for the development of [nuclear magnetic resonance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_magnetic_resonance "Nuclear magnetic resonance") (NMR), the scientific principle behind MRI. However, for decades magnetic resonance was used mainly for studying the [chemical structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_structure "Chemical structure") of substances. It wasn't until the 1970s with Lauterbur's and Mansfield's developments that NMR could be used to produce images of the body.

Lauterbur used the idea of [Robert Gabillard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Gabillard?action=edit&redlink=1 "Robert Gabillard") (developed in his doctoral thesis, 1952) of introducing gradients in the [magnetic field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_field "Magnetic field") which allows for determining the origin of the [radio waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_wave "Radio wave") emitted from the [nuclei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_nucleus "Atomic nucleus") of the object of study. This spatial information allows two-dimensional pictures to be produced.

[Raymond Damadian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raymond_Damadian "Raymond Damadian")'s 1971 *Science* paper on his observation of T1 and T2 differences in cancerous tissue was influential, as Lauterbur wrote in 1986, "… the attention of the medical community was first attracted by the report of Damadian that some animal tumors have remarkably long water proton relaxation times... I wondered whether there might be some way to noninvasively map out such quantities within the body." Prompted by Damadian's report on the potential medical uses of NMR, Lautebur expanded on [Herman Carr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herman_Carr "Herman Carr")'s technique on creating a one-dimensional magnetic resonance (MR) image to develop a way to generate the first MRI images, in 2D and 3D, using gradients.

While Lauterbur conducted his work at Stony Brook, the best NMR machine on campus belonged to the chemistry department; he had to visit it at night to use it for experimentation and would carefully change the settings so that they would return to those of the chemists' as he left. The original MRI machine is located at the Chemistry building on the campus of Stony Brook University in [Stony Brook, New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stony_Brook,_New_York "Stony Brook, New York").

Some of the first images taken by Lauterbur included those of a 4-mm-diameter clam his daughter had collected on the beach at the [Long Island Sound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_Island_Sound "Long Island Sound"), green peppers and two test tubes of heavy water within a beaker of ordinary water; no other imaging technique in existence at that time could distinguish between two different kinds of water. This last achievement is particularly important as the human body consists mostly of water.

When Lauterbur first submitted his paper with his discoveries to *[Nature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nature_(journal) "Nature (journal)")*, the paper was rejected by the editors of the journal. Lauterbur persisted and requested them to review it again, upon which time it was published and is now acknowledged as a classic *Nature* paper. The *Nature* editors pointed out that the pictures accompanying the paper were too fuzzy, although they were the first images to show the difference between heavy water and ordinary water. Lauterbur said of the initial rejection: "You could write the entire history of science in the last 50 years in terms of papers rejected by *[Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_(journal) "Science (journal)")* or *Nature*."

Peter Mansfield of the [University of Nottingham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Nottingham "University of Nottingham") in the [United Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Kingdom "United Kingdom") took Lauterbur's initial work another step further, replacing the slow (and prone to artefacts) projection-reconstruction method used by Lautebur's original technique with a method that used frequency and phase encoding by spatial gradients of magnetic field. Owing to [Larmor precession](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Larmor_precession "Larmor precession"), a mathematical technique called a [Fourier transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourier_transformation "Fourier transformation") could then be used to recover the desired image, greatly speeding up the imaging process.

Lauterbur unsuccessfully attempted to file patents related to his work to commercialize the discovery. The [State University of New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_University_of_New_York "State University of New York") chose not to pursue patents, with the rationale that the expense would not pay off in the end. "The company that was in charge of such applications decided that it would not repay the expense of getting a patent. That turned out not to be a spectacularly good decision," Lauterbur said in 2003. He attempted to get the federal government to pay for an early prototype of the MRI machine for years in the 1970s, and the process took a decade. The University of Nottingham did file patents which later made Mansfield wealthy.

## Nobel Prize

Lauterbur was awarded the Nobel Prize along with Mansfield in the fall of 2003. Controversy occurred when [Raymond Damadian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raymond_Damadian "Raymond Damadian") took out full-page ads in *[The New York Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_York_Times "The New York Times")*, *[The Washington Post](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Washington_Post "The Washington Post")* and *[The Los Angeles Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Los_Angeles_Times "The Los Angeles Times")* headlined "The Shameful Wrong That Must Be Righted" saying that the Nobel committee had not included him as a Prize winner alongside Lauterbur and Mansfield for his early work on the MRI. Damadian said that he discovered MRI and the two Nobel-winning scientists refined his technology.

*The New York Times* published an editorial saying that while scientists credit Damadian for holding an early patent in MRI technology, Lauterbur and Mansfield expanded upon [Herman Carr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herman_Carr "Herman Carr")'s technique in order to produce first 2D and then 3D MR images. The editorial deems this to be worthy of a Nobel prize even though it states clearly in Alfred Nobel's will that prizes are not to be given out solely on the basis of improving an existing technology for commercial use. The newspaper then points out a few cases in which precursor discoveries had been awarded with a Nobel, along with a few deserving cases in which it had not, such as [Rosalind Franklin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosalind_Franklin "Rosalind Franklin"), [Oswald Avery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oswald_Avery "Oswald Avery"), [Robert Gabillard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Gabillard?action=edit&redlink=1 "Robert Gabillard").

## Death

Lauterbur died aged 77 in March 2007 of [kidney disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kidney_disease "Kidney disease") at his home in [Urbana, Illinois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urbana,_Illinois "Urbana, Illinois"). University of Illinois Chancellor [Richard Herman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Herman "Richard Herman") said, "Paul's influence is felt around the world every day, every time an MRI saves the life of a daughter or a son, a mother or a father."
