**Howard Robert Horvitz** [ForMemRS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ForMemRS "ForMemRS") (born May 8, 1947) is an American [biologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biologist "Biologist") whose research on the [nematode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nematode "Nematode") worm *[Caenorhabditis elegans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caenorhabditis_elegans "Caenorhabditis elegans")* was awarded the 2002 [Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physiology_or_Medicine "Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine"), together with [Sydney Brenner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sydney_Brenner "Sydney Brenner") and [John E. Sulston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_E._Sulston "John E. Sulston"), whose "seminal discoveries concerning the genetic regulation of organ development and programmed cell death" were "important for medical research and have shed new light on the pathogenesis of many diseases".

## Early life and education

Horvitz was born in Chicago, Illinois, to Jewish parents, the son of Mary R. (Savit), a school teacher, and Oscar Freedom Horvitz, a GAO accountant. He majored in mathematics at [Massachusetts Institute of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massachusetts_Institute_of_Technology "Massachusetts Institute of Technology"), where he joined [Alpha Epsilon Pi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpha_Epsilon_Pi "Alpha Epsilon Pi") and spent his summers working for IBM, at first wiring panels for accounting machines and then in his final summer helping to develop IBM's [Conversational Programming System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conversational_Programming_System "Conversational Programming System").

During his senior year, Horvitz took his first courses in biology and was encouraged by his professors to continue to study biology in graduate school, despite his limited coursework in the field. After he completed his undergraduate studies in 1968, he enrolled in graduate studies in biology at [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University "Harvard University"), where he studied [T4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enterobacteria_phage_T4 "Enterobacteria phage T4")-induced modifications of *E. coli* [RNA polymerase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RNA_polymerase "RNA polymerase") under the direction of [Walter Gilbert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Gilbert "Walter Gilbert") and [James Watson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Watson "James Watson"). He completed his PhD in 1974.

## Career

In 1974, Horvitz took a postdoctoral position at the [Laboratory of Molecular Biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laboratory_of_Molecular_Biology "Laboratory of Molecular Biology") (LMB) in Cambridge, England, where he worked with his future Nobel prize co-winners [Sydney Brenner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sydney_Brenner "Sydney Brenner") and [John Sulston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Sulston "John Sulston") on the genetics and cell lineage of *C. elegans*. In 1978, Horvitz was offered a faculty position at MIT, where he is currently Professor of Biology and a member of the [McGovern Institute for Brain Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McGovern_Institute_for_Brain_Research "McGovern Institute for Brain Research"). He is also an Investigator of the [Howard Hughes Medical Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Howard_Hughes_Medical_Institute "Howard Hughes Medical Institute").

Horvitz serves as the chair of the board of trustees for [Society for Science & the Public](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Society_for_Science_&_the_Public "Society for Science & the Public") and is a member of the [USA Science and Engineering Festival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USA_Science_and_Engineering_Festival "USA Science and Engineering Festival")'s advisory board.

## Research

At LMB, Horvitz worked with Sulston to track every non-gonadal cell division that occurred during larval development, and published a complete description of these lineages in 1977. Later, in cooperation with Sulston and Martin Chalfie, Horvitz began investigations first characterizing several cell lineage mutants and then seeking genes that controlled cell lineage or that controlled specific lineages. In 1981, they identified and characterized the gene *[lin-4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lin-4_microRNA_precursor "Lin-4 microRNA precursor")*, a "[heterochronic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heterochrony "Heterochrony")" mutant that changes the timeline of cell fates.

In his early work at MIT, Horvitz continued his work on cell lineage and cell fate, using *C. elegans* to investigate whether there was a genetic program controlling cell death, or [apoptosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apoptosis "Apoptosis"). In 1986, he identified the first "death genes", *ced-3* and *ced-4*. He showed that functional *ced-3* and *ced-4* genes were a prerequisite for cell death to be executed. He went on to show that another gene, *ced-9*, protects against cell death by interacting with *ced-4* and *ced-3*, as well as identifying a number of genes that direct how a dead cell is eliminated. Horvitz showed that the human genome contains a *ced-3*-like gene.

Horvitz's later research continued to use *C. elegans* to analyze the genetic control of animal development and behavior, as well as to link discoveries in the nematode to human diseases, particularly cancer and neurodegenerative diseases such as [amyotrophic lateral sclerosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amyotrophic_lateral_sclerosis "Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis") (ALS). He made further advancements in defining the molecular pathway of programmed cell death, and has identified several key components, including: EGL-1, a protein which activates apoptosis by inhibiting CED-9; transcription factors *ces-1* and *ces-2,* and *ced-8,* which controls the timing of cell death. He continued working on heterochronic mutants and other aspects of cell lineage, and established lines of research in signal transduction, morphogenesis, and neural development. Horvitz has collaborated with [Victor Ambros](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victor_Ambros "Victor Ambros") and [David Bartel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Bartel "David Bartel") on a project to characterize the complete set of the more than 100 [microRNAs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MicroRNA "MicroRNA") in the *C. elegans* genome.
