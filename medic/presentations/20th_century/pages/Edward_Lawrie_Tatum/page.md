**Edward Lawrie Tatum** (December 14, 1909 – November 5, 1975) was an American [geneticist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geneticist "Geneticist"). He shared half of the [Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physiology_or_Medicine "Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine") in 1958 with [George Beadle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Beadle "George Beadle") for showing that [genes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gene "Gene") control individual steps in [metabolism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metabolism "Metabolism"). The other half of that year's award went to [Joshua Lederberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joshua_Lederberg "Joshua Lederberg"). Tatum was an elected member of the United States [National Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Academy_of_Sciences "National Academy of Sciences"), the [American Philosophical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Philosophical_Society "American Philosophical Society"), and the [American Academy of Arts and Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Academy_of_Arts_and_Sciences "American Academy of Arts and Sciences").

## Education

Edward Lawrie Tatum was born on December 14, 1909, in [Boulder, Colorado](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boulder,_Colorado "Boulder, Colorado") to Arthur L. Tatum and Mabel Webb Tatum. Arthur L. Tatum was a chemistry professor, who by 1925 was a professor of pharmacology at the University of Wisconsin at Madison.

Edward Lawrie Tatum attended college at the [University of Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Chicago "University of Chicago") for two years, before transferring to the [University of Wisconsin–Madison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Wisconsin–Madison "University of Wisconsin–Madison"), where he received his BA in 1931 and PhD in 1934. His dissertation was *Studies in the biochemistry of microorganisms* (1934).

## Career

Starting in 1937, Tatum worked at [Stanford University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford_University "Stanford University"), where he began collaborating with George Beadle on their work on fruit flies (*Drosophila melanogaster*). He then moved to [Yale University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yale_University "Yale University") in 1945 where he mentored Lederberg. He returned to Stanford in 1948 and then joined the faculty of [Rockefeller Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rockefeller_University "Rockefeller University") in 1957. He remained there until his death on November 5, 1975, in [New York City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_City "New York City"). A heavy [cigarette](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cigarette "Cigarette") smoker, Tatum died of [heart failure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heart_failure "Heart failure") complicated by chronic [emphysema](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emphysema "Emphysema"). His last wife Elsie Bergland died in 1998.

## Research

Tatum and Beadle carried out pioneering studies of biochemical mutations in Neurospora, published in 1941. Their work provided a prototype of the investigation of gene action and a new and effective experimental methodology for the analysis of mutations in biochemical pathways.
Beadle and Tatum's key experiments involved exposing the bread mold *[Neurospora crassa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neurospora_crassa "Neurospora crassa")* to [x-rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-rays "X-rays"), causing [mutations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutation "Mutation"). In a series of experiments, they showed that these mutations caused changes in specific [enzymes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enzyme "Enzyme") involved in [metabolic pathways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metabolic_pathway "Metabolic pathway"). This led them to propose a direct link between [genes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gene "Gene") and enzymatic reactions, known as the ["one gene, one enzyme" hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/"one_gene,_one_enzyme"_hypothesis "\"one gene, one enzyme\" hypothesis").

Tatum spent his career studying biosynthetic pathways and the genetics of [bacteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bacterium "Bacterium"). An active area of research in his laboratory was to understand the basis of [Tryptophan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tryptophan "Tryptophan") [biosynthesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biosynthesis "Biosynthesis") in *[Escherichia coli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Escherichia_coli "Escherichia coli")*. Tatum and his student Joshua Lederberg showed that *E. coli* could share genetic information through [recombination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bacterial_conjugation "Bacterial conjugation").
