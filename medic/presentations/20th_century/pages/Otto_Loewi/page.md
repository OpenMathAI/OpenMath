**Otto Loewi** (German: [[ˈɔtoː ˈløːvi]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Standard_German "Help:IPA/Standard German") [ⓘ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:De-Otto_Loewi.ogg "File:De-Otto Loewi.ogg"); 3 June 1873 – 25 December 1961) was a German-born [pharmacologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pharmacology "Pharmacology") and psychobiologist who discovered the role of [acetylcholine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acetylcholine "Acetylcholine") as an endogenous [neurotransmitter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neurotransmitter "Neurotransmitter"). For this discovery, he was awarded the [Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physiology_or_Medicine "Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine") in 1936, which he shared with Sir [Henry Dale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Hallett_Dale "Henry Hallett Dale"), who was a lifelong friend that helped to inspire the neurotransmitter experiment. Loewi met Dale in 1902 when spending some months in [Ernest Starling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_Starling "Ernest Starling")'s laboratory at [University College, London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_College,_London "University College, London").

## Biography

Loewi was born in [Frankfurt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frankfurt "Frankfurt"), [Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germany "Germany") on June 3, 1873, in a [Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jews "Jews") family. He went to study medicine at the [University of Strasbourg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Strasbourg "University of Strasbourg"), Germany (now part of France), in 1891, where he attended courses by famous professors [Gustav Schwalbe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gustav_Albert_Schwalbe "Gustav Albert Schwalbe"), [Oswald Schmiedeberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oswald_Schmiedeberg "Oswald Schmiedeberg"), and [Bernhard Naunyn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernhard_Naunyn "Bernhard Naunyn") among others. He received his medical doctoral degree in 1896. He also was a member of the fraternity Burschenschaft Germania Strassburg.

Subsequently, he worked with [Martin Freund](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Freund "Martin Freund") at [Goethe University of Frankfurt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goethe_University_of_Frankfurt "Goethe University of Frankfurt") and with [Franz Hofmeister](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_Hofmeister "Franz Hofmeister") in [Strasbourg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strasbourg "Strasbourg"). From 1897 to 1898, he served as an assistant to Carl von Noorden, clinician at the City Hospital in Frankfurt. Soon, however, after seeing the high mortality in countless cases of far-advanced [tuberculosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuberculosis "Tuberculosis") and [pneumonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pneumonia "Pneumonia"), left without any treatment because of lack of therapy, he decided to drop his intention to become a clinician and instead to carry out research in basic medical science, in particular pharmacology. In 1898, he became an assistant of Professor [Hans Horst Meyer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Horst_Meyer "Hans Horst Meyer"), the renowned pharmacologist at the [University of Marburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Marburg "University of Marburg"). During his first years in [Marburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marburg "Marburg"), Loewi's studies were in the field of [metabolism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metabolism "Metabolism"). As a result of his work on the action of phlorhizin, a glucoside provoking glycosuria, and another one on nuclein metabolism in man, he was appointed «Privatdozent» ([lecturer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lecturer "Lecturer")) in 1900. Two years later he published his paper «Über Eiweisssynthese im Tierkörper» (on protein synthesis in the animal body), proving that animals are able to rebuild their proteins from their degradation products, the amino acids – an essential discovery with regard to nutrition.

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/52/Sir_Henry_Hallett_Dale_and_Otto_Loewi._Photograph._Wellcome_V0026258.jpg/250px-Sir_Henry_Hallett_Dale_and_Otto_Loewi._Photograph._Wellcome_V0026258.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Sir_Henry_Hallett_Dale_and_Otto_Loewi._Photograph._Wellcome_V0026258.jpg)

Sir Henry Dale and Otto Loewi

In 1902, Loewi was a guest researcher in Ernest Starling's laboratory in London, where he met his lifelong friend Henry Dale.

In 1903, he accepted an appointment at the [University of Graz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Graz "University of Graz") in [Austria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austria "Austria"), where he would remain until being forced out of the country in 1938. In 1905, Loewi became Associate Professor at Meyer's laboratory and received Austrian citizenship. In 1909 he was appointed to the Chair of Pharmacology in Graz. He had also been a professor at the [University of Vienna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Vienna "University of Vienna").

He married Guida Goldschmiedt (1889–1958) in 1908. They had three sons and a daughter. He was the last Jew hired by the University between 1903 and the end of the war.

In 1921, Loewi investigated how vital organs respond to [chemical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical "Chemical") and [electrical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical "Electrical") stimulation. He also established their relative dependence on [epinephrine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epinephrine "Epinephrine") for proper function. Consequently, he learnt how [nerve impulses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nerve_impulse "Nerve impulse") are transmitted by chemical messengers. The first chemical [neurotransmitter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neurotransmitter "Neurotransmitter") that he identified was acetylcholine.

After being arrested, along with two of his sons, on the night of the [German invasion of Austria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anschluss "Anschluss"), March 11, 1938, Loewi was released after three months on condition that he "voluntarily" [relinquish all his possessions, including his research, to the Nazis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racial_policy_of_Nazi_Germany "Racial policy of Nazi Germany"). He arrived to Britain in September 1938 and shortly afterwards he was offered a visiting professorship at the [Université libre de Bruxelles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Université_libre_de_Bruxelles "Université libre de Bruxelles") via the [Francqui Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francqui_Foundation "Francqui Foundation").

After teaching one semester in the first half of 1939 and going on vacation in England Loewi did not return to Brussels in September due to the outbreak of [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II"). Loewi worked at the [Nuffield Institute for Medical Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuffield_Institute_for_Medical_Research?action=edit&redlink=1 "Nuffield Institute for Medical Research") affiliated with Oxford before accepting an offer of a tenured research professor position at the [New York University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_University "New York University") [College of Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_University_Grossman_School_of_Medicine "New York University Grossman School of Medicine"). He arrived to the US in June 1940 and was joined by his wife only in early 1941 (she was not allowed to leave earlier).

In 1946, Loewi became a [naturalized citizen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naturalized_citizen "Naturalized citizen") of the [United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States "United States"). In 1954, he became a Foreign Member of the [Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society "Royal Society"). He died in [New York City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_City "New York City") on December 25, 1961.

Shortly after Loewi's death in late 1961, his youngest son bestowed the gold Nobel medal on the [Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society "Royal Society") in London. He gave the Nobel diploma to the [University of Graz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Graz "University of Graz") in [Austria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austria "Austria") in 1983, where it currently resides, along with a bronze copy of a bust of Loewi. The original of the bust is at the [Marine Biological Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marine_Biological_Laboratory "Marine Biological Laboratory") in [Woods Hole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woods_Hole "Woods Hole"), Massachusetts, Loewi's summer home from his arrival in the US until his death.

## Research

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c6/Vagusstoff2.png/330px-Vagusstoff2.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Vagusstoff2.png)

In his most famous experiment, Loewi took fluid from one frog heart and applied it to another, slowing the second heart and showing that synaptic signaling used chemical messengers.

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/bb/Loewi_Nobel_20040420.jpg/250px-Loewi_Nobel_20040420.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Loewi_Nobel_20040420.jpg)

The Nobel Prize diploma of Otto Loewi, housed at the [University of Graz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Graz "University of Graz")

Before Loewi's experiments, it was unclear whether signaling across the [synapse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synapse "Synapse") was [bioelectrical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bioelectricity "Bioelectricity") or [chemical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical "Chemical"). While pharmacology experiments had established that physiological responses such as muscle contraction could be induced by chemical application, there was no evidence that cells released chemical substances to cause these responses. On the contrary, researchers had shown that physiological responses could be caused by applying an electrical impulse, which suggested that electrical transmission may be the only mode of endogenous signaling. In the early 20th century the controversy of whether cells used chemical or electrical transmission divided even the most prominent scientists.

Loewi's famous experiment, published in 1921, largely answered this question. He dissected out of frogs two beating [hearts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heart "Heart"): one with the [vagus nerve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vagus_nerve "Vagus nerve") which controls heart rate attached, the other heart on its own. Both hearts were bathed in a saline solution (i.e. Ringer's solution). By electrically stimulating the vagus nerve, Loewi made the first heart beat slower. Then, Loewi took some of the liquid bathing the first heart and applied it to the second heart. The application of the liquid made the second heart also beat slower, proving that some soluble chemical released by the vagus nerve was controlling the heart rate. He called the unknown chemical [Vagusstoff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vagusstoff "Vagusstoff"), naming it after the nerve and the German word for substance. It was later found that this chemical corresponded to [acetylcholine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acetylcholine "Acetylcholine"). His experiment was iconic because it was the first to demonstrate the endogenous release of a chemical substance that could cause a response in the absence of electrical stimulation. It paved the way for the understanding that the electrical signaling event (action potential) causes a chemical event (release of neurotransmitter from synapses) that is ultimately the effector on the tissue.

Loewi's investigations "On an augmentation of adrenaline release by cocaine" and "On the connection between digitalis and the action of calcium" stimulated a considerable body of research in the years following their publication.

He also clarified two mechanisms of therapeutic importance: the blockade and the augmentation of nerve action by certain drugs.

Loewi is also known for the means by which the idea for his experiment came to him. On Easter Saturday 1921, he dreamed of an experiment that would prove once and for all that transmission of nerve impulses was chemical, not electrical. He woke up, scribbled the experiment onto a scrap of paper on his night-stand, and went back to sleep.

The next morning, he found, to his horror, that he couldn't read his midnight scribbles. That day, he said, was the longest day of his life, as he could not remember his dream. That night, however, he had the same dream. This time, he immediately went to his lab to perform the experiment. From that point on, the consensus was that the Nobel was not a matter of "if" but of "when."

Thirteen years later, Loewi was awarded the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, which he shared with Sir [Henry Hallett Dale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Hallett_Dale "Henry Hallett Dale").

## Loewi's mydriatic test

Loewi observed that by removing the pancreas from dogs, it gave them an experimental form of [diabetes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diabetes_mellitus "Diabetes mellitus"), which led to a change of the response of the eye to adrenaline. This compound in normal dogs has no effect, but in the dogs without a pancreas the pupil dilated.
This test involves instilling repeated doses of 1:1000 adrenaline solution into the eye and looking for [pupillary dilation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mydriasis "Mydriasis"). Surgeons used this as a diagnostic test for [acute pancreatitis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pancreatitis "Pancreatitis"), which was based on Loewi's observation of such a phenomenon in dogs that had had their pancreas removed. The usefulness of this test was reported in a case series of two patients; it was, as expected, negative in a case involving carcinoma of the bile duct, but positive in a case of pancreatitis. The effectiveness of this test was subsequently investigated. The mechanism of action of this phenomenon is unclear, but has been attributed to "a functional toxic disturbance of the sympathetic post-ganglionic neuron innervating the iris".
