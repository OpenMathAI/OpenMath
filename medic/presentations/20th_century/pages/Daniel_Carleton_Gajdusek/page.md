**Daniel Carleton Gajdusek** ([/ˈɡaɪdəʃɛk/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English") [*GHY-də-shek*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:Pronunciation_respelling_key "Help:Pronunciation respelling key"); September 9, 1923 – December 12, 2008) was an American [physician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physician "Physician"), medical researcher, and convicted child [sex offender](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sex_offender "Sex offender"), who was the co-recipient (with [Baruch S. Blumberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baruch_S._Blumberg "Baruch S. Blumberg")) of the [Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physiology_or_Medicine "Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine") in 1976 for work on the transmissibility of [kuru](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuru_(disease) "Kuru (disease)"), implying the existence of an infectious agent, which he named an 'unconventional virus'.

In 1996, Gajdusek was charged with [child molestation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Child_molestation "Child molestation"), and spent 12 months in prison after being convicted. He subsequently entered a self-imposed exile in Europe, where he died a decade later. Despite openly admitting to molesting boys and his approval of [incest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incest "Incest"), Gajdusek still received support from many peers who advocated clemency and felt his crimes were lessened by his scientific contributions.

His papers are held at the [National Library of Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Library_of_Medicine "National Library of Medicine") in [Bethesda, Maryland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bethesda,_Maryland "Bethesda, Maryland"), and at the [American Philosophical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Philosophical_Society "American Philosophical Society") in [Philadelphia, Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philadelphia,_Pennsylvania "Philadelphia, Pennsylvania").

## Early life and education

Gajdusek's father, Karol Gajdusek, was a Slovak from [Smrdáky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smrdáky "Smrdáky"), [Slovakia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slovakia "Slovakia"). His mother Ottilia Dobróczki emigrated from [Debrecen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Debrecen "Debrecen"), today Hungary. Gajdusek was born in [Yonkers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yonkers "Yonkers"), New York, and graduated in 1943 from the [University of Rochester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Rochester "University of Rochester"), where he studied physics, biology, chemistry, and mathematics.

He obtained an [M.D.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Medicine "Doctor of Medicine") from [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University "Harvard University") in 1946 and performed postdoctoral research at [Columbia University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia_University "Columbia University"), the [California Institute of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California_Institute_of_Technology "California Institute of Technology"), and [Harvard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University "Harvard University"). In 1951, Gajdusek was drafted into the U.S. Army and assigned as a research [virologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virology "Virology") at the Walter Reed [Army Medical Service Graduate School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Army_Medical_Service_Graduate_School "Army Medical Service Graduate School"). He was discharged from the military in 1954.

## Career

In 1954, he worked as a visiting investigator at the [Walter and Eliza Hall Institute of Medical Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_and_Eliza_Hall_Institute_of_Medical_Research "Walter and Eliza Hall Institute of Medical Research") in [Melbourne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melbourne "Melbourne"), [Australia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australia "Australia"). There, he began the work that culminated in the [Nobel Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize "Nobel Prize"). From 1970 to 1996, Gajdusek was the chief of the Laboratory of Central Nervous System Studies at [NINDS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NINDS "NINDS") at the National Institutes of Health (NIH). He was elected as a member of the [American Philosophical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Philosophical_Society "American Philosophical Society") in 1978.

### Kuru research

Gajdusek's best-known work focused on [kuru](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuru_(disease) "Kuru (disease)"). This disease was rampant among the [South Fore people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fore_tribe "Fore tribe") of [New Guinea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Guinea "New Guinea") in the 1950s and 1960s. He received the 1976 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for demonstrating, alongside [Michael Alpers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Alpers "Michael Alpers") and Clarence Gibbs Jr, that kuru was a transmittable disease.

Gajdusek was introduced to the problem of kuru by [Vincent Zigas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vincent_Zigas "Vincent Zigas"), a district medical officer in the Fore Tribe region of New Guinea. Gajdusek provided the first medical description of this unique [neurological disorder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neurological_disorder "Neurological disorder"), which was miscast in the popular press as the "laughing sickness" because some patients displayed [risus sardonicus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Risus_sardonicus "Risus sardonicus") as a symptom. Gajdusek began investigating the disease in 1957 but could find no likely cause for the epidemic; no conventional infectious agent, no nutritional deficiency, no toxin, as both men wrote in their 1977 paper.

He is often incorrectly credited for establishing the direct link between cannibalism and kuru disease. In reality, the first investigators to propose the hypothesis were married couple [Shirley Lindenbaum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shirley_Lindenbaum "Shirley Lindenbaum") and Robert Glasse. In 1968, Shirley Lindenbaum and Robert Glasse, together with John D. Mathews, an epidemiologist based at Okapa, published an article in the *Lancet* that consolidated all the anthropological and epidemiological evidence, demonstrating definitively that cannibalism was responsible for the spread of kuru among the Fore.

Gajdusek was initially dismissive of anthropological evidence gathered by Shirley Lindenbaum and Robert Glasse in 1963 that suggested not only that kuru was transmissible, but that cannibalism was the likely mode of transmission. Lindenbaum and Glasse felt further evidence was required to support this theory and discussed it with Alpers who relayed the theory to Gajdusek. It was with Alpers' encouragement that the experiment involving the inoculation of affected brain tissue into chimpanzees was conducted and then established in 1965 the transmissibility of the disease. This was done by drilling holes into chimps' heads and placing pureed brain matter into the cerebellum. These animals then developed symptoms of kuru.

In the 2000s, Gajdusek was asked when the hypothesis of cannibalism as a vehicle to spread kuru was first envisaged. His response, "even completely drunk would come to the conclusion that a disease endemic among cannibals must be spread through eating corpses". Contradictingly, during his Nobel Prize lecture he stated that kuru was a "slow virus" spread by "conjunctival, nasal, and skin contamination with highly infectious brain tissue"

Kuru was shown to have remarkable similarity to [scrapie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scrapie "Scrapie"), a disease of sheep and goats caused by an unconventional infectious agent. Subsequently, additional human agents belonging to the same group were discovered. They include sporadic, familial, and variant [Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creutzfeldt–Jakob_disease "Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease"). Gajdusek recognized that diseases like kuru and Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease were caused by a new infectious agent that had not yet been identified. Further research on the scrapie agent by [Stanley Prusiner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanley_Prusiner "Stanley Prusiner") and others led to the identification of endogenous proteins called [prions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prion "Prion") as the cause of these diseases.

### "Unconventional viruses"

In his 1977 paper "Unconventional Viruses and the Origin and Disappearance of Kuru", Gajdusek postulated that the cause of kuru, scrapie and Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease were caused by what he termed an "unconventional virus". In comparison to normal viruses, unconventional viruses had a long [incubation period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incubation_period "Incubation period") and did not cause an [immune response](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immune_response "Immune response") in the host. Although Gajdusek said there were no demonstrable [nucleic acids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nucleic_acid "Nucleic acid") in unconventional viruses, he did not rule out the possibility that unconventional viruses contained [RNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RNA "RNA") at a low level, despite their [radiation resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiation_resistance "Radiation resistance").

These infectious agents were later discovered to be misfolded proteins, or [prions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prions "Prions").

## Child molestation and convictions

In the course of his research trips in the South Pacific, Gajdusek had brought 56 mostly male children back to live with him in the United States and provided them with the opportunity to receive high school and college education. One of these boys, now a grown man, later accused Gajdusek of [molesting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Child_molestation "Child molestation") him as a child.

Gajdusek was charged with child molestation in April 1996, based on incriminating entries in published journals, his personal diary, and statements from a victim. In journals published and distributed by the [NIH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Institutes_of_Health "National Institutes of Health"), Gajdusek wrote about men's sexual assault of boys in New Guinea, Micronesia, and other Polynesian islands, and about sexually assaulting boys during his research trips.

Gajdusek pleaded guilty in 1997 and, under a [plea bargain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plea_bargain "Plea bargain"), was sentenced to 12 months in prison. After his release in 1998, he was permitted to serve his five-year unsupervised [probation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probation "Probation") in Europe. He never returned to the United States and lived in [Amsterdam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amsterdam "Amsterdam"), spending winters in [Tromsø](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tromsø "Tromsø"), [Norway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norway "Norway"), where the [polar night](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_night "Polar night") around the [winter solstice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winter_solstice "Winter solstice") helped him to do more work.

Gajdusek's treatment was denounced in October 1996 as [anti-elitist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-elitism "Anti-elitism") and unduly harsh by controversial former [Edinburgh University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edinburgh_University "Edinburgh University") psychologist [Chris Brand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chris_Brand "Chris Brand").

The documentary *The Genius and the Boys* by [Bosse Lindquist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bosse_Lindquist "Bosse Lindquist"), first shown on [BBC Four](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Four "BBC Four") on June 1, 2009, notes that "seven men testified in confidentiality about Gajdusek [raping] them when they were boys", that four said "the sex was untroubling", while for three of them "the sex was a shaming, abusive, and a violation". One of these boys, the son of a friend and now an adult, appears in the film. Furthermore, Gajdusek openly admits to molesting boys and his approval of [incest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incest "Incest"). The film tries to analyse Gajdusek's sexual behaviour, and also to understand his motivations for science, exploration, and life.

## Death and legacy

Gajdusek died December 12, 2008, in [Tromsø](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tromsø "Tromsø"), [Norway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norway "Norway"), at the age of 85. He was working and visiting colleagues in Tromsø at the time of his death.

[Hanya Yanagihara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanya_Yanagihara "Hanya Yanagihara")'s 2013 novel, *[The People in the Trees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_People_in_the_Trees "The People in the Trees")*, is based on Gajdusek's life, research, and child molestation conviction. The novel centers on a character named A. Norton Perina, inspired by Gajdusek, who researches the life-extending properties of sacred turtle meat in Micronesia.

In 2026 journalist [Rachel Aviv](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rachel_Aviv "Rachel Aviv") wrote an article for [The New Yorker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_Yorker "The New Yorker") on Gajdusek's life and crimes, including an interview with one of the boys he worked with, Luwi Ikabala.
