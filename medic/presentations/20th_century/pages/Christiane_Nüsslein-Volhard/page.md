**Christiane (Janni) Nüsslein-Volhard** (German pronunciation: [[kʁɪsˈti̯anə ˈnʏslaɪ̯n ˈfɔlˌhaʁt]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Standard_German "Help:IPA/Standard German") [ⓘ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:De-Christiane_N%C3%BCsslein-Volhard.ogg "File:De-Christiane Nüsslein-Volhard.ogg"); born 20 October 1942) is a German [developmental biologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Developmental_biologist "Developmental biologist") and a 1995 [Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physiology_or_Medicine "Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine") laureate. She is the only woman from Germany to have received a [Nobel Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize "Nobel Prize") in the sciences.

Nüsslein-Volhard earned her PhD in 1974 from the [University of Tübingen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Tübingen "University of Tübingen"), where she studied [protein-DNA interaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protein-DNA_interaction "Protein-DNA interaction"). She won the [Albert Lasker Award for Basic Medical Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Lasker_Award_for_Basic_Medical_Research "Albert Lasker Award for Basic Medical Research") in 1991 and the [Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physiology_or_Medicine "Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine") in 1995, together with [Eric Wieschaus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eric_F._Wieschaus "Eric F. Wieschaus") and [Edward B. Lewis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_B._Lewis "Edward B. Lewis"), for their research on the [genetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetics "Genetics") control of [embryonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embryo "Embryo") [development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Developmental_biology "Developmental biology").

## Early life and education

Nüsslein-Volhard was born in [Magdeburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magdeburg "Magdeburg") on 20 October 1942, the second of five children to Rolf Volhard, an architect, and Brigitte Haas Volhard, a nursery school teacher. She has four siblings: three sisters and one brother. She grew up and went to school in south [Frankfurt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frankfurt "Frankfurt"), where she was exposed to art and music and thus was "trained in looking at things and recognizing things". Her great-grandfather was the chemist [Jacob Volhard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacob_Volhard "Jacob Volhard"), and her grandfather was the known [internist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internist "Internist") [Franz Volhard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_Volhard "Franz Volhard"). She is also the aunt of the Nobel laureate in chemistry [Benjamin List](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benjamin_List "Benjamin List").

After the [Abitur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abitur "Abitur") in 1962, she briefly considered pursuing medicine, but dropped the idea after doing a month-long nursing course in a hospital. Instead, she opted to study biology at [Goethe University Frankfurt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goethe_University_Frankfurt "Goethe University Frankfurt"). In 1964 Nüsslein-Volhard left Frankfurt for the [University of Tübingen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Tübingen "University of Tübingen"), to start a new course in biochemistry. She originally wanted to do [behavioral biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Behavioral_biology "Behavioral biology"), "but then somehow I ended up in biochemistry (...) and molecular genetics because at the time this was the most modern aspect, and I was ambitious — I wanted to go where the leaders were. The old-fashioned botanists and zoologists were such dull people— there was nothing interesting there."

She received a diploma in biochemistry in 1969 and earned a [PhD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PhD "PhD") in 1974 for research into [protein–DNA interactions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protein–DNA_interaction "Protein–DNA interaction") and the binding of [RNA polymerase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RNA_polymerase "RNA polymerase") in *[Escherichia coli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Escherichia_coli "Escherichia coli")*.

## Career

In 1975, Nüsslein-Volhard became a postdoctoral researcher in [Walter Gehring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Gehring "Walter Gehring")´s laboratory at the [Biozentrum, University of Basel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biozentrum_University_of_Basel "Biozentrum University of Basel"). She was a specialist in the developmental biology of *[Drosophila melanogaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drosophila_melanogaster "Drosophila melanogaster")* (fruit fly) supported by a long-term fellowship from the [European Molecular Biology Organization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Molecular_Biology_Organization "European Molecular Biology Organization") (EMBO).
In 1977, she continued in the laboratory of [Klaus Sander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klaus_Sander?action=edit&redlink=1 "Klaus Sander") at [University of Freiburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Freiburg "University of Freiburg"), who was an expert in embryonic patterning.
In 1978, she set up her own lab in the newly founded [European Molecular Biology Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Molecular_Biology_Laboratory "European Molecular Biology Laboratory") in Heidelberg with [Eric Wieschaus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eric_Wieschaus "Eric Wieschaus"), whom she had met in Basel. Over the next three years they examined about 20,000 mutated fly families, collected about 600 mutants with an altered body pattern and found that out of the approximately 5,000 essential genes only 120 were essential for early development. In October 1980, they published the mere 15 genes controlling the segmented pattern of the Drosophila larva.

In 1981, Nüsslein-Volhard moved to the [Friedrich Miescher Laboratory of the Max Planck Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Miescher_Laboratory_of_the_Max_Planck_Society "Friedrich Miescher Laboratory of the Max Planck Society") in Tübingen.
From 1984 until her retirement in 2014, she was the director of the [Max Planck Institute for Developmental Biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Planck_Institute_for_Developmental_Biology "Max Planck Institute for Developmental Biology") in Tübingen and also led its genetics department. After 1984, she launched work on the [developmental biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Developmental_biology "Developmental biology") of vertebrates, using the zebrafish ([Danio rerio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danio_rerio "Danio rerio")) as her research model.

In 2001, she became a member of the *Nationaler Ethikrat* (National Ethics Council of Germany) for the [ethical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethics "Ethics") assessment of new developments in the [life sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life_sciences "Life sciences") and their influence on the individual and society. Her primer for the lay-reader, *Coming to Life: How Genes Drive Development*, was published in April 2006.

In 2004, she started the Christiane Nüsslein-Volhard Foundation (*Christiane Nüsslein-Volhard Stiftung*) which aids promising young female German scientists with children. The foundation's main focus is to facilitate childcare as a supplement to existing stipends and day care.

### Research

During the late 1970's and early 1980's, little was known about the genetic and molecular mechanisms by which multicellular organisms [develop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Developmental_biology "Developmental biology") from single cells to morphologically complex forms during [embryogenesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embryogenesis "Embryogenesis"). Nüsslein-Volhard and Wieschaus identified genes involved in embryonic development by a series of  [genetic screens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_screen "Genetic screen"), generating random [mutations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutations "Mutations") in fruit flies using [ethyl methanesulfonate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethyl_methanesulfonate "Ethyl methanesulfonate"). Some of these mutations affected genes involved in the development of the embryo. They took advantage of the [segmented](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Segmentation_(biology) "Segmentation (biology)") form of *Drosophila* larvae to address the logic of the genes controlling development. They looked at the pattern of segments and denticles in each mutant under the microscope, and were therefore able to work out that particular genes were involved in different processes during development based on their differing mutant phenotypes (such as fewer segments, gaps in the normal segment pattern, and alterations in the patterns of denticles on the segments). Many of these genes were given descriptive names based on the appearance of the mutant larvae, such as [*hedgehog*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hedgehog_signaling_pathway "Hedgehog signaling pathway"), *[gurken](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gurken "Gurken")* (German: "cucumbers"), and *[Krüppel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krüppel "Krüppel")* ("cripple"). Later, researchers Pavel Tomancak, Amy Beaton, et al., identified exactly which gene had been affected by each mutation, thereby identifying a set of genes crucial for [Drosophila embryogenesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drosophila_embryogenesis "Drosophila embryogenesis").

The subsequent study of these mutants and their interactions led to important new insights into early *Drosophila* development, especially the mechanisms that underlie the step-wise development of body segments. These experiments are not only distinguished by their sheer scale (with the methods available at the time, they involved an enormous workload), but more importantly by their significance for organisms other than fruit flies.

Her findings led to important realizations about evolution – for example, that [protostomes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protostomes "Protostomes") and [deuterostomes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deuterostomes "Deuterostomes") are likely to have had a relatively well-developed [common ancestor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_ancestor "Common ancestor") with a much more complex body plan than had been conventionally thought.

Additionally, they greatly increased our understanding of the regulation of [transcription](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transcription_(genetics) "Transcription (genetics)"), as well as cell fate during development.

Nüsslein-Volhard is associated with the discovery of *[Toll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toll_(gene) "Toll (gene)")*, which led to the identification of [toll-like receptors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toll-like_receptor "Toll-like receptor").

As of 2023, Nüsslein-Volhard has an [h-index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H-index "H-index") of 104 according to [Scopus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scopus "Scopus").

## Personal life

Nüsslein-Volhard married in the mid-1960s while studying at the [Goethe University Frankfurt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goethe_University_Frankfurt "Goethe University Frankfurt"), but divorced soon afterward and did not have any children. She lives in [Bebenhausen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bebenhausen "Bebenhausen"), Germany. She has said that she loves to sing, play the flute and do chamber music. She published a cookbook in 2006.
