**François Jacob** (French: [[ʒakɔb]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/French "Help:IPA/French"); 17 June 1920 – 19 April 2013) was a French [biologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biologist "Biologist"). He shared the 1965 [Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physiology_or_Medicine "Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine") with [Jacques Monod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques_Monod "Jacques Monod") and [André Lwoff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/André_Lwoff "André Lwoff") "for their discoveries concerning [genetic control of enzyme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transcription_(genetics) "Transcription (genetics)") and [virus synthesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Provirus "Provirus")". He and Monod originated the idea that control of [enzyme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enzyme "Enzyme") levels in all [cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_(biology) "Cell (biology)") occurs through regulation of [transcription](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transcription_(genetics) "Transcription (genetics)"). For his work in the [French Resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Resistance "French Resistance"), he received the [Cross of Liberation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordre_de_la_Libération "Ordre de la Libération"), the [Légion d'honneur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Légion_d'honneur "Légion d'honneur") and [Croix de guerre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Croix_de_guerre_1939–1945 "Croix de guerre 1939–1945").

He wrote popular science, and in 1994 received the inaugural [Lewis Thomas Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lewis_Thomas_Prize "Lewis Thomas Prize").

## Early years

Jacob was born the only child of Simon, a merchant, and Thérèse (Franck) Jacob, in [Nancy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nancy,_Meurthe-et-Moselle "Nancy, Meurthe-et-Moselle"), France. An inquisitive child, he learned to read at a young age. Albert Franck, Jacob's maternal grandfather, a four-star general, was Jacob's childhood role model. At seven he entered the [Lycée Carnot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lycée_Carnot "Lycée Carnot"), where he was schooled for the next ten years; in his autobiography, he describes his impression of it: "a cage". He was antagonized by rightist youth at the Lycée Carnot around 1934. He describes his father as a "conformist in religion", while his mother and other family members important in his childhood were [secular Jews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_culture "Jewish culture"); shortly after his [bar mitzvah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bar_mitzvah "Bar mitzvah"), he became an [atheist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atheist "Atheist").

Though interested (and talented) in [physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics "Physics") and [mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics "Mathematics"), Jacob was horrified at the prospect of spending two additional years in "an even more draconian regime" to prepare for higher study at the [Polytechnique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/École_Polytechnique "École Polytechnique"). Instead, after observing a surgical operation that cemented his "slight interest" in medicine, he entered medical school.

During the German occupation of France—and on the heels of his mother's death—Jacob left France for Great Britain to join the war effort. Jacob, who had only completed his second year of medical studies, joined the medical company of the [French 2nd Armored Division](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2nd_Armored_Division_(France) "2nd Armored Division (France)") in 1940. He was injured in a German air attack in 1944 and returned to now-[liberated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberation_of_France "Liberation of France") Paris on 1 August 1944. For his wartime service, he was awarded France's WWII highest decoration for valor, the [Cross of Liberation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordre_de_la_Libération "Ordre de la Libération"), as well as [Légion d'honneur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Légion_d'honneur "Légion d'honneur") and [Croix de guerre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Croix_de_guerre_1939–1945 "Croix de guerre 1939–1945").

After his recovery, Jacob returned to medical school and began researching [tyrothricin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tyrothricin "Tyrothricin") and learning the methods of [bacteriology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bacteriology "Bacteriology") in the process. He completed a thesis he described as "replicating American work" on the effectiveness of the antibiotic against local infections, and became a medical doctor in 1947. Though attracted to research as a career, he was discouraged by his own perceived ignorance after attending a microbiology congress that summer. Instead, he took a position at the Cabanel Center, where he had done his thesis research; his new work entailed the manufacture of an antibiotic, [tyrothricin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tyrothricin "Tyrothricin"). Later, the center was contracted to convert gunpowder factories for penicillin production (though this proved impossible).

Also in this period, he met and began courting his future wife, the concert pianist, Lise Bloch. They had four children together. Lise died in 1983 and Jacob married Geneviève Barrier in1999.

## Research

In 1958, Monod was watching a film with his wife and told her "I think I’ve just thought up something important." In 1961 Jacob and Monod explored the idea that the control of [enzyme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enzyme "Enzyme") [expression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gene_expression "Gene expression") levels in cells is a result of regulation of [transcription](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transcription_(genetics) "Transcription (genetics)") of [DNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA "DNA") sequences. Their experiments and ideas gave impetus to the emerging field of molecular [developmental biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Developmental_biology "Developmental biology"), and of [transcriptional regulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transcriptional_regulation "Transcriptional regulation") in particular.

For many years it had been known that bacterial and other cells could respond to external conditions by regulating levels of their key [metabolic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metabolic "Metabolic") [enzymes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enzyme "Enzyme"), and/or the activity of these [enzymes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enzyme "Enzyme"). For instance, if a bacterium finds itself in a [broth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broth "Broth") containing [lactose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lactose "Lactose"), rather than the simpler sugar [glucose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glucose "Glucose"), it must adapt itself to the need to 1) import lactose, 2) cleave lactose to its constituents glucose and [galactose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galactose "Galactose"), and 3) convert the galactose to glucose. It was known that cells ramp up their production of the enzymes that do these steps when exposed to lactose, rather than wastefully producing these enzymes all the time. Studies of enzyme activity control were progressing through theories of the (allosteric) action of small [molecules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecule "Molecule") on the enzyme molecule itself (switching it on or off), but the method of controlling the enzyme production was not well understood at the time.

With the earlier determination of the structure and central importance of [DNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA "DNA"), it became clear that all proteins were being produced in some way from its genetic code, and that this step might form a key control point. Jacob and Monod made key experimental and theoretical discoveries that demonstrated that in the case of the lactose system outlined above (in the bacterium *[E. coli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Escherichia_coli "Escherichia coli")*), there are specific proteins that are devoted to repressing the transcription of the DNA to its product ([RNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RNA "RNA"), which in turn is [decoded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Code "Code") into [protein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protein "Protein")).

This repressor (the [lac repressor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lac_repressor "Lac repressor")) is made in all cells, binding directly to DNA at the [genes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gene "Gene") it controls, and physically preventing the transcription apparatus from gaining access to the DNA. In the presence of lactose, some of the lactose is converted to allolactose, which binds to the repressor making it no longer able to bind to DNA, and the transcriptional repression is lifted. In this way, a robust [feedback loop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feedback_loop "Feedback loop") is constructed that allows the set of lactose-digesting protein products to be made only when they are needed.

Jacob and Monod extended this repressor model to all genes in all [organisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organism "Organism") in their initial exuberance. The regulation of gene activity has developed into a very large sub-discipline of [molecular biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_biology "Molecular biology"), and in truth exhibits enormous variety in mechanism and many levels of complexity. Current researchers find regulatory events at every conceivable level of the processes that express genetic information. In the relatively simple [genome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genome "Genome") of baker's yeast, (*[Saccharomyces cerevisiae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saccharomyces_cerevisiae "Saccharomyces cerevisiae")*), 405 of its 6,419 protein-encoding genes are directly involved in transcriptional control, compared to 1,938 that are enzymes.
