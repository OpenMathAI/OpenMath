**Niels Kaj Jerne**, [FRS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow_of_the_Royal_Society "Fellow of the Royal Society") (23 December 1911 – 7 October 1994) was a Danish [immunologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immunologist "Immunologist"). He shared the [Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physiology_or_Medicine "Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine") in 1984 with [Georges J. F. Köhler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georges_J._F._Köhler "Georges J. F. Köhler") and [César Milstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/César_Milstein "César Milstein") "for theories concerning the specificity in development and control of the immune system and the discovery of the principle for production of [monoclonal antibodies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monoclonal_antibodies "Monoclonal antibodies")".

Jerne is known for three significant ideas. Firstly, instead of the body producing antibodies in response to an antigen, Jerne postulated that the immune system already has the specific antibodies it needs to fight antigens. Secondly, it was known that the immune system learns to be tolerant to the individual's own self. Jerne postulated that this learning takes place in the thymus. Thirdly, it was known that T cells and B cells communicate with each other.

Jerne's network theory proposed that the active sites of antibodies are attracted to both specific antigens ([idiotypes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idiotype "Idiotype")) and to other antibodies that bind to the same site. The antibodies are in balance, until an antigen disturbs the balance, stimulating an immune reaction.

## Early years and education

His ancestors had lived on the small Danish island of [Fanø](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fanø "Fanø") for centuries, but in 1910 his parents moved to London, where Jerne was born in 1911.

During the [First World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_World_War "First World War"), his parents moved to the [Netherlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Netherlands "Netherlands") and Jerne spent his youth in [Rotterdam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotterdam "Rotterdam"). After studying physics for two years at the [Leiden University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leiden_University "Leiden University"), Jerne moved to Copenhagen and changed his studies to the field of medicine. He graduated from the [University of Copenhagen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Copenhagen "University of Copenhagen") with a degree in medicine in 1947. Four years later, he was awarded the doctorate for his thesis, *A Study of Avidity Based on Rabbit Skin Responses to Diphtheria Toxin-Antitoxin Mixtures*.

## Research positions

From 1943 to 1956 Jerne was a research worker at the [Danish National Serum Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statens_Serum_Institut "Statens Serum Institut") and during this time he formulated a theory on [antibody](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antibody "Antibody") formation. It is said that Jerne got his revolutionary scientific idea while bicycling across the [Langebro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Langebro "Langebro") bridge in [Copenhagen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copenhagen "Copenhagen") on his way home from work.

The [antibody](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antibody "Antibody") formation theory gave Jerne international recognition and in 1956 Jerne went to work for the [World Health Organization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Health_Organization "World Health Organization") in Geneva, where he served as the Head of the Sections of Biological Standards and of [Immunology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immunology "Immunology"). He held this post for six years until moving to the United States and the [University of Pittsburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Pittsburgh "University of Pittsburgh") in 1962 to work as Professor of [Microbiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microbiology "Microbiology") and Chairman of the [Department of Microbiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Pittsburgh_School_of_Medicine "University of Pittsburgh School of Medicine") for four years. Jerne continued to do work for the [World Health Organization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Health_Organization "World Health Organization") as a member of the Expert Advisory Panel of [Immunology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immunology "Immunology") from 1962 and onwards.

In 1966 Jerne moved back to Europe and took up the position of Professor of Experimental Therapy at the [Johann Wolfgang Goethe University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Wolfgang_Goethe_University "Johann Wolfgang Goethe University") in Frankfurt. From 1966 to 1969 he was the Director of the [Paul-Ehrlich-Institut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul-Ehrlich-Institut_(Germany) "Paul-Ehrlich-Institut (Germany)"), also in [Frankfurt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frankfurt "Frankfurt"). In 1969 Jerne again switched jobs, this time to [Basel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basel "Basel") in Switzerland, where he was the Director of the [Basel Institute for Immunology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basel_Institute_for_Immunology "Basel Institute for Immunology") until his retirement in 1980. During the 1970s and 1980s, Jerne was a pioneer in the development of [immune network theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immune_network_theory "Immune network theory"). It was met by skepticism among his colleagues at first, [James Watson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Watson "James Watson") for example told Jerne bluntly that his theory "stinks".

## Family life

Jerne was married three times. He had two sons, Ivar Jerne (born 1936) and Donald Jerne (born 1941), with Tjek Jerne, a painter. Jerne had a third son, Andreas Wettstein, with Gertrud Wettstein, in 1971.
