**Albert Claude** (French pronunciation: [[albɛʁ klod]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/French "Help:IPA/French"); 24 August 1899 – 22 May 1983) was a [Belgian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belgium "Belgium")-[American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States "United States") [cell biologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_biologist "Cell biologist") and [medical doctor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_doctor "Medical doctor") who shared the [Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physiology_or_Medicine "Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine") in 1974 with [Christian de Duve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_de_Duve "Christian de Duve") and [George Emil Palade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Emil_Palade "George Emil Palade"). His elementary education started in a comprehensive primary school at Longlier, his birthplace. He served in the [British Intelligence Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Intelligence "British Intelligence") during the [First World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_World_War "First World War"), and got imprisoned in [concentration camps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concentration_camps "Concentration camps") twice. In recognition of his service, he was granted enrolment at the [University of Liège](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Liège "University of Liège") in [Belgium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belgium "Belgium") to study medicine without any formal education required for the course. He earned his [Doctor of Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Medicine "Doctor of Medicine") degree in 1928. Devoted to medical research, he initially joined German institutes in Berlin. In 1929 he found an opportunity to join the [Rockefeller Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rockefeller_University "Rockefeller University") in [New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_City "New York City"). At Rockefeller University he made his most groundbreaking achievements in cell biology. In 1930 he developed the technique of [cell fractionation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_fractionation "Cell fractionation"), by which he discovered the agent of the [Rous sarcoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rous_sarcoma_virus "Rous sarcoma virus"), as well as components of [cell organelles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_organelles "Cell organelles") such as the [mitochondrion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitochondrion "Mitochondrion"), [chloroplast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloroplast "Chloroplast"), [endoplasmic reticulum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endoplasmic_reticulum "Endoplasmic reticulum"), [Golgi apparatus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golgi_apparatus "Golgi apparatus"), [ribosome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ribosome "Ribosome"), and [lysosome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lysosome "Lysosome"). He was the first to employ the [electron microscope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_microscope "Electron microscope") in the field of biology. In 1945 he published the first detailed structure of cell. His collective works established the complex functional and structural properties of cells.

Claude served as director at [Jules Bordet Institute for Cancer Research and Treatment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institut_Jules_Bordet "Institut Jules Bordet") and *Laboratoire de Biologie Cellulaire et Cancérologie* in [Louvain-la-Neuve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louvain-la-Neuve "Louvain-la-Neuve"); Professor at the [Free University of Brussels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_University_of_Brussels_(1834–1969) "Free University of Brussels (1834–1969)"), the [University of Louvain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Université_catholique_de_Louvain "Université catholique de Louvain"), and [Rockefeller University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rockefeller_University "Rockefeller University"). For his pioneering works he received the [Louisa Gross Horwitz Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louisa_Gross_Horwitz_Prize "Louisa Gross Horwitz Prize") in 1970, together with his student [George Palade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Emil_Palade "George Emil Palade") and [Keith Porter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keith_R._Porter "Keith R. Porter"), the [Paul Ehrlich and Ludwig Darmstaedter Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Ehrlich_and_Ludwig_Darmstaedter_Prize "Paul Ehrlich and Ludwig Darmstaedter Prize") in 1971, and the [Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physiology_or_Medicine "Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine") in 1974 with Palade and his friend [Christian de Duve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_de_Duve "Christian de Duve").

## Early life and education

Albert Claude was born in 1899 (but according to civil register 1898) in Longlier, a hamlet in [Neufchâteau, Belgium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neufchâteau,_Luxembourg_Province "Neufchâteau, Luxembourg Province"), to Florentin Joseph Claude and Marie-Glaudice Watriquant Claude. He was the youngest among three brothers and one sister. His father was a Paris-trained baker and ran a bakery-cum-general store at Longlier valley near railroad station. His mother, who developed [breast cancer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breast_cancer "Breast cancer") in 1902, died when he was seven years old. He spent his pre-school life with his ailing mother. He started education in Longlier Primary School, a pluralistic school of single room, mixed grades, and all under one teacher. In spite of the inconveniences, he remarked the education system as "excellent." He served as a bell boy, ringing the church bell every morning at 6. Due to economic depression the family moved to [Athus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athus "Athus"), a prosperous region with steel mills, in 1907. He entered German-speaking school. After two years he was asked to look after his uncle who was disabled with cerebral haemorrhage in Longlier. He dropped out of school and practically nursed his uncle for several years. At the outbreak of the [First World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_World_War "First World War") he was apprenticed to steel mills and worked as an industrial designer. Inspired by [Winston Churchill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winston_Churchill "Winston Churchill"), then British [Minister of War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_of_War "Minister of War"), he joined the resistance and volunteered in [British Intelligence Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Intelligence "British Intelligence") in which he served during the whole war. At the end of the war he was decorated with the Interallied Medal along with [veteran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_veteran "War veteran") status. He then wanted to continue education. Since he had no formal secondary education, particularly required for medicine course, such as in [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language "Greek language") and [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin "Latin"), he tried to join School of Mining in [Liège](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liège "Liège"). By that time [Marcel Florkin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcel_Florkin "Marcel Florkin") became head of the Direction of Higher Education in Belgium's Ministry of Public Instruction, and under his administration passed the law that enabled war veterans to pursue higher education without diploma or other examinations. As an honour to his war service, he was given admission to the [University of Liège](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Liège "University of Liège") in 1922 to study medicine. He obtained his degree of [Doctor of Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Medicine "Doctor of Medicine") in 1928.

## Career

Claude received travel grants from Belgian government for his doctoral thesis on the [transplantation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organ_transplantation "Organ transplantation") of mouse cancers into rats. With this he worked his postdoctoral research in [Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berlin "Berlin") during the winter of 1928–1929, first at the Institut für Krebsforschung, and then at the [Kaiser Wilhelm Institute for Biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaiser_Wilhelm_Institute_for_Biology "Kaiser Wilhelm Institute for Biology"), [Dahlem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dahlem_(Berlin) "Dahlem (Berlin)"), in the laboratory of tissues culture of Prof. Albert Fischer. Back in Belgium he received fellowship in 1929 from the [Belgian American Educational Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belgian_American_Educational_Foundation "Belgian American Educational Foundation") (Commission for Relief in Belgium, CRB) for research in the [United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States "United States"). He applied for the Rockefeller Institute (now the [Rockefeller University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rockefeller_University "Rockefeller University")) in New York, USA. [Simon Flexner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simon_Flexner "Simon Flexner"), then Director, accepted his proposal to work on the isolation and identification of the Rous sarcoma virus. In September 1929 he joined the Rockefeller Institute. In 1930, he discovered the process of [cell fractionation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_fractionation "Cell fractionation"), which was groundbreaking in his time. The process consists of grinding up cells to break the membrane and release the cell's contents. He then filtered out the cell membranes and placed the remaining cell contents in a [centrifuge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centrifuge "Centrifuge") to separate them according to mass. He divided the [centrifuged](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centrifuged "Centrifuged") contents into fractions, each of a specific mass, and discovered that particular fractions were responsible for particular cell functions. In 1938 he identified and purified for the first time component of Rous sarcoma virus, the causal agent of [carcinoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carcinoma "Carcinoma"), as "ribose nucleoprotein" (eventually named [RNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ribonucleic_acid "Ribonucleic acid")). He was the first to use [electron microscope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_microscope "Electron microscope") to study [biological cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_(biology) "Cell (biology)"). Earlier electron microscopes were used only in physical researches. His first electron microscopic study was on the structure of mitochondria in 1945. He was given [American citizenship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naturalized_American_citizen "Naturalized American citizen") in 1941. He discovered that mitochondria are the "power houses" of all cells. He also discovered cytoplasmic granules full of RNA and named them "microsomes", which were later renamed ribosomes, the protein synthesizing machineries of cell. With his associate, [Keith Porter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keith_Porter "Keith Porter"), he found a "lace-work" structure that was eventually proven to be the major structural feature of the interior of all [eukaryotic cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eukaryotic_cells "Eukaryotic cells"). This was the discovery of endoplasmic reticulum (a Latin for "fishnet").

In 1949, he became Director of the Jules Bordet Institute for Cancer Research and Treatment ([Institut Jules Bordet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institut_Jules_Bordet "Institut Jules Bordet")) and Professor at the Faculty of Medicine of the [Free University of Brussels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_University_of_Brussels_(1834–1969) "Free University of Brussels (1834–1969)"), where he was emeritus in 1971.
In the mid sixties during an Electron Microscopy symposium in ([Bratislava](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bratislava "Bratislava"))-([Czechoslovakia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Czechoslovakia "Czechoslovakia")) organized by the ([UNESCO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UNESCO "UNESCO")) at the Czechoslovak Academy of Sciences, he meets young scientist Dr. Emil Mrena who was at that time head of the Electron Microscopy department. He invited him to come and work with him in Brussels, making it possible for Dr. Mrena's family to escape the communist regime. Their close collaboration gave fruition to 5 publications from 1969 to 1974.
With the support of his colleague and friend [Christian de Duve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_de_Duve "Christian de Duve"), he became in 1972 Professor at the University of Louvain ([Université catholique de Louvain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Université_catholique_de_Louvain "Université catholique de Louvain")) and Director of the "Laboratoire de Biologie Cellulaire et Cancérologie" in [Louvain-la-Neuve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louvain-la-Neuve "Louvain-la-Neuve") where he moved with Dr. Emil Mrena as sole collaborator. At the same time, he was appointed Professor at the [Rockefeller University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rockefeller_University "Rockefeller University"), an institution with which he had remained connected, in different degrees, since 1929.

## Personal life

He married Julia Gilder in 1935, with whom he had a daughter, Philippa. They were divorced while he was at Rockefeller. Philippa became a [neuroscientist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neuroscientist "Neuroscientist") and married [Antony Stretton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antony_Stretton "Antony Stretton").

Claude was known to be a bit of an eccentric and had close friendship with painters, including [Diego Rivera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diego_Rivera "Diego Rivera") and [Paul Delvaux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Delvaux "Paul Delvaux"), and musicians such as [Edgard Varèse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edgard_Varèse "Edgard Varèse").

After his retirement in 1971 from the [Université libre de Bruxelles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Université_libre_de_Bruxelles "Université libre de Bruxelles") and from the directorship of the Institut Jules Bordet, he continued his research at the [University of Louvain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Université_catholique_de_Louvain "Université catholique de Louvain") with his collaborator Dr. Emil Mrena, who ended up resigning in 1977 due to decreasing activity of the Laboratory, moving to other research works. It is said that he continued his research in seclusion until he died of [natural causes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_causes "Natural causes"), at his home in Brussels, on Sunday night on 22 May 1983, but he had stopped visiting his own laboratory in Louvain already in 1976 due to his weak health.
