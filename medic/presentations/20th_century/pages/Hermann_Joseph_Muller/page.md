**Hermann Joseph Muller** (December 21, 1890 – April 5, 1967) was an American [geneticist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geneticist "Geneticist") who was awarded the 1946 [Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physiology_or_Medicine "Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine") "for the discovery of the production of [mutations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutations "Mutations") by means of [X-ray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray "X-ray") irradiation". Muller warned of long-term dangers of [radioactive fallout](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radioactive_fallout "Radioactive fallout") from [nuclear war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_war "Nuclear war") and [nuclear testing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_testing "Nuclear testing"), which resulted in greater public scrutiny of these practices.

## Early life

Muller was born in [New York City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_City "New York City"), the son of Frances (Lyons) and Hermann Joseph Muller Sr., an artisan who worked with metals. Muller was a third-generation American whose father's ancestors were originally Catholic and came to the United States from [Koblenz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koblenz "Koblenz"). His mother's family was of mixed [Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jews "Jews") (descended from [Spanish and Portuguese Jews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_and_Portuguese_Jews "Spanish and Portuguese Jews")) and Anglican background, and had come from Britain. Among his first cousins was [Alfred Kroeber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Kroeber "Alfred Kroeber") (father of [Ursula Le Guin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ursula_Le_Guin "Ursula Le Guin")) and first cousins once removed was [Herbert J. Muller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbert_J._Muller "Herbert J. Muller"). As an adolescent, Muller attended a [Unitarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unitarianism "Unitarianism") church and considered himself a [pantheist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pantheist "Pantheist"); in high school, he became an [atheist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atheist "Atheist"). He excelled in the public schools. At 16, he entered [Columbia College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia_University "Columbia University"). From his first semester, he was interested in biology; he became an early convert of the [Mendelian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mendelian_inheritance "Mendelian inheritance")-[chromosome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromosome "Chromosome") theory of heredity—and the concept of genetic [mutations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutation "Mutation") and [natural selection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_selection "Natural selection") as the basis for [evolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolution "Evolution"). He formed a biology club and also became a proponent of [eugenics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugenics "Eugenics"); the connections between biology and society would be his perennial concern. Muller earned a [Bachelor of Arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_Arts "Bachelor of Arts") degree in 1910.

Muller remained at Columbia (the pre-eminent American zoology program at the time, due to [E. B. Wilson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmund_Beecher_Wilson "Edmund Beecher Wilson") and his students) for graduate school. He became interested in the *[Drosophila](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drosophila "Drosophila")* genetics work of [Thomas Hunt Morgan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Hunt_Morgan "Thomas Hunt Morgan")'s fly lab after undergraduate bottle washers [Alfred Sturtevant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Sturtevant "Alfred Sturtevant") and [Calvin Bridges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calvin_Bridges "Calvin Bridges") joined his biology club. In 1911–1912, he studied metabolism at [Cornell University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornell_University "Cornell University"), but remained involved with Columbia. He followed the [drosophilists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drosophilist "Drosophilist") as the first genetic maps emerged from Morgan's experiments, and joined Morgan's group in 1912 (after two years of informal participation).

In the fly group, Muller's contributions were primarily theoretical—explanations for experimental results and ideas and predictions for new experiments. In the emerging collaborative culture of the drosophilists, however, credit was assigned based on results rather than ideas; Muller felt cheated when he was left out of major publications.

## Career

In 1914, [Julian Huxley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julian_Huxley "Julian Huxley") offered Muller a position at the recently founded William Marsh Rice Institute, now [Rice University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rice_University "Rice University"); he hurried to complete his [Doctor of philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_philosophy "Doctor of philosophy") degree and moved to Houston for the beginning of the 1915–1916 academic year (his degree was issued in 1916). At Rice, Muller taught biology and continued *Drosophila* lab work. In 1918, he proposed an explanation for the dramatic discontinuous alterations in *[Oenothera lamarckiana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oenothera_lamarckiana "Oenothera lamarckiana")* that were the basis of [Hugo de Vries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugo_de_Vries "Hugo de Vries")'s
theory of [mutationism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutationism "Mutationism"): "balanced lethals" allowed the accumulation of recessive mutations, and rare [crossing over](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromosomal_crossover "Chromosomal crossover") events resulted in the sudden expression of these hidden traits. In other words, de Vries's experiments were explainable by the Mendelian-chromosome theory. Muller's work was increasingly focused on [mutation rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutation_rate "Mutation rate") and [lethal mutations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gene_lethality "Gene lethality"). In 1918, Morgan, short-handed because many of his students and assistants were drafted for the U.S. entry into [World War I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I "World War I"), convinced Muller to return to Columbia to teach and to expand his experimental program.

At Columbia, Muller and his collaborator and longtime friend [Edgar Altenburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edgar_Altenburg "Edgar Altenburg") continued the investigation of lethal mutations. The primary method for detecting such mutations was to measure the sex ratios of the offspring of female flies. They predicted the ratio would vary from 1:1 due to recessive mutations on the X chromosome, which would be expressed only in males (which lacked the functional allele on a second X chromosome). Muller found a strong temperature dependence in mutation rate, leading him to believe that spontaneous mutation was the dominant mode (and to initially discount the role of external factors such as ionizing radiation or chemical agents). In 1920, Muller and Altenburg coauthored a seminal paper in *[Genetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetics_(journal) "Genetics (journal)")* on "modifier genes" that determine the size of mutant *Drosophila* wings. In 1919, Muller made the important discovery of a mutant (later found to be a [chromosomal inversion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromosomal_inversion "Chromosomal inversion")) that appeared to suppress crossing over, which opened up new avenues in mutation-rate studies. However, his appointment at Columbia was not continued; he accepted an offer from the [University of Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Texas_at_Austin "University of Texas at Austin") and left Columbia after the summer of 1920.

Muller taught at the University of Texas from 1920 until 1932. Soon after returning to Texas, he married mathematics professor [Jessie Marie Jacobs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jessie_Marie_Jacobs_Muller_Offermann "Jessie Marie Jacobs Muller Offermann"), whom he had courted previously. In his early years at Texas, Muller's *Drosophila* work was slow going; the data from his mutation rate studies were difficult to interpret. In 1923, he began using [radium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radium "Radium") and [X-rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-rays "X-rays"), but the relationship between radiation and mutation was difficult to measure because such radiation also sterilized the flies. In this period, he also became involved with eugenics and human genetics. He carried out a study of twins separated at birth that seemed to indicate a strong hereditary component of [I.Q.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I.Q. "I.Q.") Muller was critical of the new directions of the eugenics movement (such as anti-immigration), but was hopeful about the prospects for positive eugenics. In 1932, at the [Third International Eugenics Congress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Eugenics_Conference "International Eugenics Conference"), Muller gave a speech and stated, "eugenics might yet perfect the human race, but only in a society consciously organized for the common good".

### Discovery of X-ray mutagenesis

In 1926, a series of major breakthroughs began. In November, Muller carried out two experiments with varied doses of X-rays, the second of which used the crossing over suppressor stock ("ClB") he had found in 1919. A clear, quantitative connection between radiation and lethal mutations quickly emerged. Muller's discovery created a media sensation after he delivered a paper entitled "The Problem of Genetic Modification" at the Fifth International Congress of Genetics in [Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berlin "Berlin"); it would make him one of the better-known public intellectuals of the early 20th century. By 1928, others had replicated his dramatic results, expanding them to other [model organisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Model_organism "Model organism"), such as [wasps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wasp "Wasp") and [maize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maize "Maize"). In the following years, he began publicizing the likely dangers of radiation exposure in humans (such as physicians who frequently operate X-ray equipment or shoe sellers who radiated their customers' feet).

His lab grew quickly, but it shrank again following the onset of the [Great Depression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Depression "Great Depression"). Especially after the stock market crash, Muller was increasingly pessimistic about the prospects of [capitalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capitalism "Capitalism"). Some of his visiting lab members were from the [USSR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USSR "USSR"), and he helped edit and distribute an illegal leftist student newspaper, *The Spark*. It was a difficult period for Muller both scientifically and personally; his marriage was falling apart, and he was increasingly dissatisfied with his life in Texas. Meanwhile, the waning of the eugenics movement, ironically hastened by his own work pointing to the previously ignored connections between environment and genetics, meant that his ideas on the future of human evolution had reduced impact in the public sphere. Muller's speech before the [Third International Eugenics Conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_International_Eugenics_Conference "Third International Eugenics Conference") in New York has been credited with marking the end of [Galtonism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Galton "Francis Galton"), and perhaps even [eugenics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugenics "Eugenics") itself, as a popular movement in the sciences. [H. Bentley Glass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H._Bentley_Glass "H. Bentley Glass"), a contemporary observer and Ph.D. student of Muller's, would say Muller's speech "just about finished the activity of the Eugenics Society". Muller told the assembled that eugenic ideals could no longer be achieved, because the capitalistic system produces the wrong motives of individual action, and he disdained the natures of the dominant class, and the type of society they were creating.

### Work in Europe

In September 1932, Muller moved to Berlin to work with the Russian expatriate geneticist [Nikolay Timofeeff-Ressovsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nikolay_Timofeeff-Ressovsky "Nikolay Timofeeff-Ressovsky"); a trip intended as a limited sabbatical stretched into an eight-year, five-country journey. In Berlin, he met two physicists who would later be significant to the biology community: [Niels Bohr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niels_Bohr "Niels Bohr") and [Max Delbrück](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Delbrück "Max Delbrück"). The [Nazi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi_Party "Nazi Party") movement was precipitating the rapid emigration of scientific talent from Germany, and Muller was particularly opposed to the politics of National Socialism. The FBI was investigating Muller because of his involvement with *The Spark*, so he chose instead to go to the Soviet Union (an environment better suited to his political beliefs). In 1933, Muller and his wife reconciled, and their son [David E. Muller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_E._Muller "David E. Muller") and she moved with Hermann to [Leningrad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leningrad "Leningrad"). There, at the Institute of Genetics, he imported the basic equipment for a *Drosophila* lab—including the flies—and set up shop. The institute was moved to [Moscow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moscow "Moscow") in 1934, and Muller and his wife were divorced in 1935.

In the USSR, Muller supervised a large and productive lab, and organized work on medical genetics. Most of his work involved further explorations of genetics and radiation. There he completed his eugenics book, *Out of the Night*, the main ideas of which dated to 1910. By 1936, however, [Joseph Stalin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Stalin "Joseph Stalin")'s repressive policies and the rise of [Lysenkoism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lysenkoism "Lysenkoism") was making the USSR an increasingly problematic place to live and work. Muller and many of the Russian genetics community did what they could to oppose [Trofim Lysenko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trofim_Lysenko "Trofim Lysenko") and his [Larmarckian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lamarckism "Lamarckism") evolutionary theory, but Muller was soon forced to leave the Soviet Union after Stalin read a translation of his eugenics book and was "displeased by it, and...ordered an attack prepared against it." By this time, Muller had already asked for a leave of absence. News of the [Lysenko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lysenkoism "Lysenkoism") trials had reached the United States, and his son David was being raised there, after his divorce. In the official declaration by the institute, biological determinism was rejected: "The development of society is subject not to biological laws but to higher social laws. Attempts to spread to humanity the laws of the animal kingdom are an attempt to lower the human being to the level of beasts."

Muller, with about 250 strains of *Drosophila*, moved to [University of Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Edinburgh "University of Edinburgh") in September 1937, after brief stays in [Madrid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madrid "Madrid") and [Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paris "Paris"). In 1938, with war on the horizon, he began looking for a permanent position back in the United States. He also began courting Dorothea "Thea" Kantorowicz, a German refugee; they were married in May 1939. The Seventh International Congress on Genetics was held in Edinburgh later that year; Muller wrote a "[Geneticists' Manifesto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geneticists'_Manifesto "Geneticists' Manifesto")" in response to the question: "How could the world's population be improved most effectively genetically?" He also engaged in a debate with the perennial genetics gadfly [Richard Goldschmidt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Goldschmidt "Richard Goldschmidt") over the existence of the gene, for which little direct physical evidence existed at the time.

### Later career

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/50/First_Street_1001%2C_Muller_House%2C_Vinegar_Hill_HD.jpg/250px-First_Street_1001%2C_Muller_House%2C_Vinegar_Hill_HD.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:First_Street_1001,_Muller_House,_Vinegar_Hill_HD.jpg)

Muller's house in [Bloomington, Indiana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bloomington,_Indiana "Bloomington, Indiana")

When Muller returned to the United States in 1940, he took an untenured research position at [Amherst College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amherst_College "Amherst College"), in the department of [Otto C. Glaser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otto_C._Glaser "Otto C. Glaser"). After the U.S. entry into [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II"), his position was extended indefinitely and expanded to include teaching. His *Drosophila* work in this period focused on measuring the rate of spontaneous (as opposed to radiation-induced) mutations. Muller's publication rate decreased greatly in this period, from a combination of lack of lab workers and experimentally challenging projects. However, he also worked as an adviser in the [Manhattan Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manhattan_Project "Manhattan Project") (though he did not know that was what it was), as well as a study of the mutational effects of [radar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radar "Radar"). Muller's appointment was ended after the 1944–1945 academic year, and despite difficulties stemming from his socialist political activities, he found a position as professor of zoology at [Indiana University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indiana_University_(Bloomington) "Indiana University (Bloomington)"). Here, he lived in a [Dutch Colonial Revival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Colonial_Revival_architecture "Dutch Colonial Revival architecture") house in [Bloomington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bloomington,_Indiana "Bloomington, Indiana")'s [Vinegar Hill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vinegar_Hill_Historic_District "Vinegar Hill Historic District") neighborhood.

In 1946, Muller was awarded the [Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physiology_or_Medicine "Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine"), "for the discovery that mutations can be induced by X-rays". Genetics, and especially the physical and physiological nature of the gene, was becoming a central topic in biology, and X-ray mutagenesis was a key to many recent advances, among them [George Beadle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Beadle "George Beadle") and [Edward Tatum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Tatum "Edward Tatum")'s work on *[Neurospora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neurospora "Neurospora")* that established in 1941 the [one gene-one enzyme hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One_gene-one_enzyme_hypothesis "One gene-one enzyme hypothesis"). In Muller's Nobel Prize lecture, he argued that no threshold dose of radiation existed that did not produce [mutagenesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutagenesis "Mutagenesis"), which led to the adoption of the [linear no-threshold model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_no-threshold_model "Linear no-threshold model") of radiation on cancer risks.

The Nobel Prize, in the wake of the [atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_bombings_of_Hiroshima_and_Nagasaki "Atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki"), focused public attention on a subject Muller had been publicizing for two decades - the dangers of radiation. In 1952, [nuclear fallout](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_fallout "Nuclear fallout") became a public issue; since [Operation Crossroads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Crossroads "Operation Crossroads"), more and more evidence had been leaking out about [radiation sickness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiation_sickness "Radiation sickness") and death caused by [nuclear testing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_testing "Nuclear testing"). Muller and many other scientists pursued an array of political activities to defuse the threat of [nuclear war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_warfare "Nuclear warfare"). With the [Castle Bravo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Castle_Bravo "Castle Bravo") fallout controversy in 1954, the issue became even more urgent. In 1955, Muller was one of 11 prominent intellectuals to sign the [Russell–Einstein Manifesto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russell–Einstein_Manifesto "Russell–Einstein Manifesto"), the upshot of which was the first [1957 Pugwash Conference on Science and World Affairs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pugwash_Conferences_on_Science_and_World_Affairs "Pugwash Conferences on Science and World Affairs"), which addressed the control of nuclear weapons. He was a signatory (with many other scientists) of the 1958 petition to the United Nations, calling for an end to nuclear weapons testing, which was initiated by the Nobel Prize-winning chemist [Linus Pauling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linus_Pauling "Linus Pauling").

Muller's opinions on the effect of radiation on mutagenesis were used by [Rachel Carson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rachel_Carson "Rachel Carson") in her book *[Silent Spring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silent_Spring "Silent Spring")*, however, his opinions have been criticized by some scientists; geneticist [James F. Crow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_F._Crow "James F. Crow") called Muller's view "alarmist" and wrote that it created in the public "an irrational fear of low-level radiation relative to other risks". It has been argued that Muller's opinion was not supported by studies on the [survivors of the atomic bombings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hibakusha "Hibakusha"), or by research on mice, and that he ignored another study that contradicted the [linear no-threshold model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_no-threshold_model "Linear no-threshold model") he supported, thereby affecting the formulation of policy that favored this model. He was also accused of suppressing opposing views and of being part of a US National Academy of Sciences Committee that misrepresented the research record to secure continued funding and strengthen his influence on US health policy.

Muller was elected to the [American Academy of Arts and Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Academy_of_Arts_and_Sciences "American Academy of Arts and Sciences") in 1942 and the [American Philosophical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Philosophical_Society "American Philosophical Society") in 1947 Muller was awarded the [Linnean Society of London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linnean_Society_of_London "Linnean Society of London")'s [Darwin-Wallace Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darwin-Wallace_Medal "Darwin-Wallace Medal") in 1958 and the Kimber Genetics Award of the U.S. National Academy of Sciences, of which he was a member, in 1955. He served as president of the American Humanist Association from 1956 to 1958. The [American Mathematical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Mathematical_Society "American Mathematical Society") selected him as its Gibbs Lecturer for 1958. He retired in 1964. The *Drosophila* basic units of inheritance, their [chromosomal arms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromosome "Chromosome"), are named "[Muller elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muller_elements?action=edit&redlink=1 "Muller elements")" in Muller's honor. He died in 1967.

H. J. Muller and science fiction writer [Ursula K. Le Guin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ursula_K._Le_Guin "Ursula K. Le Guin") were [first cousins once removed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_cousins_once_removed "First cousins once removed"); his father (Hermann J. Muller Sr.) and her father's mother (Johanna Muller Kroeber) were siblings, the children of Nicholas Müller, who immigrated to the United States in 1848, and at that time dropped the umlaut from his name. Another cousin was [Herbert J. Muller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbert_J._Muller "Herbert J. Muller"), whose grandfather Otto was another son of Nicholas and a sibling of Hermann Sr. and Johanna.

### Legacy

In a recent retrospective article about Muller's contribution, [James Haber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Haber "James Haber") wrote as follows:

> Drosophila geneticist, Hermann Muller, envisioned the fundamental principles that such a molecule must have: to be auto-assembling and to be mutable but then again stable. He followed his prescient review of these properties with a remarkable prediction: learning about the hereditary material and its properties would not come from studying Drosophila, but from studying bacteria and their bacteriophages.

## Global policy

He was one of the signatories of the agreement to convene a convention for drafting a [world constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_constitution "World constitution"). As a result, for the first time in human history, a [World Constituent Assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Constituent_Assembly "World Constituent Assembly") convened to draft and adopt a [Constitution for the Federation of Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_for_the_Federation_of_Earth "Constitution for the Federation of Earth").

## Personal life

Muller had a daughter, Helen J. Muller, now a professor emerita at the [University of New Mexico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_New_Mexico "University of New Mexico"). Her daughter, [Mala Htun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mala_Htun "Mala Htun"), was also a professor at the University of New Mexico until her death in January 2025. His son, [David E. Muller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_E._Muller "David E. Muller"), professor emeritus of mathematics and computer science at the [University of Illinois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Illinois "University of Illinois") and at [New Mexico State University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Mexico_State_University "New Mexico State University"), died in 2008 in [Las Cruces, New Mexico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Las_Cruces,_New_Mexico "Las Cruces, New Mexico"). David's mother was Jessie Jacobs Muller Offermann (1890–1954), Hermann's first wife. Helen's mother was Dorothea Kantorowicz Muller (1909–1986), Hermann's second wife, who came to the U.S. in 1940 as a German Jewish refugee. He had a brief affair with [Milly Bennett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milly_Bennett "Milly Bennett").
