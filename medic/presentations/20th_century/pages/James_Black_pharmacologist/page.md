**Sir James Whyte Black** born (14 June 1924 – Died 22 March 2010) was a Scottish [physician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physician "Physician") and [pharmacologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pharmacology "Pharmacology"). Together with [Gertrude B. Elion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gertrude_B._Elion "Gertrude B. Elion") and [George H. Hitchings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_H._Hitchings "George H. Hitchings"), he shared the [Nobel Prize for Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physiology_or_Medicine "Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine") in 1988 for pioneering strategies for [rational drug-design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drug_design "Drug design"), which, in his case, led to the development of [propranolol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propranolol "Propranolol") and [cimetidine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cimetidine "Cimetidine"). Black established a Veterinary Physiology department at the [University of Glasgow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Glasgow "University of Glasgow"), where he became interested in the effects of [adrenaline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adrenaline "Adrenaline") on the [human heart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_heart "Human heart"). He went to work for [ICI Pharmaceuticals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_Chemical_Industries "Imperial Chemical Industries") in 1958 and, while there, developed propranolol, a [beta blocker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beta_blocker "Beta blocker") used for the treatment of heart disease. Black was also responsible for the development of [cimetidine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cimetidine "Cimetidine"), an H2 receptor antagonist, a drug used to treat [stomach ulcers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stomach_ulcers "Stomach ulcers").

## Early life and education

Black was born on 14 June 1924 in [Uddingston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uddingston "Uddingston"), Lanarkshire, the fourth of five sons of a [Baptist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baptist_Union_of_Scotland "Baptist Union of Scotland") family which traced its origins to [Balquhidder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balquhidder "Balquhidder"), Perthshire. His father Walter Black was a mining engineer and his mother was Catherine Reid Whyte. He was brought up in Fife, educated at [Beath High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beath_High_School "Beath High School"), Cowdenbeath, and, at the age of 15, won a scholarship to the [University of St Andrews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_St_Andrews "University of St Andrews"). His family had been too poor to send him to university and he had been persuaded to sit the St Andrews entrance exam by his maths teacher at Beath.

Until 1967, [University College, Dundee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Dundee "University of Dundee") was the site for all clinical medical activity for the [University of St Andrews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_St_Andrews "University of St Andrews"). He matriculated at University College (which eventually became the [University of Dundee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Dundee "University of Dundee")) in 1943 and graduated from [University of St Andrews School of Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_St_Andrews_School_of_Medicine "University of St Andrews School of Medicine") with an MB ChB in 1946.

After graduating, he stayed at University College to join the physiology department as an assistant lecturer before taking a lecturer position at King Edward VII College of Medicine in Singapore that later became part of the [University of Malaya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Malaya "University of Malaya"). Black had decided against a career as a medical practitioner as he objected to what he considered the insensitive treatment of patients at the time.

## Career

Black had large debts upon his graduation from university, so he took a teaching job in Singapore for three years, before moving to London in 1950 and then on to join the [University of Glasgow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Glasgow "University of Glasgow") (Veterinary School) where he established the Veterinary Physiology Department and developed an interest in the way [adrenaline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adrenaline "Adrenaline") affects the [human heart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_heart "Human heart"), particularly in those suffering from [angina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angina_pectoris "Angina pectoris"). Having formulated a theory of an approach by which the effects of adrenaline might be annulled, he joined [ICI Pharmaceuticals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_Chemical_Industries "Imperial Chemical Industries") in 1958, remaining with the company until 1964, during which time he invented [propranolol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propranolol "Propranolol"), which later became the world's best-selling drug. During this time Black pioneered a method of research whereby drug molecules were purposefully built instead of being synthesised first and then investigated for their potential medical uses. The discovery of propranolol was hailed as the greatest breakthrough in the treatment of heart disease since the discovery of [digitalis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digitalis#Uses "Digitalis").

At the same time, Black was developing a similar method of inventing drugs for treatment of [stomach ulcers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stomach_ulcers "Stomach ulcers"), but ICI did not wish to pursue the idea so Black resigned in 1964 and joined [Smith, Kline and French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smith,_Kline_and_French "Smith, Kline and French") where he worked for nine years until 1973. While there, Black developed his second major drug, [cimetidine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cimetidine "Cimetidine"), which was launched under the brand name Tagamet in 1975 and soon outsold propranolol to become the world's largest-selling prescription drug.

Black was appointed professor, and head of department, of pharmacology at [University College London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_College_London "University College London") in 1973 where he established a new undergraduate course in [medicinal chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medicinal_chemistry "Medicinal chemistry") but he became frustrated by the lack of funding for research and accepted the post of director of therapeutic research at the [Wellcome Research Laboratories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wellcome_Research_Laboratories "Wellcome Research Laboratories") in 1978. However he did not agree with his immediate boss there, [Sir John Vane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Vane "John Vane"), and resigned in 1984. Black then became Professor of Analytical Pharmacology at the Rayne Institute of [King's College London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King's_College_London "King's College London") medical school, where he remained until 1992. He established the James Black Foundation in 1988 with funding from [Johnson & Johnson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johnson_&_Johnson "Johnson & Johnson") and led a team of 25 scientists in drugs research, including work on [gastrin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gastrin "Gastrin") inhibitors which can prevent some stomach cancers.

Black contributed to basic scientific and clinical knowledge in [cardiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiology "Cardiology"), both as a physician and as a basic scientist. His invention of [propranolol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propranolol "Propranolol"), the [beta adrenergic receptor antagonist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beta_blocker "Beta blocker") that revolutionised the medical management of [angina pectoris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angina_pectoris "Angina pectoris"), is considered to be one of the most important contributions to clinical medicine and [pharmacology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pharmacology "Pharmacology") of the 20th century. Propranolol has been described as the greatest breakthrough in heart disease treatments since the 18th century discovery of [digitalis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digitalis "Digitalis") and has benefited millions of people. Black's method of research, his discoveries about adrenergic pharmacology, and his clarification of the mechanisms of cardiac action are all strengths of his work.

He was greatly involved in the synthesis of [cimetidine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cimetidine "Cimetidine"), at the time a revolutionary drug for the treatment and prevention of [peptic ulcers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peptic_ulcer "Peptic ulcer"). Cimetidine was the first of a new class of drugs, the [H2-receptor antagonists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H2-receptor_antagonist "H2-receptor antagonist").

### Chancellor of the University of Dundee

In 1980, Black's association with the [University of Dundee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Dundee "University of Dundee") was renewed when the institution recognised his many achievements by conferring him with the Honorary Degree of Doctor of Laws. In 1992 he accepted an offer to succeed the [16th Earl of Dalhousie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simon_Ramsay,_16th_Earl_of_Dalhousie "Simon Ramsay, 16th Earl of Dalhousie") as [chancellor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chancellor_(education) "Chancellor (education)") of the university and was installed as chancellor at the award ceremony held in [Dundee Repertory Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dundee_Repertory_Theatre "Dundee Repertory Theatre") on 29 April 1992. Appropriately the first degree he conferred was to Professor [Robert Campbell Garry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Campbell_Garry "Robert Campbell Garry"), who had been responsible for his original appointment at University College Dundee. Sir James remarked at this ceremony that by returning to Dundee he was "in a real sense, coming home".

As Chancellor, Sir James Black did much to promote the University of Dundee and was a popular figure within the university. He was awarded a second honorary degree, that of Doctor of Science, in 2005. He retired from his post the following year, and his association with the University of Dundee was marked with launching of the £20 million Sir James Black Centre. The centre, intended to promote interdisciplinary research in the life sciences, was opened by [Sydney Brenner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sydney_Brenner "Sydney Brenner") in 2006. Sir James Black himself visited the centre in October 2006 and was reportedly excited and pleased by what he saw.

A portrait of Black in his chancellor's robes, by Helene Train, is held as part of the university's fine art collection. The portrait is currently displayed in the foyer of the Sir James Black Centre.

## Honours and awards

Black was made a [Knight Bachelor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knight_Bachelor "Knight Bachelor") on 10 February 1981 for services to medical research, receiving the honour from the Queen at [Buckingham Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buckingham_Palace "Buckingham Palace"). On 26 May 2000, he was appointed to the [Order of Merit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_Merit "Order of Merit"), of which there are only 24 members at any one time, by [Queen Elizabeth II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_II_of_the_United_Kingdom "Elizabeth II of the United Kingdom").

Black was elected a [Fellow of the Royal Society (FRS) in 1976](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Fellows_of_the_Royal_Society_elected_in_1976 "List of Fellows of the Royal Society elected in 1976") and the same year he was awarded the [Lasker award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Lasker_Award_for_Clinical_Medical_Research "Albert Lasker Award for Clinical Medical Research"). His certificate of election to the [Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society "Royal Society") reads:

> Has made outstanding contributions, based on fundamental considerations of receptor theory, to the development of new drugs of clinical importance. Introduced the first effective beta blockers and analysed their cardiovascular effects. These drugs have opened up new perspectives for the treatment of angina pectoris, cardiac irregularities and hypertension. More recently, in a concerted drive carried out with great vision, he has developed a new type of histamine antagonist capable of inhibiting gastric acid secretion induced by [histamine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Histamine "Histamine") or [pentagastrin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pentagastrin "Pentagastrin"). Apart from its practical implications this work has considerable fundamental importance since it provided evidence for the existence of a new type of histamine receptor. This work has also provided experimental support for the hypothesis that endogenous histamine may be involved in the secretion of acid in the stomach.

In 1979, he was awarded the [Artois-Baillet Latour Health Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artois-Baillet_Latour_Health_Prize "Artois-Baillet Latour Health Prize"). Black was awarded the [Cameron Prize for Therapeutics of the University of Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cameron_Prize_for_Therapeutics_of_the_University_of_Edinburgh "Cameron Prize for Therapeutics of the University of Edinburgh") in 1980. In 1982 Black was awarded the [Wolf Prize in Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolf_Prize_in_Medicine "Wolf Prize in Medicine"), and the year after the [Scheele Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scheele_Award "Scheele Award"). He was awarded the 1988 [Nobel Prize in Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physiology_or_Medicine "Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine") along with [Gertrude B. Elion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gertrude_B._Elion "Gertrude B. Elion") and [George H. Hitchings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_H._Hitchings "George H. Hitchings") for their work on drug development. Black's [Nobel Prize medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_medal "Nobel Prize medal") was donated to the [National Museum of Scotland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Museum_of_Scotland "National Museum of Scotland") in 2009.

In 1994, he received the [Ellison-Cliffe Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ellison-Cliffe_Lecture "Ellison-Cliffe Lecture") from the [Royal Society of Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society_of_Medicine "Royal Society of Medicine") and in 2004 the [Royal Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Medal "Royal Medal") from the [Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society "Royal Society"). In 2008, he was Awarded a [Lifetime Achievement Award](https://www.realwire.com/realResource.asp?ReleaseID=10744&d=w&name=SIRJAMESBLACK%2Ejpg&title=Sir+James+Black) at the Medical Futures Innovation Awards, accompanied by his wife Professor Rona Mackie Black, in front of a distinguished audience of one thousand guests.

In the town of [Lochgelly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lochgelly "Lochgelly"), Fife, there is a street called Sir James Black Gait.

On 23 November 2018, the University of Glasgow renamed the West Medical Building in honour of Black. Professor Sir Anton Muscatelli, principal and vice-chancellor of the University of Glasgow, said: “Sir James was undoubtedly one of the greatest scientists of the modern age. Through his commitment, intellect and insight he achieved a global impact and his discoveries continue to improve the lives of many.” The unveiling of a plaque was attended by Sir James Black’s wife, Professor Rona Mackie Black, emeritus professor and senior research fellow in public health within the university's Institute of Health and Wellbeing.

*James Black Place* at [Ninewells Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ninewells_Hospital "Ninewells Hospital") is named in his honour.

## Personal life

Black met Hilary Joan Vaughan (1924–1986) at a university ball in 1944 and the couple married in 1946 upon his graduation. He described her as the "mainspring" of his life until she died aged 61 in [Surrey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surrey "Surrey"). The couple had a daughter, Stephanie, born in 1951. Black remarried in 1994, to Professor [Rona MacKie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rona_MacKie "Rona MacKie"). Black was a very private man who was averse to publicity and was horrified to discover he had won the Nobel Prize.

Black died, aged 85, on the morning of 22 March 2010, after a long illness. His death was announced by the [University of Dundee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Dundee "University of Dundee"), where Black served as Chancellor from 1992 to 2006. His funeral was held on 29 March at St. Columba's Church, London. He is buried at the Ardclach cemetery, a parish established in 1655, near Nairn, Scotland. Upon hearing of Black's death, Professor Pete Downes, Principal and Vice-Chancellor of the University of Dundee said Black "was a great scientist, but he was also a great man to know" while the [BBC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC "BBC") said he was "hailed as one of the great Scottish scientists of the 20th Century". He was described by *[The Daily Telegraph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Daily_Telegraph "The Daily Telegraph")* as the man who earned the most for the [pharmaceutical industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pharmaceutical_industry "Pharmaceutical industry") through his drug development, though he received little personal financial gain from his work.

Shortly after his death the [Bute Medical School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_St_Andrews_School_of_Medicine "University of St Andrews School of Medicine") of the University of St Andrews, where Black had studied for his initial degree in medicine, announced that an honorary 'Sir James Black Chair of Medicine' would be created. In September 2010 the first chair of medicine at the university was given to Professor Stephen H Gillespie MD, DSc, FRCP (Edin), FRC Path, who left his post as professor of medical microbiology at [UCL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_College_London "University College London").
