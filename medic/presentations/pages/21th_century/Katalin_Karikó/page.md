**Katalin** "**Kati**" **Karikó** (born 17 January 1955) is a Hungarian and American [biochemist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biochemist "Biochemist") who specializes in ribonucleic acid ([RNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RNA "RNA"))-mediated mechanisms, particularly [in vitro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/In_vitro "In vitro")-[transcribed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transcription_(biology) "Transcription (biology)") [messenger RNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Messenger_RNA "Messenger RNA") (mRNA) for [protein replacement therapy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protein_replacement_therapy "Protein replacement therapy"). Karikó laid the scientific groundwork for [mRNA vaccines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MRNA_vaccine "MRNA vaccine"), overcoming major obstacles and skepticism in the scientific community. Karikó received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 2023 for her work, along with American [immunologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immunologist "Immunologist") [Drew Weissman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drew_Weissman "Drew Weissman").

Karikó co-founded and was CEO of RNARx from 2006 to 2013. From 2013 to 2022, she was associated with [BioNTech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BioNTech "BioNTech") RNA Pharmaceuticals, first as a vice president and promoted to senior vice president in 2019. In 2022, she left BioNTech to devote more time to research. In 2021, she received an honorary doctorate from the [University of Szeged](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Szeged "University of Szeged") in Hungary, where she has since become a professor. While Karikó has also been associated with the [University of Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Pennsylvania "University of Pennsylvania"), which would benefit financially from her eventual discovery, the university had actively discouraged her from pursuing research by underfunding and deprioritizing work on mRNA. After being [demoted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demotion "Demotion") by the University of Pennsylvania in 1995, Karikó was never granted [tenure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academic_tenure "Academic tenure") and joined BioNTech in 2013 after the university had declined to reinstate her.

Karikó's work includes scientific research on RNA-mediated immune activation, resulting in the co-discovery with Drew Weissman of the [nucleoside modifications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nucleoside-modified_messenger_RNA "Nucleoside-modified messenger RNA") that suppress the [immunogenicity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immunogenicity "Immunogenicity") of RNA. This is seen as a further contribution to the therapeutic use of mRNA. Together with Weissman, she holds [United States patents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_patent "United States patent") for the application of non-immunogenic, nucleoside-modified RNA. This technology has been licensed by BioNTech and [Moderna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moderna "Moderna") to develop their protein replacement technologies, but it was also used for their [COVID-19 vaccines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19_vaccine "COVID-19 vaccine").

The messenger RNA-based technology developed by Karikó and the two most effective vaccines based on it, [BioNTech/Pfizer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pfizer–BioNTech_COVID-19_vaccine "Pfizer–BioNTech COVID-19 vaccine") and Moderna, formed the basis for the effective and successful fight against [SARS-CoV-2 virus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SARS-CoV-2_virus "SARS-CoV-2 virus") worldwide and contributed significantly to the containment of the [COVID-19 pandemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic "COVID-19 pandemic"). For their work, [Karikó and Weissman have received numerous other awards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_awards_and_honors_received_by_Katalin_Karikó "List of awards and honors received by Katalin Karikó") besides the Nobel, including the [Lasker–DeBakey Clinical Medical Research Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lasker–DeBakey_Clinical_Medical_Research_Award "Lasker–DeBakey Clinical Medical Research Award"), [Time Magazine's Hero of the Year 2021](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_(magazine) "Time (magazine)"), and the [Tang Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tang_Prize "Tang Prize") Award in Biopharmaceutical Science in 2022.

## Early life and education

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b2/Karik%C3%B3_Katalin_with_Statue_of_Albert_Szent-Gy%C3%B6rgyi.jpg/330px-Karik%C3%B3_Katalin_with_Statue_of_Albert_Szent-Gy%C3%B6rgyi.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Karikó_Katalin_with_Statue_of_Albert_Szent-Györgyi.jpg)

Karikó Katalin with a statue of [Albert Szent-Györgyi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Szent-Györgyi "Albert Szent-Györgyi"), a fellow [Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physiology_or_Medicine "Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine") winner, at the [University of Szeged](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Szeged "University of Szeged")

Katalin Karikó was born in [Szolnok](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Szolnok "Szolnok"), and grew up in [Kisújszállás](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kisújszállás "Kisújszállás"), Hungary, in a small home without running water, a refrigerator, or television. Her father János was a butcher, and her mother was a bookkeeper. Her father was punished for participating in the [revolt of 1956](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungarian_Revolution_of_1956 "Hungarian Revolution of 1956"). She excelled in science during her primary education, earning third place in Hungary in a biology competition.

A 2024 retrospective by the University of Szeged further documents Karikó's upbringing in Kisújszállás, Hungary, where her early passion for chemistry and biology was nurtured through academic competitions, achieving top national rankings by eighth grade. This foundation is recognized as a key influence on her later breakthroughs in mRNA research.

Karikó obtained a [BSc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_science "Bachelor of science") degree in [biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biology "Biology") in 1978 and her [PhD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Philosophy "Doctor of Philosophy") in [biochemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biochemistry "Biochemistry") in 1982, both from the [University of Szeged](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Szeged "University of Szeged"). She worked with Jenő Tomasz and continued her [postdoctoral research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postdoctoral_researcher "Postdoctoral researcher") at the [Institute of Biochemistry, Biological Research Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biological_Research_Centre_(Hungarian_Academy_of_Sciences) "Biological Research Centre (Hungarian Academy of Sciences)") (BRC) of Hungary. From 1978 until 1985, she was listed as an intelligence asset by the Communist [Hungarian secret police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Internal_Affairs_III "Ministry of Internal Affairs III"), something she says she was [blackmailed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blackmail "Blackmail") into out of fear of repercussions on her career or reprisals against her father. She claims that she did not provide them with information nor was she active as an agent.

In 1985, her lab at the BRC lost its funding, and Karikó sought work at institutions in other countries. After being offered a research position by [Robert J. Suhadolnik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_J._Suhadolnik "Robert J. Suhadolnik") of [Temple University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_University "Temple University"), Karikó left Hungary for the United States with her husband and [two-year-old daughter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susan_Francia "Susan Francia"), carrying her daughter's teddy bear stuffed with £900 that they had received from selling their car and exchanging currency on the [black market](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_market "Black market").

## Career

Between 1985 and 1988, Karikó was a postdoctoral fellow at [Temple University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_University "Temple University") in Philadelphia. Karikó participated in a clinical trial in which patients with AIDS, [hematologic diseases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hematologic_disease "Hematologic disease"), and [chronic fatigue syndrome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chronic_fatigue_syndrome "Chronic fatigue syndrome") were treated with [double-stranded RNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RNA#Double-stranded_RNA "RNA") (dsRNA). This was considered groundbreaking research, as the molecular mechanism of [interferon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interferon "Interferon") induction by dsRNA was not known, although the antiviral and [antineoplastic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antineoplastic "Antineoplastic") effects of interferons were well-documented.

In 1988, Karikó accepted a job at [Johns Hopkins University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johns_Hopkins_University "Johns Hopkins University") without first informing her lab advisor Suhadolnik of her intention to leave Temple, as recounted in [Gregory Zuckerman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregory_Zuckerman "Gregory Zuckerman")'s 2021 book *A Shot to Save the World*. Suhadolnik told her that if she went to Johns Hopkins, he would have her [deported](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deportation "Deportation"), and subsequently reported her to U.S. immigration authorities, claiming that she was "illegally" in the United States. In the time it took her to successfully challenge the resulting extradition order, Johns Hopkins withdrew the job offer. Suhadolnik "continued bad-mouthing Karikó, making it impossible for her to get a new position" at other institutions, until she met a researcher at [Bethesda Naval Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bethesda_Naval_Hospital "Bethesda Naval Hospital") who "had his own difficult history with Suhadolnik". Karikó subsequently confirmed that the incident had happened as Zuckerman described, but emphasized that "more importantly I was always grateful to [Suhadolnik for] sending me the [IAP66 form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Immigration_and_Customs_Enforcement "U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement") in 1985, for the opportunity he gave me to work in his lab", noting that "when I gave a lecture [at Temple, a] couple of years later, I thanked him for the science I learned from him." From 1988 to 1989, she worked at the [Uniformed Services University of the Health Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniformed_Services_University_of_the_Health_Sciences "Uniformed Services University of the Health Sciences") in [Bethesda, Maryland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bethesda,_Maryland "Bethesda, Maryland") where she worked with signal protein interferons.

In 1989, she was hired by the [University of Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Pennsylvania "University of Pennsylvania") to work with [cardiologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiology "Cardiology") Elliot Barnathan on [messenger RNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Messenger_RNA "Messenger RNA") (mRNA). In 1990, while an [adjunct professor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjunct_professor "Adjunct professor") at the [Perelman School of Medicine at the University of Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perelman_School_of_Medicine_at_the_University_of_Pennsylvania "Perelman School of Medicine at the University of Pennsylvania"), Karikó submitted her first grant application in which she proposed establishing mRNA-based [gene therapy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gene_therapy "Gene therapy"). Ever since, mRNA-based therapy has been Karikó's primary research interest. However, in the 1990s, mRNA fell out of favor as many researchers, biotechs, and pharmaceutical companies doubted its potential. Though supported by Elliot Barnathan (who left UPenn in 1997) and [David Langer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Langer_(neurosurgeon) "David Langer (neurosurgeon)") (who then hired her), Karikó found it difficult to gain funding. She was initially on track to become a full professor, but after repeated grant rejections the university demoted her in 1995. Nevertheless, she chose to remain and continue her mRNA research.

Karikó (right) with Drew Weissman in 2022

In 1997, she met [Drew Weissman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drew_Weissman "Drew Weissman"), a professor of [immunology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immunology "Immunology") who had recently arrived at the University of Pennsylvania. They began to exchange ideas and then to collaborate. Weissman's funding was critical in helping Karikó to continue and extend her research and the combination of Weissman's immunology and Karikó's biochemistry was extremely effective. They began to move the technology forward, solving problems one at a time, and eventually gaining recognition. Weissman has commented "We had to fight the entire way." Karikó's persistence was noted as exceptional against the norms of academic research work conditions.

> Kate was really just unbelievable... She was always incredibly inquisitive. She read voraciously. She would always know the latest technology or the latest paper, even if it was in a totally different area, and she'd put two and two together and say, 'Well why don't we do this?' Or, 'Why don't we try this formulation?'

— Elliot Barnathan

Before 2005, a major problem with the proposed therapeutic use of mRNA was that in vivo use led to inflammatory reactions. A key insight came about when Karikó focused on why [transfer RNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transfer_RNA "Transfer RNA") (tRNA), used as a [control](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_control "Scientific control") in an experiment, did not provoke the same immune reaction as mRNA. A series of landmark studies beginning in 2005 demonstrated that while synthetic mRNA was highly inflammatory, tRNA was noninflammatory. Karikó and Weissman determined how specific nucleoside modifications in mRNA led to a reduced immune response: by replacing [uridine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uridine "Uridine") with [pseudouridine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudouridine "Pseudouridine"). Their key finding of a chemical modification of mRNA to render it non-immunogenic was rejected by the journals *[Nature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nature_(journal) "Nature (journal)")* and *[Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_(journal) "Science (journal)")*, but eventually accepted by the publication *[Immunity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immunity_(journal) "Immunity (journal)")*.

Another important achievement by the researchers was the development of a delivery technique to package the mRNA in [lipid nanoparticles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lipid_nanoparticles "Lipid nanoparticles"), a novel pharmaceutical drug delivery system for mRNA. The mRNA is injected into tiny fat droplets (lipid nanoparticles) which protect the fragile molecule until it can reach the desired area of the body. They demonstrated its effectiveness in animals.

Katalin Karikó wall painting in [Budapest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Budapest "Budapest"), [Hungary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungary "Hungary")

Karikó and Weissman founded a small company, RNARx, and in 2006 and 2013 received patents for the use of several modified nucleosides to reduce the antiviral immune response to mRNA. Soon afterward, the University of Pennsylvania sold the intellectual property license to Gary Dahl, the head of a lab supply company that eventually became Cellscript. Weeks later, [Flagship Pioneering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flagship_Pioneering "Flagship Pioneering"), the [venture capital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venture_capital "Venture capital") company backing [Moderna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moderna "Moderna"), contacted her in an attempt to license the patent, at which point Karikó had to tell them it was no longer available.

In 2006, Katalin Karikó reached out to biochemist Ian MacLachlan to work with him on the chemically altered mRNA. Initially, MacLachlan and Tekmira turned away from the collaboration. Karikó wanted to team up with Ian MacLachlan because he was the leader of a team that helped advance mRNA technology. Karikó was working on establishing the formulated [lipid nanoparticle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid_lipid_nanoparticle "Solid lipid nanoparticle") delivery system that encapsulates mRNA in a dense particle through a mixing process.

In early 2013, Karikó heard of Moderna's $240 million deal with [AstraZeneca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AstraZeneca "AstraZeneca") to develop a [Vascular endothelial growth factor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vascular_endothelial_growth_factor "Vascular endothelial growth factor") mRNA. Karikó realized that she would not get a chance to apply her experience with mRNA at the University of Pennsylvania, so she took a role as vice president at [BioNTech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BioNTech "BioNTech") RNA Pharmaceuticals (and subsequently became a senior vice president in 2019), while maintaining an adjunct professorship at the university.

During Nobel Week 2023, Karikó remarked in an interview, "I dreamt about doing research, not getting an award," emphasizing her lifelong dedication to discovery over recognition and the central role of perseverance in her scientific path.

In November 2024, Karikó returned to Temple University's Hill College House and delivered a public lecture recounting her Nobel Prize experience including the iconic early morning notification and encouraging students to pursue research with persistence and enthusiasm.

Then in May 2025, Karikó delivered the prestigious Mendel Lecture at the European Society of Human Genetics Annual Meeting, where she discussed clinical applications of mRNA therapeutics and reflected on the collaborative efforts that enabled rapid COVID‑19 vaccine development.

As of October 2023, Karikó is a professor at [University of Szeged](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Szeged "University of Szeged") in Hungary.

## Scientific contributions

Karikó's research and its specializations have a broad impact with potential implications for areas such as the generation of [pluripotent stem cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pluripotent_stem_cells "Pluripotent stem cells"), and messenger RNA-based [gene therapy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gene_therapy "Gene therapy"), as well as "a new class of drugs".

Karikó's work laid the foundation for [BioNTech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BioNTech "BioNTech") and [Moderna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moderna "Moderna") to create therapeutic mRNAs that do not induce an immune response. In 2020, Karikó and Weissman's technology was used in vaccines for [COVID-19](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19 "COVID-19") produced by BioNTech and its partner [Pfizer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pfizer "Pfizer") and by Moderna. The mRNA vaccines were developed and approved for use at unprecedented speed, and demonstrated over 90% efficacy. In addition to vaccines for infectious diseases, mRNA has potential applications in treatment of some cancers, cardiovascular, and metabolic diseases including [ischemia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ischemia "Ischemia").

## Awards and honors

Karikó has received more than 130 international awards and honors for her pioneering and globally significant work in [biochemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biochemistry "Biochemistry").

The [Nobel Assembly at the Karolinska Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Assembly_at_the_Karolinska_Institute "Nobel Assembly at the Karolinska Institute") announced on 2 October 2023, that the [2023 Nobel Prize in Physiology and Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physiology_or_Medicine "Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine") was awarded to Katalin Karikó and [Drew Weissman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drew_Weissman "Drew Weissman") for the development of mRNA technology.

Katalin Karikó donated the more than half a million dollars she received from her Nobel Prize to her former alma mater, the University of Szeged on 16 April 2024.

In 2022, Karikó was awarded VinFuture Grand Prize from the VinFuture Foundation.

In 2022, Karikó was awarded [The Novo Nordisk Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Novo_Nordisk_Prize "The Novo Nordisk Prize") along with [Drew Weissman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drew_Weissman "Drew Weissman") for their pioneering forces for more than a decade in discovering a nucleoside-modified form of mRNA.

In 2023, Karikó was inducted into the [National Inventors Hall of Fame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Inventors_Hall_of_Fame "National Inventors Hall of Fame") for her research into messenger RNA.

Karikó was named in the 2024 Time 100 influential people in health.

In June 2024, Karikó received the Paul Karrer Medal from the University of Zurich.

In December 2024, Katalin Karikó was included on the [BBC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC "BBC")'s *[100 Women](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/100_Women_(BBC) "100 Women (BBC)")* list.

In May 2025, Karikó was elected to the US [National Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Academy_of_Sciences "National Academy of Sciences") in recognition of her pioneering contributions to mRNA vaccine research.

Starting in 2026, she has served as a member of the Advisory Board to Hungary's Minister of Health, [Zsolt Hegedűs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zsolt_Hegedűs "Zsolt Hegedűs"). She was also named to [Forbes Hungary's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forbes "Forbes") *"50 Most Influential Hungarian Women, 2026"* list.

## Personal life

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/38/Susan_Francia_Rower.jpg/250px-Susan_Francia_Rower.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Susan_Francia_Rower.jpg)

Karikó's daughter [Susan Francia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susan_Francia "Susan Francia")

Karikó is married to Béla Francia, and they are the parents of two-time Olympic gold medalist rower [Susan Francia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susan_Francia "Susan Francia"). Their grandson was born in the U.S. in February 2021 to their daughter and son-in-law, architect Ryan Amos.

## Media visibility and memoir

In April 2021, *[The New York Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_York_Times "The New York Times")* featured her career, which laid the groundwork for [mRNA vaccines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RNA_vaccine "RNA vaccine") to fight the [COVID-19 pandemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic "COVID-19 pandemic").

On 10 June 2021, *[The Daily](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Daily_(podcast) "The Daily (podcast)")* podcast from *The New York Times* highlighted Karikó's career, emphasizing the many challenges she had to overcome before her work was recognized.

In November 2021, the US online publication *Glamour* named her a Woman of the Year.

In 2023, two children's books were released about her: *Never Give Up: Dr. Kati Karikó and the Race for the Future of Vaccines*, by Debbie Dadey and Juliana Oakley, and *Kati's Tiny Messengers: Dr. Katalin Karikó and the Battle Against COVID-19*, by Megan Hoyt and Vivien Mildenberger.

Katalin Karikó's autobiography was published by [Crown Publishing Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crown_Publishing_Group "Crown Publishing Group") on 10 October 2023, just days after she won the Nobel Prize. It is titled *Breaking Through: My Life in Science*. The book became the best-selling non-fiction book in Hungary in 2023, and was awarded the Libri Literary Prize in June 2024 and the ASIMOV Prize in May 2025. By this time, her memoir had been translated into 9 languages.
