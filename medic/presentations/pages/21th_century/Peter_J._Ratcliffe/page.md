**Sir Peter John Ratcliffe** (born 14 May 1954) is a British [physician-scientist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physician-scientist "Physician-scientist") who is trained as a [nephrologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nephrologist "Nephrologist"). He was a practising clinician at the [John Radcliffe Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Radcliffe_Hospital "John Radcliffe Hospital"), Oxford and [Nuffield Professor of Clinical Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuffield_Professor_of_Clinical_Medicine "Nuffield Professor of Clinical Medicine") and head of the Nuffield Department of Clinical Medicine at the [University of Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Oxford "University of Oxford") from 2004 to 2016. He has been a Fellow of [Magdalen College, Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magdalen_College,_Oxford "Magdalen College, Oxford") since 2004. In 2016 he became Clinical Research Director at the [Francis Crick Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Crick_Institute "Francis Crick Institute"), retaining a position at Oxford as a Member of the [Ludwig Institute for Cancer Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludwig_Institute_for_Cancer_Research "Ludwig Institute for Cancer Research") and director of the Target Discovery Institute, University of Oxford.

Ratcliffe is best known for his work on cellular reactions to [hypoxia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypoxia_(medical) "Hypoxia (medical)"), for which he shared the 2019 [Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physiology_or_Medicine "Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine") with [William Kaelin Jr.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Kaelin_Jr. "William Kaelin Jr.") and [Gregg L. Semenza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregg_L._Semenza "Gregg L. Semenza").

## Education and training

Ratcliffe was born in Lancashire on 14 May 1954, to William Ratcliffe, a lawyer, and Alice Margaret Ratcliffe, a telephonist. He attended [Lancaster Royal Grammar School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lancaster_Royal_Grammar_School "Lancaster Royal Grammar School") from 1965 to 1972.

He won an open scholarship to [Gonville and Caius College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gonville_and_Caius_College,_Cambridge "Gonville and Caius College, Cambridge") in 1972 to study Medicine at the [University of Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Cambridge "University of Cambridge") and then completed his [MB BChir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_Medicine,_Bachelor_of_Surgery "Bachelor of Medicine, Bachelor of Surgery") medical degree with [distinction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_honors "Latin honors") at [St Bartholomew's Hospital Medical College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Bartholomew's_Hospital_Medical_College "St Bartholomew's Hospital Medical College") in 1978.

Ratcliffe then trained in renal medicine at [Oxford University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford_University "Oxford University"), focusing on renal oxygenation. He earned a higher [MD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Medicine#United_Kingdom,_Ireland_and_some_Commonwealth_countries "Doctor of Medicine") degree from [University of Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Cambridge "University of Cambridge") in 1987.

## Career

In 1990, Ratcliffe received a [Wellcome Trust Senior Fellowship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wellcome_Trust "Wellcome Trust") to study cellular responses to [hypoxia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypoxia_(medical) "Hypoxia (medical)") from low oxygen levels in the blood.
From 1992 to 2004 he was senior research fellow in clinical medicine at [Jesus College, Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesus_College,_Oxford "Jesus College, Oxford").
In 2002, Ratcliffe was accepted into the [Academy of Medical Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academy_of_Medical_Sciences_(United_Kingdom) "Academy of Medical Sciences (United Kingdom)") and was appointed the following year the Nuffield Professor and head of the Nuffield Department of Clinical Medicine at Oxford. In 2012 Ratcliffe joined the [Ludwig Institute for Cancer Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludwig_Institute_for_Cancer_Research "Ludwig Institute for Cancer Research") as a Member, and was later named Ludwig Distinguished Scholar in 2022.

## Research

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/5b/HIF_Nobel_Prize_Physiology_Medicine_2019_Hegasy_ENG.png/250px-HIF_Nobel_Prize_Physiology_Medicine_2019_Hegasy_ENG.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:HIF_Nobel_Prize_Physiology_Medicine_2019_Hegasy_ENG.png)

Illustration of how cells sense and adapt to oxygen availability

In 1989, Ratcliffe established a laboratory in [Oxford University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Oxford "University of Oxford")'s [Nuffield Department of Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuffield_Department_of_Population_Health "Nuffield Department of Population Health") to explore the regulation of [erythropoietin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erythropoietin "Erythropoietin") (EPO), a hormone released by the [kidneys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kidney "Kidney") and responsible for stimulating the production of [red blood cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_blood_cell "Red blood cell"). EPO was known to be produced by the kidneys in response to low oxygen levels, and Ratcliffe's work looked to understand the mechanisms of how the kidneys detected hypoxia (low oxygen levels in the blood) to trigger EPO production. From his studies, Ratcliffe discovered that the [mRNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MRNA "MRNA") from kidneys that were part of the EPO production pathway that were capable of detecting hypoxia was also present in several other organs, both human and animal, including the spleen, brain, and testes. His group found that cells from these organs could switch on EPO production when deprived of oxygen. Further, Ratcliffe was able to modify other cells using the identified mRNA to give these cells oxygen-sensing capabilities.

Building on these discoveries, the Ratcliffe group, along with joint studies with [William Kaelin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Kaelin "William Kaelin") and [Gregg Semenza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregg_Semenza "Gregg Semenza"), helped to uncover a detailed molecular chain of events that cells use to sense oxygen. A specific step identified was the binding of proteins expressed by the [Von Hippel–Lindau tumor suppressor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Von_Hippel–Lindau_tumor_suppressor "Von Hippel–Lindau tumor suppressor") gene (VHL) to [hypoxia-inducible factors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypoxia-inducible_factors "Hypoxia-inducible factors") (HIF), a [transcription factor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transcription_factor "Transcription factor") which trans-activates the EPO gene. Ratcliffe found that the VHL protein can bind a hydroxylated residues of HIF when oxygen is present at acceptable levels; the VHL protein then ubiquitylates the HIF protein which ultimately leads to the HIF protein's destruction. When oxygen levels fall, oxygen-requiring [HIF hydroxylase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HIF_hydroxylase "HIF hydroxylase") enzymes, PHD1, 2 and 3 no longer act and VHL does not bind HIF, allowing HIF to remain and activate the EPO gene. This is a process that takes minutes to complete allowing the body to react quickly to hypoxia.

This same pathway is also switched on in many cancer tumours, allowing them to create new blood vessels to sustain their growth. Much of the current understanding of hypoxia has emerged from the laboratory of Ratcliffe. The understanding of the molecular pathway of EPO production from hypoxia has led to the development of drugs that block VHL from binding with HIF to help treat patients with [anaemia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anaemia "Anaemia") and [kidney failure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kidney_failure "Kidney failure").

## Personal life

Ratcliffe married Fiona Mary MacDougall in 1983.

## Selected honours and awards

Ratcliffe has received a number of awards, accolades, and honours for his seminal work on hypoxia.

- The [Louis-Jeantet Prize for Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis-Jeantet_Prize_for_Medicine "Louis-Jeantet Prize for Medicine") (2009)
- The [Canada Gairdner International Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canada_Gairdner_International_Award "Canada Gairdner International Award") (2010)
- The [Lasker Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lasker_Award "Lasker Award"), along with [William Kaelin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Kaelin "William Kaelin") and [Gregg Semenza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregg_Semenza "Gregg Semenza") (2016)
- [Buchanan Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buchanan_Medal "Buchanan Medal") of the Royal Society (2017)
- [Massry Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massry_Prize "Massry Prize") (2018)
- [Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physiology_or_Medicine "Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine"), along with [William Kaelin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Kaelin "William Kaelin") and [Gregg Semenza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregg_Semenza "Gregg Semenza") (2019), awarded by the Nobel Prize committee "for their discoveries of how cells sense and adapt to oxygen availability."
- Member of the [German Academy of Sciences Leopoldina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Academy_of_Sciences_Leopoldina "German Academy of Sciences Leopoldina") (2020)
- [Sir Hans Krebs Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_Hans_Krebs_Medal "Sir Hans Krebs Medal") (2026), awarded at the 50th FEBS Congress

He was [knighted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knight_Bachelor "Knight Bachelor") in the 2014 New Year Honours for services to clinical medicine.
