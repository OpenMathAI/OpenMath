**Jack William Szostak** [FRS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow_of_the_Royal_Society "Fellow of the Royal Society") (born November 9, 1952) is a Canadian American [biologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biologist "Biologist") of [Polish British](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_British "Polish British") descent, [Nobel Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize "Nobel Prize") laureate, university professor at the [University of Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Chicago "University of Chicago"), former professor of genetics at [Harvard Medical School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_Medical_School "Harvard Medical School"), and Alexander Rich Distinguished Investigator at [Massachusetts General Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massachusetts_General_Hospital "Massachusetts General Hospital"), [Boston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boston "Boston").

Szostak has made significant contributions to the field of [genetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetics "Genetics"). His achievement helped scientists to map the location of [genes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gene "Gene") in mammals and to develop techniques for manipulating genes.

His research findings in this area are also instrumental to the [Human Genome Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_Genome_Project "Human Genome Project"). He was awarded the 2009 [Nobel Prize for Physiology or Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_for_Physiology_or_Medicine "Nobel Prize for Physiology or Medicine"), along with [Elizabeth Blackburn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_Blackburn "Elizabeth Blackburn") and [Carol W. Greider](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carol_W._Greider "Carol W. Greider"), for the discovery of how chromosomes are protected by [telomeres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telomere "Telomere").

## Early life and education

Szostak grew up in [Montreal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montreal "Montreal") and [Ottawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottawa "Ottawa"). Although Szostak does not speak Polish, he stated in an interview with [Wprost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wprost "Wprost") weekly that he remembers his Polish roots. He attended [Riverdale High School (Quebec)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riverdale_High_School_(Quebec) "Riverdale High School (Quebec)") and graduated at the age of 15 with the scholars prize. He graduated with a [B.Sc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_Science "Bachelor of Science") in [cell biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_biology "Cell biology") from [McGill University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McGill_University "McGill University") at the age of 19. In 1970, as an undergraduate, he participated in [The Jackson Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Jackson_Laboratory "The Jackson Laboratory")'s Summer Student Program under the mentorship of Dr. Chen K. Chai. He completed his [PhD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PhD "PhD") in [biochemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biochemistry "Biochemistry") at [Cornell University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornell_University "Cornell University") (advisor Prof. [Ray Wu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ray_Wu "Ray Wu")) before moving to Harvard Medical School to start his own lab at the Sidney Farber Cancer Institute. He credits [Ruth Sager](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruth_Sager "Ruth Sager") for giving him his job there when he had little yet to show. In 1984 Howard Goodman recruited him to Massachusetts General Hospital and the Department of Molecular Biology. He was granted tenure and a full professorship at Harvard Medical School in 1988. In 2022, he moved to the [University of Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Chicago "University of Chicago") as a university professor in the Department of Chemistry and the college.

## Research and career

Szostak is credited with the construction of the world's first [yeast artificial chromosome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yeast_artificial_chromosome "Yeast artificial chromosome"), important in mapping the location of [genes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gene "Gene") in mammals and to develop techniques for manipulating genes.

His discoveries have helped to clarify the events that lead to [chromosomal recombination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_recombination#Chromosomal_crossover "Genetic recombination")—the reshuffling of genes that occurs during [meiosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meiosis "Meiosis")—and the function of [telomeres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telomere "Telomere"), the specialized [DNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA "DNA") sequences at the tips of chromosomes.

In the early 90s his laboratory shifted its research direction and focused on studying RNA enzymes, which had been recently discovered by Cech and Altman. He developed the technique of in vitro evolution of RNA (also developed independently by [Gerald Joyce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerald_Joyce "Gerald Joyce")) which enables the discovery of RNAs with desired functions through successive cycles of selection, amplification and mutation. He isolated the first [aptamer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aptamer "Aptamer") (term he used for the first time). He isolated RNA enzymes with RNA ligase activity directly from random sequence (project of [David Bartel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Bartel "David Bartel")).

His lab focuses on the challenges of understanding the [origin of life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Origin_of_life "Origin of life") on Earth, and the construction of artificial cellular life in the laboratory. They have conducted detailed studies of mechanisms by which RNA templates may have replicated on early Earth before the emergence of enzyme catalysts. In particular, they have focused on [imidazole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imidazole "Imidazole")-activated ribonucleotides ([phosphorimidazolides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phosphorimidazolide "Phosphorimidazolide")) as monomers capable of elongating a new RNA strand. Significantly, the Szostak group discovered that phosphorimidazolide-mediated template elongation occurs via 5'-5'-imidazolium bridged dinucleotide intermediates which accelerate polymerization. Phosphorimidazolides were first proposed to be critical for early-Earth nucleotide polymerization by [Leslie E. Orgel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leslie_Orgel "Leslie Orgel") and colleagues.
Szostak and [Katarzyna Adamala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kate_Adamala "Kate Adamala") demonstrated that the issues of a degrading effect of magnesium ions on RNA and the disruption of a fatty acid membrane by magnesium ions can be simultaneously solved by the presence of weak cation chelator like citric acid in primitive protocells.

Szostak developed [functional information](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functional_information "Functional information"), a concept for quantifying the information content of biological molecules, such as DNA or RNA, based on their function.

In September 2022, Szostak joined the faculty of the University of Chicago as university professor, leading a new interdisciplinary program called the Origins of Life Initiative.

Szostak is also an important advocate for caution regarding the potential development of [mirror life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mirror_life "Mirror life"), having published a commentary in *[Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_(journal) "Science (journal)")* in December 2024.

### Awards and honours

Szostak has received several awards and honours for his contributions. He is a member of the [National Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Academy_of_Sciences "National Academy of Sciences"), [American Academy of Arts and Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Academy_of_Arts_and_Sciences "American Academy of Arts and Sciences") and [New York Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_Academy_of_Sciences "New York Academy of Sciences"), the [American Philosophical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Philosophical_Society "American Philosophical Society"), and is a member of the [Kosciuszko Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kosciuszko_Foundation "Kosciuszko Foundation") Collegium of Eminent Scientists of Polish Origin and Ancestry.

He has received the following awards:

- [United States National Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_National_Academy_of_Sciences "United States National Academy of Sciences") Award in Molecular Biology in 1994
- [Hans Sigrist Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Sigrist_Prize "Hans Sigrist Prize"), University of Bern, Switzerland, in 1997
- [Genetics Society of America Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetics_Society_of_America_Medal "Genetics Society of America Medal") in 2000
- The 2006  [Lasker Award for Basic Medical Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lasker_Award "Lasker Award")
- The 2008 [Dr H.P. Heineken Prize for Biochemistry and Biophysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dr_H.P._Heineken_Prize_for_Biochemistry_and_Biophysics "Dr H.P. Heineken Prize for Biochemistry and Biophysics"), [Royal Netherlands Academy of Arts and Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Netherlands_Academy_of_Arts_and_Sciences "Royal Netherlands Academy of Arts and Sciences")
- The 2009 [Nobel Prize for Physiology or Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_for_Physiology_or_Medicine "Nobel Prize for Physiology or Medicine") (shared with [Elizabeth Blackburn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_Blackburn "Elizabeth Blackburn") and [Carol W. Greider](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carol_W._Greider "Carol W. Greider"))
- The 2011 [Oparin Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oparin_Medal "Oparin Medal")

> An organism's genes are stored within DNA molecules, which are found in chromosomes inside its cells' nuclei. When a cell divides, it is important that its chromosomes are copied in full, and that they are not damaged. At each end of a chromosome lies a "cap" or telomere, as it is known, which protects it. After Elizabeth Blackburn discovered that telomeres have a particular DNA, through experiments conducted on ciliates and yeast, she and Jack Szostak proved in 1982 that the telomeres' DNA prevents chromosomes from being broken down,

according to the statement released by the Alfred Nobel Foundation.
