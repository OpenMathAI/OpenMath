**Sir Godfrey Newbold Hounsfield** ([/ˈhaʊnzfiːld/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English") [*HOWNZ-feeld*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:Pronunciation_respelling_key "Help:Pronunciation respelling key"); 28 August 1919 – 12 August 2004) was a British [electrical engineer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_engineer "Electrical engineer") who shared the 1979 [Nobel Prize for Physiology or Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_for_Physiology_or_Medicine "Nobel Prize for Physiology or Medicine") with [Allan MacLeod Cormack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allan_MacLeod_Cormack "Allan MacLeod Cormack") for his part in developing the diagnostic technique of [X-ray computed tomography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray_computed_tomography "X-ray computed tomography") (CT).

His name is immortalised in the [Hounsfield scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hounsfield_scale "Hounsfield scale"), a quantitative measure of [radiodensity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiodensity "Radiodensity") used in evaluating CT scans. The scale is defined in Hounsfield units (symbol **HU**), running from air at −1000 HU, through water at 0 HU, and up to dense cortical bone at +1000 HU and more.

## Early life

Hounsfield was born in [Sutton-on-Trent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sutton-on-Trent "Sutton-on-Trent"), [Nottinghamshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nottinghamshire "Nottinghamshire"), England on 28 August 1919. He was the youngest of five children (he had two brothers and two sisters). His father, Thomas Hounsfield, was a farmer from Beighton, and was linked to the prominent Hounsfield and Newbold families of [Hackenthorpe Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hackenthorpe_Hall "Hackenthorpe Hall"), his mother was Blanche Dilcock.
As a child he was fascinated by the electrical gadgets and machinery found all over his parents' farm. Between the ages of eleven and eighteen, he tinkered with his own electrical recording machines, launched himself off haystacks with his own home-made [glider](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glider_aircraft "Glider aircraft"), and almost killed himself by using water-filled tar barrels and acetylene to see how high they could be waterjet propelled. He attended the [Magnus Grammar School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnus_Church_of_England_Academy "Magnus Church of England Academy") in [Newark-on-Trent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newark-on-Trent "Newark-on-Trent"), but was not academic.

## Military service and education

Shortly before [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II"), he joined the [Royal Air Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Air_Force "Royal Air Force") as a volunteer reservist, where he learned the basics of [electronics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronics "Electronics") and [radar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radar "Radar"). After the war, he attended [Faraday House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faraday_House "Faraday House") Electrical Engineering College in London, graduating with the DFH (Diploma of Faraday House). Before the advent of most university engineering departments, Faraday House was a specialist Electrical Engineering college that provided university level education that combined practical experience with theoretical study.

## Career

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7f/CT_Scan_General_Illustration_Upper_Abdomen.jpg/250px-CT_Scan_General_Illustration_Upper_Abdomen.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:CT_Scan_General_Illustration_Upper_Abdomen.jpg)

One frame of a modern CT scan of the abdomen

In 1949, Hounsfield began work at [EMI, Ltd.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EMI "EMI") in Hayes, Middlesex, where he researched guided weapon systems and radar. (Hounsfield incorrectly gave this date as 1951 when he wrote his autobiography which is available on the Nobel Prize website; the correct date is 10 October 1949 as stated in a biography of Hounsfield.) At EMI, he became interested in [computers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer "Computer") and in 1958, he helped design the first commercially available all-[transistor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transistor "Transistor") computer made in Great Britain: the [EMIDEC 1100](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EMIDEC_1100 "EMIDEC 1100"). Shortly afterwards, he began work on the CT scanner at EMI. He continued to improve CT scanning, introducing a whole-body scanner in 1975, and was senior researcher (and after his retirement in 1984, consultant) to the laboratories.

While on an outing in the country, Hounsfield came up with the idea that one could determine what was inside a box by taking X-ray readings at all angles around the object. He then set to work constructing a computer that could take input from X-rays at various angles to create an image of the object in "slices". Applying this idea to the medical field led him to propose what is now known as computed tomography. At the time, Hounsfield was not aware of the work that Cormack had done on the theoretical mathematics for such a device. Hounsfield built a prototype head scanner and tested it first on a preserved [human brain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_brain "Human brain"), then on a fresh cow [brain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brain "Brain") from a butcher’s shop, and later on himself. On 1 October 1971, CT scanning was introduced into [medical practice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medicine "Medicine") with a successful scan on a cerebral [cyst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyst "Cyst") patient at [Atkinson Morley Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atkinson_Morley_Hospital "Atkinson Morley Hospital") in [Wimbledon, London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wimbledon,_London "Wimbledon, London"), [United Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Kingdom "United Kingdom"). In 1975, Hounsfield built a whole-body scanner. The principles of computed tomography developed by Hounsfield remain in use today.

## Awards and honours

In 1979, Hounsfield and Cormack received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.

Hounsfield received numerous awards in addition to the Nobel Prize. He was appointed [Commander of the Order of the British Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_the_British_Empire "Order of the British Empire") in 1976 and [knighted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knight "Knight") in the [1981 Birthday Honours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1981_Birthday_Honours "1981 Birthday Honours").

In 1974, he received the [Wilhelm Exner Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilhelm_Exner_Medal "Wilhelm Exner Medal"). Hounsfield was elected a [Fellow of the Royal Society (FRS) in 1975](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Fellows_of_the_Royal_Society_elected_in_1975 "List of Fellows of the Royal Society elected in 1975"). In 1976, he received the Golden Plate Award of the [American Academy of Achievement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academy_of_Achievement "Academy of Achievement"). He was awarded the [Howard N. Potts Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Franklin_Institute_Awards "The Franklin Institute Awards") in 1977. In 1994 he was elected an Honorary Fellow of the [Royal Academy of Engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Academy_of_Engineering "Royal Academy of Engineering").

The Hounsfield Facility for 3-D CT imaging at the University of Nottingham, opened in 2014, was named after him. It was designed to apply CT scanning to biomaterials, especially within soil, and thus to the exploring the environment.

## Personal life and death

Hounsfield enjoyed hiking and skiing. He had resolved to develop what came to be CT scanning while on a country ramble.

He retired from EMI in 1986 and used the prize money from his Nobel to build a personal laboratory in his home. Hounsfield died at [Kingston upon Thames](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingston_upon_Thames "Kingston upon Thames"), [Greater London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_London "Greater London"), in 2004, at the age of 84.
