**Herbert Spencer Gasser** (July 5, 1888 – May 11, 1963) was an American [physiologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physiologist "Physiologist"), and recipient of the [Nobel Prize for Physiology or Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_for_Physiology_or_Medicine "Nobel Prize for Physiology or Medicine") in 1944 for his work with [action potentials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_potential "Action potential") in [nerve fibers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nerve_fiber "Nerve fiber") while on the faculty of [Washington University in St. Louis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington_University_in_St._Louis "Washington University in St. Louis"), awarded jointly with [Joseph Erlanger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Erlanger "Joseph Erlanger").

## Education

Gasser was born in [Platteville, Wisconsin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platteville,_Wisconsin "Platteville, Wisconsin"), to Herman Gasser and Jane Elisabeth Griswold Gasser. His father was a physician from Dornbirn in the Austrian province of [Vorarlberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vorarlberg "Vorarlberg"); his mother was of [New England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_England "New England") Yankee and [German Russian ancestry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germans_from_Russia "Germans from Russia").

## Biography

Gasser attended State Normal School in Platteville, then entered the [University of Wisconsin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Wisconsin "University of Wisconsin") in 1907. Finishing his undergraduate studies in zoology in only two years, he enrolled in the university's medical school in 1909, studying physiology under [Joseph Erlanger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Erlanger "Joseph Erlanger"), and pharmacology under Arthur S. Loevenhart. While still a student, he was named an instructor in pharmacology (1911). Since UW only provided preclinical medical instruction, Gasser transferred to [Johns Hopkins School of Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johns_Hopkins_School_of_Medicine "Johns Hopkins School of Medicine") in 1913, where he received his medical degree in 1915. He then returned to UW as a pharmacology instructor.
In 1916 Gasser moved to the department of physiology at [Washington University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington_University_in_St._Louis "Washington University in St. Louis").

As the United States became involved in [World War I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I "World War I") and the armies began using chemical warfare, Gasser was urged to contribute his knowledge of human physiology to the subject. Accordingly, in the summer of 1918 he joined the Armed Forces Chemical Warfare Service in Washington D.C. After the [Armistice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armistice_of_11_November_1918 "Armistice of 11 November 1918") he returned to [Washington University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington_University_School_of_Medicine "Washington University School of Medicine"), where he was made a professor of [pharmacology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pharmacology "Pharmacology") in 1921.

During the years 1923–1925 Gasser studied in London, Paris and Munich under a [Rockefeller Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rockefeller_Foundation "Rockefeller Foundation") grant, with the goal of improving the caliber of US medical education. After completing these studies he returned to Washington University.

In 1931 Gasser moved to New York City and became a professor of physiology at [Cornell Medical College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weill_Cornell_Medical_College "Weill Cornell Medical College"). After four years at that post, he was named the second director of the [Rockefeller Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rockefeller_University "Rockefeller University"), following the long tenure of [Simon Flexner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simon_Flexner "Simon Flexner"), who had founded the institute. He remained in that position until 1953. During his tenure there, he was elected a member of the United States [National Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Academy_of_Sciences "National Academy of Sciences"), the [American Philosophical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Philosophical_Society "American Philosophical Society"), and the [American Academy of Arts and Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Academy_of_Arts_and_Sciences "American Academy of Arts and Sciences").

In 1936 Gasser and Erlanger gave a series of lectures at the [University of Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Pennsylvania "University of Pennsylvania"), summarizing their investigations into the actions of human [nerve cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neuron "Neuron"). This work led to their recognition in 1944, when they jointly received the Nobel Prize (Gasser used his prize money to fund further research into the subject).

After his retirement from the Rockefeller Institute in 1953, Gasser continued his research. He published over 100 scientific papers during his lifetime. He died in New York City on May 11, 1963.
