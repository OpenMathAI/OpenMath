**Renato Dulbecco** ([/dʌlˈbɛkoʊ/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English") [*dul-BEK-oh*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:Pronunciation_respelling_key "Help:Pronunciation respelling key"), Italian: [[reˈnaːto dulˈbɛkko, -ˈbek-]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Italian "Help:IPA/Italian"); February 22, 1914 – February 19, 2012) was an Italian–American [virologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virologist "Virologist") who won the 1975 [Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physiology_or_Medicine "Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine") for his work on [oncoviruses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oncovirus "Oncovirus"), which are viruses that can cause cancer when they infect animal cells. He studied at the [University of Turin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Turin "University of Turin") under [Giuseppe Levi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giuseppe_Levi "Giuseppe Levi"), along with fellow students [Salvador Luria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salvador_Luria "Salvador Luria") and [Rita Levi-Montalcini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rita_Levi-Montalcini "Rita Levi-Montalcini"), who also moved to the U.S. with him and won Nobel prizes. He was drafted into the Italian army in World War II, but later joined the resistance.

## Early life

Dulbecco was born in [Catanzaro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catanzaro "Catanzaro") ([Calabria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calabria "Calabria"), Southern [Italy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Italy_(1861–1946) "Kingdom of Italy (1861–1946)")), but spent his childhood and grew up in [Liguria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liguria "Liguria"), in the coastal city [Imperia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperia "Imperia"). He graduated from high school at 16, then moved to the [University of Turin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Turin "University of Turin"). Despite a strong interest in [mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics "Mathematics") and [physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics "Physics"), he decided to study [medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medicine "Medicine"). At only 22, he graduated in morbid [anatomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatomy "Anatomy") and [pathology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pathology "Pathology") under the supervision of professor [Giuseppe Levi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giuseppe_Levi "Giuseppe Levi"). During these years he met [Salvador Luria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salvador_Luria "Salvador Luria") and [Rita Levi-Montalcini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rita_Levi-Montalcini "Rita Levi-Montalcini"), whose friendship and encouragement would later bring him to the [United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States "United States"). In 1936 he was called up for military service as a medical officer, and later (1938) discharged. In 1940 [Italy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italy "Italy") entered [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II") and Dulbecco was recalled and sent to the front in [France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/France "France") and [Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russia "Russia"), where he was wounded. After hospitalization and the collapse of [Fascism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fascism "Fascism"), he joined the [resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resistance_during_World_War_II "Resistance during World War II") against the [German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germany "Germany") [occupation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_occupation "Military occupation").

## Career and research

After the war he resumed his work at Levi's [laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laboratory "Laboratory"), but soon he moved, together with Levi-Montalcini, to the U.S., where, at [Indiana University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indiana_University "Indiana University"), he worked with [Salvador Luria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salvador_Luria "Salvador Luria") on [bacteriophages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bacteriophage "Bacteriophage"). In the summer of 1949 he moved to [Caltech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California_Institute_of_Technology "California Institute of Technology"), joining [Max Delbrück](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Delbrück "Max Delbrück")'s group (see [Phage group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phage_group "Phage group")). In the early 1950s, on Delbruck's advice, Dulbecco visited the major centers of [animal virus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animal_virus "Animal virus") work in the US in order to discover a way to quantitatively assay animal viruses by a plaque technique, similar to the technique that had recently been developed for [bacterial viruses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bacteriophage "Bacteriophage"). Within less than a year, he worked out such a method for [Western equine encephalitis virus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_equine_encephalitis_virus "Western equine encephalitis virus"), which then opened up animal virology to quantitative work. The technique was then used by Dulbecco and Vogt to study the biological properties of [poliovirus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poliovirus "Poliovirus"). These accomplishments led to Dulbecco's appointment first to associate professor, and then to full professor at the [California Institute of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California_Institute_of_Technology "California Institute of Technology"). There he started his studies about [animal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animal "Animal") [oncoviruses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oncovirus "Oncovirus"), especially of [polyoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyoma "Polyoma") family. In the late 1950s, he took [Howard Temin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Howard_Temin "Howard Temin") as a student, with whom, and together with [David Baltimore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Baltimore "David Baltimore"), he would later share the 1975 [Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physiology_or_Medicine "Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine") for "their discoveries concerning the interaction between [tumour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tumour "Tumour") [viruses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virus "Virus") and the [genetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetics "Genetics") material of the [cell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_(biology) "Cell (biology)")." Temin and Baltimore arrived at the discovery of reverse transcriptase simultaneously and independently from each other; although Dulbecco did not take direct part in either of their experiments, he had taught the two methods they used to make the discovery.

Throughout this time he also worked with [Marguerite Vogt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marguerite_Vogt "Marguerite Vogt"). In 1962, he moved to the [Salk Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salk_Institute "Salk Institute") and then in 1972 to The Imperial Cancer Research Fund (now named the Cancer Research UK [London Research Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Research_Institute "London Research Institute")) where he was first appointed associate professor and then full professor. Like many Italian scientists, Dulbecco did not have a PhD because it did not exist in the Italian higher education system (until it was introduced in 1980). In 1986 he was among the [scientists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientist "Scientist") who launched the [Human Genome Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_Genome_Project "Human Genome Project"). From 1993 to 1997 he moved back to Italy, where he was president of the Institute of Biomedical Technologies at C.N.R. ([National Council of Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Research_Council_(Italy) "National Research Council (Italy)")) in [Milan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milan "Milan"). He also retained his position on the faculty of [Salk Institute for Biological Studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salk_Institute_for_Biological_Studies "Salk Institute for Biological Studies"). Dulbecco was actively involved in research into identification and characterization of mammary gland cancer stem cells until December 2011. His research using a stem cell model system suggested that a single malignant cell with stem cell properties may be sufficient to induce cancer in mice and can generate distinct populations of tumor-initiating cells also with cancer stem cell properties. Dulbecco's examinations into the origin of mammary gland cancer stem cells in solid tumors was a continuation of his early investigations of cancer being a disease of acquired mutations. His interest in cancer stem cells was strongly influenced by evidence that in addition to genomic mutations, epigenetic modification of a cell may contribute to the development or progression of cancer.

### Nobel Prize

Dulbecco and his group demonstrated that the infection of normal cells with certain types of viruses (oncoviruses) led to the incorporation of virus-derived [genes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gene "Gene") into the host-cell [genome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genome "Genome"), and that this event lead to the transformation (the acquisition of a tumor [phenotype](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phenotype "Phenotype")) of those cells. As demonstrated by Temin and Baltimore, who shared the [Nobel Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physiology_or_Medicine "Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine") with Dulbecco, the transfer of viral genes to the cell is mediated by an [enzyme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enzyme "Enzyme") called [reverse transcriptase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reverse_transcriptase "Reverse transcriptase") (or, more precisely, RNA-dependent [DNA polymerase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA_polymerase "DNA polymerase")), which replicates the viral genome (in this case made of [RNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RNA "RNA")) into [DNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA "DNA"), which is later incorporated in the host genome.

Oncoviruses are the cause of some forms of human [cancers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cancer "Cancer"). Dulbecco's study gave a basis for a precise understanding of the [molecular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecule "Molecule") mechanisms by which they propagate, thus allowing humans to better fight them. Furthermore, the mechanisms of [carcinogenesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carcinogenesis "Carcinogenesis") mediated by oncoviruses closely resemble the process by which normal cells degenerate into cancer cells. Dulbecco's discoveries allowed humans to better understand and fight cancer. In addition, it is well known that in the 1980s and 1990s, an understanding of reverse transcriptase and of the origins, nature, and properties of [human immunodeficiency virus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_immunodeficiency_virus "Human immunodeficiency virus") (HIV, of which there are two well-understood serotypes, HIV-1, and the less-common and less virulent HIV-2), the virus which, if unchecked, ultimately causes [acquired immunodeficiency syndrome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acquired_immunodeficiency_syndrome "Acquired immunodeficiency syndrome") (AIDS), led to the development of the first group of drugs that could be considered successful against the virus, the [reverse-transcriptase inhibitors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reverse-transcriptase_inhibitors "Reverse-transcriptase inhibitors"), of which [zidovudine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zidovudine "Zidovudine") is a well-known example. These drugs are still used today as one part of the [highly-active antiretroviral therapy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Highly-active_antiretroviral_therapy "Highly-active antiretroviral therapy") drug cocktail that is in contemporary use.

### Other awards

In 1965 he received the [Marjory Stephenson Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marjory_Stephenson_Prize "Marjory Stephenson Prize") from the [Society for General Microbiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Society_for_General_Microbiology "Society for General Microbiology"). That same year, he was elected to the [American Academy of Arts and Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Academy_of_Arts_and_Sciences "American Academy of Arts and Sciences"). In 1973 he was awarded the [Louisa Gross Horwitz Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louisa_Gross_Horwitz_Prize "Louisa Gross Horwitz Prize") from [Columbia University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia_University "Columbia University") together with [Theodore Puck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodore_Puck "Theodore Puck") and [Harry Eagle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harry_Eagle "Harry Eagle"). Dulbecco was the recipient of the [Selman A. Waksman Award in Microbiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selman_A._Waksman_Award_in_Microbiology "Selman A. Waksman Award in Microbiology") from the [National Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_National_Academy_of_Sciences "United States National Academy of Sciences") in 1974. He was elected a [Foreign Member of the Royal Society (ForMemRS) in 1974](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Fellows_of_the_Royal_Society_elected_in_1974 "List of Fellows of the Royal Society elected in 1974"). In 1993, he was elected to the [American Philosophical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Philosophical_Society "American Philosophical Society").
