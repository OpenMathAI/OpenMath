**Marshall Warren Nirenberg** (April 10, 1927 – January 15, 2010) was an American [biochemist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biochemist "Biochemist") and [geneticist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geneticist "Geneticist"). He shared a [Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physiology_or_Medicine "Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine") in 1968 with [Har Gobind Khorana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Har_Gobind_Khorana "Har Gobind Khorana") and [Robert W. Holley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_W._Holley "Robert W. Holley") for "breaking the [genetic code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_code "Genetic code")" and describing how it operates in [protein synthesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protein_biosynthesis "Protein biosynthesis"). In the same year, together with [Har Gobind Khorana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Har_Gobind_Khorana "Har Gobind Khorana"), he was awarded the [Louisa Gross Horwitz Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louisa_Gross_Horwitz_Prize "Louisa Gross Horwitz Prize") from [Columbia University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia_University "Columbia University").

## Biography

Nirenberg was born in New York City to a [Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish "Jewish") family, the son of Minerva (Bykowsky) and Harry Edward Nirenberg, a shirtmaker. He developed [rheumatic fever](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rheumatic_fever "Rheumatic fever") as a boy, so the family moved to [Orlando](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orlando,_Florida "Orlando, Florida"), Florida to take advantage of the [subtropical climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subtropical_climate "Subtropical climate"). He developed an early interest in [biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biology "Biology"). In 1948 he received his [BS degree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BS_degree "BS degree"), and in 1952, a [master's degree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master's_degree "Master's degree") in [zoology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoology "Zoology") from the [University of Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Florida "University of Florida") at Gainesville where he was also a member of the [Pi Lambda Phi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pi_Lambda_Phi "Pi Lambda Phi") [Fraternity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fraternity "Fraternity"). His [dissertation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissertation "Dissertation") for the [Master's thesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master's_thesis "Master's thesis") was an [ecological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecological "Ecological") and [taxonomic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taxonomy_(biology) "Taxonomy (biology)") study of [caddis flies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caddis_flies "Caddis flies") ([Trichoptera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trichoptera "Trichoptera")). He received his [PhD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PhD "PhD") in [biochemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biochemistry "Biochemistry") from the [University of Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Michigan "University of Michigan"), [Ann Arbor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ann_Arbor "Ann Arbor") in 1957, studying hexose uptake in tumor cells with his advisor James F. Hogg.

He began his [postdoctoral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postdoctoral "Postdoctoral") work at the [National Institutes of Health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Institutes_of_Health "National Institutes of Health") (NIH) in 1957 as a fellow of the [American Cancer Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Cancer_Society "American Cancer Society") in what was then called the [National Institute of Arthritis and Metabolic Diseases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Institute_of_Arthritis_and_Musculoskeletal_and_Skin_Diseases#Legislative_chronology "National Institute of Arthritis and Musculoskeletal and Skin Diseases"). In 1959 he became a research biochemist at the [NIH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NIH "NIH") and began to study the steps that relate [DNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA "DNA"), [RNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RNA "RNA") and [protein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protein "Protein"). Nirenberg's groundbreaking experiments advanced him to become the head of the Section of Biochemical Genetics in 1962 in the [National Heart Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Heart_Institute "National Heart Institute") (now the [National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Heart,_Lung,_and_Blood_Institute "National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute")), where he remained a laboratory chief until his death. Fellow laboratory chiefs included [Ernst Freese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernst_Freese "Ernst Freese") and [Daniel Carleton Gajdusek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Carleton_Gajdusek "Daniel Carleton Gajdusek"). He was married in 1961 to [Perola Zaltzman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perola_Zaltzman?action=edit&redlink=1 "Perola Zaltzman"), a [chemist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemist "Chemist") from the [University of Brazil, Rio de Janeiro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Brazil,_Rio_de_Janeiro "University of Brazil, Rio de Janeiro"), who also worked at [NIH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NIH "NIH") and died in 2001. Nirenberg married [Myrna Weissman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myrna_Weissman "Myrna Weissman"), [PhD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PhD "PhD"), Professor of [Epidemiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epidemiology "Epidemiology") and [Psychiatry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychiatry "Psychiatry") at [Columbia University College of Physicians and Surgeons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia_University_College_of_Physicians_and_Surgeons "Columbia University College of Physicians and Surgeons") in 2005. He had four stepchildren: Susan Weissman of [Evanston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evanston,_Illinois "Evanston, Illinois"), Illinois, Judith Weissman of [New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York,_New_York "New York, New York"), New York, Sharon Weissman of [New Haven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Haven,_Connecticut "New Haven, Connecticut"), Connecticut, and Jonathan Weissman of [San Francisco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Francisco,_California "San Francisco, California"), California. He was also survived by his sister, Joan Nirenberg Geiger of Dallas, Texas, several nieces and a nephew.

Nirenberg was awarded the [National Medal of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Medal_of_Science "National Medal of Science") in 1964 and the National Medal of Honor in 1968 by [President](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_of_the_United_States "President of the United States") [Lyndon B. Johnson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyndon_B._Johnson "Lyndon B. Johnson"). In 1981, Nirenberg became a founding member of the [World Cultural Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Cultural_Council "World Cultural Council"). In 1986, Nirenberg's achievements and contributions to the field of biochemistry genetics was recognized at an event honoring [Maimonides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maimonides "Maimonides") and [Menachem M. Schneerson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Menachem_M._Schneerson "Menachem M. Schneerson"), in the nation's capital, hosted by [Bob Dole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bob_Dole "Bob Dole") and [Joe Biden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joe_Biden "Joe Biden"). He was elected to the [American Philosophical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Philosophical_Society "American Philosophical Society") in 2001. He died on January 15, 2010, from [cancer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cancer "Cancer") after several months of illness.

## Research

By 1958, experiments and analysis such as the [Avery–MacLeod–McCarty experiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avery–MacLeod–McCarty_experiment "Avery–MacLeod–McCarty experiment"), the [Hershey–Chase experiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hershey–Chase_experiment "Hershey–Chase experiment"), the [Watson–Crick structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_Structure_of_Nucleic_Acids:_A_Structure_for_Deoxyribose_Nucleic_Acid "Molecular Structure of Nucleic Acids: A Structure for Deoxyribose Nucleic Acid") and the [Meselson–Stahl experiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meselson–Stahl_experiment "Meselson–Stahl experiment") had shown [DNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA "DNA") to be the molecule of genetic information. It was not known, however, how DNA directed the expression of [proteins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protein "Protein"), or what role [RNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RNA "RNA") had in these processes. Nirenberg teamed up with [Heinrich J. Matthaei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heinrich_J._Matthaei "Heinrich J. Matthaei") at the [National Institutes of Health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Institutes_of_Health "National Institutes of Health") to answer these questions. They produced [RNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RNA "RNA") composed solely of [uracil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uracil "Uracil"), a [nucleotide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nucleotide "Nucleotide") that only occurs in RNA. They then added this synthetic poly-uracil RNA into a cell-free extract of *[Escherichia coli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Escherichia_coli "Escherichia coli")* which contained the DNA, RNA, [ribosomes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ribosome "Ribosome") and other cellular machinery for protein synthesis. They added [DNase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNase "DNase"), which breaks apart the DNA, so that no additional proteins would be produced other than that from their synthetic [RNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RNA "RNA"). They then added 1 radioactively labeled [amino acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amino_acid "Amino acid"), the building blocks of proteins, and 19 unlabeled amino acids to the extract, varying the labeled amino acid in each sample. Only in the extract containing the radioactively labeled [phenylalanine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phenylalanine "Phenylalanine"), was the resulting protein also [radioactive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radioactive "Radioactive"). This implied that the [genetic code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_code "Genetic code") for phenylalanine on RNA consisted of a repetition of uracil bases. Indeed, as we know now, it is UUU (three uracil bases in a row). This was the first step in deciphering the [codons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Codon "Codon") of the genetic code and the first demonstration of [messenger RNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Messenger_RNA "Messenger RNA") (see *[Nirenberg and Matthaei experiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nirenberg_and_Matthaei_experiment "Nirenberg and Matthaei experiment")*).

In August 1961, at the International Congress of Biochemistry in Moscow, Nirenberg presented a paper to a small group of scientists, reporting the decoding of the first codon of the genetic code. [Matthew Meselson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matthew_Meselson "Matthew Meselson"), who was in the audience, spontaneously hugged Nirenberg at the end of the talk and then told [Francis Crick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Crick "Francis Crick") about Nirenberg's result. Crick invited Nirenberg to repeat his performance the next day in a talk to a much larger audience. Speaking before the assembled congress of more than a thousand people, Nirenberg electrified the scientific community. He quickly received great scientific attention for these experiments. Within a few years, his research team had performed similar experiments and found that three-base repeats of [adenosine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adenosine "Adenosine") (AAA) produced the amino acid [lysine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lysine "Lysine"), and [cytosine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cytosine "Cytosine") repeats (CCC) produced [proline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proline "Proline"). The next breakthrough came when [Philip Leder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_Leder "Philip Leder"), a postdoctoral researcher in Nirenberg's lab, developed a method for determining the genetic code on pieces of [tRNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TRNA "TRNA") (see *[Nirenberg and Leder experiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nirenberg_and_Leder_experiment "Nirenberg and Leder experiment")*). This greatly sped up the assignment of three-base codons to amino acids so that 50 codons were identified in this way. Khorana's experiments confirmed these results and completed the genetic code translation.

The period between 1961 and 1962 is often referred to as the "coding race" because of the competition between the labs of Nirenberg at NIH and Nobel laureate [Severo Ochoa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Severo_Ochoa "Severo Ochoa") at New York University Medical School, who had a massive staff. Faced with the possibility of helping the first NIH scientist win a Nobel prize, many NIH scientists put aside their own work to help Nirenberg in deciphering the mRNA codons for amino acids. Dr. [DeWitt Stetten, Jr.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DeWitt_Stetten,_Jr. "DeWitt Stetten, Jr."), director of the National Institute of Arthritis and Metabolic Diseases, called this period of collaboration "NIH's finest hour".

Nirenberg's later research focused on [neuroscience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neuroscience "Neuroscience"), neural development, and the [homeobox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeobox "Homeobox") genes.
