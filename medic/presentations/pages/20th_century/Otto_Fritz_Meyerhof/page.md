**Otto Fritz Meyerhof** (German pronunciation: [[ˈɔto ˈmaɪɐhoːf]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Standard_German "Help:IPA/Standard German") [ⓘ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:De-Otto_Meyerhof.ogg "File:De-Otto Meyerhof.ogg"); 12 April 1884 – 6 October 1951) was a German [physician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physician "Physician") and [biochemist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biochemist "Biochemist") who won the 1922 [Nobel Prize in Physiology and Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physiology_and_Medicine "Nobel Prize in Physiology and Medicine").

## Biography

Otto Fritz Meyerhof was born in [Hannover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hannover "Hannover"), at Theaterplatz 16A (now:Rathenaustrasse 16A), the son of wealthy Jewish parents. In 1888, his family moved to Berlin, where Otto spent most of his childhood, and where he started his study of medicine. He continued these studies in [Strasbourg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Strasbourg "University of Strasbourg") and [Heidelberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Heidelberg "University of Heidelberg"), from which he graduated in 1909, with a work titled "Contributions to the Psychological Theory of Mental Illness".

In Heidelberg, he met Hedwig Schallenberg. They married in 1914 and had three children together: a daughter, Bettina, and two sons, Gottfried (who after emigration used the anglicized name Geoffrey) and Walter.

In 1912, Otto Meyerhof moved to the [University of Kiel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Kiel "University of Kiel"), where he received a professorship in 1918. In 1922, he was awarded the [Nobel Prize in Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Medicine "Nobel Prize in Medicine"), with [Archibald Vivian Hill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archibald_Vivian_Hill "Archibald Vivian Hill"), for his work on muscle metabolism, including [glycolysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glycolysis "Glycolysis"). In 1929, he became one of the directors of the [Kaiser Wilhelm Institute for Medical Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Planck_Institute_for_Medical_Research "Max Planck Institute for Medical Research") in Heidelberg, a position he held until 1938, when Jews were expelled from university teaching positions.

To escape the increasing oppression of Jews by the [Nazi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazism "Nazism") regime, in 1938 Meyerhof emigrated with his family to Paris. After the fall of France in 1940, they fled to Marseille. Aided by the [Emergency Rescue Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emergency_Rescue_Committee "Emergency Rescue Committee"), they left the country by ship to the United States that year. Meyerhof was appointed to a guest professorship at the [University of Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Pennsylvania "University of Pennsylvania") in Philadelphia.

Meyerhof died in [Philadelphia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philadelphia "Philadelphia") at the age of 67. In addition to receiving the Nobel Prize, he was recognized for his contributions to the study of glycolysis, by the naming of the common series of reactions for the pathway in [Eukaryotes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eukaryotes "Eukaryotes") as the *Embden–Meyerhof–Parnas Pathway*.
