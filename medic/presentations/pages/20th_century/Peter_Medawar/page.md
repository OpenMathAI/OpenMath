**Sir Peter Brian Medawar** ([/ˈmɛdəwər/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English"); 28 February 1915 – 2 October 1987) was a Brazilian-born biologist and writer of Lebanese-British descent who, from a succession of research and teaching posts ([Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford_University "Oxford University") through the [Royal Institution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Institution "Royal Institution")), and senior U.K. biomedical leadership positions ([MRC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_Research_Council_(United_Kingdom) "Medical Research Council (United Kingdom)") and [RPMC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Postgraduate_Medical_School "Royal Postgraduate Medical School")), contributed seminal discoveries in [immunology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immunology "Immunology"), including one honoured by a [Nobel Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physiology_or_Medicine "Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine") to him and Australian [Mac Burnet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Macfarlane_Burnet "Frank Macfarlane Burnet") in 1960. Medawar's works on the discovery of [acquired immune tolerance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acquired_immune_tolerance "Acquired immune tolerance"), and on [graft rejection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graft_rejection "Graft rejection"), have been fundamental to the medical practice of tissue and [organ transplants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organ_transplants "Organ transplants"). For his scientific work, he has been termed the "father of transplantation". He is remembered, as well, for his wit, both in person and in his popular writings. [Richard Dawkins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Dawkins "Richard Dawkins") referred to him as "the wittiest of all scientific writers"; [Stephen Jay Gould](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Jay_Gould "Stephen Jay Gould") as "the cleverest man I have ever known".

Medawar was the youngest child of a Lebanese father and a British mother, and a Brazilian and British citizen by birth. He prepared for university admission at a [public secondary school](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_school_(United_Kingdom) "Public school (United Kingdom)"), [Marlborough College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marlborough_College "Marlborough College"), about which he had ill feelings generally, but was positive with regard to his biology teacher, [Ashley Gordon Lowndes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashley_Gordon_Lowndes "Ashley Gordon Lowndes"). He then pursued his university training in zoology under [John Young](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Zachary_Young "John Zachary Young") at [Magdalen College, Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magdalen_College,_Oxford "Magdalen College, Oxford"); after a scholarship and fellowship there, he went on to hold [named professorial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academic_ranks_in_the_United_Kingdom#Named_professorships "Academic ranks in the United Kingdom") positions in zoology at the [University of Birmingham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Birmingham "University of Birmingham") and [University College London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_College_London "University College London"). Until he was partially disabled by a [cerebral infarction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cerebral_infarction "Cerebral infarction"), he was Director of the [National Institute for Medical Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Institute_for_Medical_Research "National Institute for Medical Research") (at [Mill Hill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mill_Hill "Mill Hill")).

With his Oxford, then Birmingham trainees, doctoral student [Leslie Brent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leslie_Brent "Leslie Brent") and [postdoctoral fellow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postdoc "Postdoc") [Rupert E. Billingham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rupert_E._Billingham "Rupert E. Billingham"), he demonstrated the principle of acquired immunological tolerance (the phenomenon of unresponsiveness of the immune system to certain molecules), which was theoretically predicted by [Mac Burnet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Macfarlane_Burnet "Frank Macfarlane Burnet"). The understanding of immunological tolerance became the foundation of tissue and organ transplantation. He and Burnet shared the 1960 [Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physiology_or_Medicine "Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine") "for discovery of acquired immunological tolerance".

## Early life and education

Medawar is stated as having been born on 28 February 1915 in Rio de Janeiro, Brazil, a place his parents had taken residence, and elsewhere as having been born in [Petrópolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petrópolis "Petrópolis"), a Brazilian *Imperial City*, 40 kilometres (25 mi) north of Rio de Janeiro city. He was the third of three children of his British mother Edith Muriel (née Dowling), and his father, a [naturalised](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naturalization "Naturalization") British citizen, Nicholas Agnatius Medawar, who had been born in the village of [Jounieh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jounieh "Jounieh"), north of [Beirut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beirut "Beirut"), Lebanon. He had a brother Philip and a sister Pamela. (Pamela was later married to Sir [David Hunt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Hunt_(diplomat) "David Hunt (diplomat)"), who served as Private Secretary to prime ministers [Clement Attlee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clement_Attlee "Clement Attlee") and [Winston Churchill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winston_Churchill "Winston Churchill").) His father, a Lebanese [Maronite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maronite_Catholic "Maronite Catholic") by heritage, worked for a British dental supplies manufacturer that sent him to Brazil as an agent. He later described his father's profession as selling "false teeth in South America". Peter Medawar's status as a British citizen was acquired at birth, as he said, "My birth was registered at the British [Consulate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consulate "Consulate") in good time to acquire the status of 'natural-born British subject'."

Medawar left Brazil with his family for England at the end of [World War I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I "World War I"), in 1918 and he lived there for the rest of his life. According to other accounts, he moved to England when he was 13 (i.e., 1928–1929) or 14 (i.e., 1929–1930). Under [Brazilian nationality law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brazilian_nationality_law "Brazilian nationality law"), he had Brazilian citizenship from having been born there ([jus soli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jus_soli "Jus soli")). When he turned 18, the age at which [Brazilians are liable to conscription](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conscription_in_Brazil "Conscription in Brazil"), he applied for exemption to [Joaquim Pedro Salgado Filho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joaquim_Pedro_Salgado_Filho "Joaquim Pedro Salgado Filho"), his godfather and the then Minister of Aviation. This was denied by President [Eurico Gaspar Dutra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurico_Gaspar_Dutra "Eurico Gaspar Dutra"), so Medawar [renounced](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renunciation_of_citizenship "Renunciation of citizenship") his Brazilian citizenship.

In 1928, before university, Medawar went to the U.K. secondary educational institution, the [public school](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_school_(United_Kingdom) "Public school (United Kingdom)"), [Marlborough College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marlborough_College "Marlborough College"), in [Wiltshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wiltshire "Wiltshire"). (That school, while not disclosed in his autobiography, is explicitly disclosed in other media venues, including an extensive 1984 profile in *[New Scientist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Scientist "New Scientist")*, a profile in which he is quoted as identifying the school.)

Medawar has spoken—in statements reproduced in quotation—both negatively and positively about his secondary school; on the one hand, he has noted that his classmates "were critical and querulous at the same time, wondering what kind of person [he, ] a Lebanese was—something foreign you can be sure", and that the school emphasised sport, an area in which he was weak. Medawar refers to himself, again in his own words, as "uniformly unhappy from beginning to end" in his time at Marlborough, reflecting—in addition to the response of fellow students to his foreign heritage—that he witnessed bullying there, things that left him "resentful and disgusted at the manners and mores of [what he viewed as that] essentially tribal institution" the rest of his life. (He would go in his statements to further liken Marlborough to the training schools for the [Nazi SS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi_SS "Nazi SS"), "founded upon the twin pillars of sex and sadism.") With regard to their biology instruction, Medawar found anatomical dissection of animals, an area in which he was weak, to be "pedagogically useless and exasperatingly boring"; the school dictum's about which was, by Medawar's description, "[b]loody foolish is the boy whose drawing of his dissection differs in any way whatsoever from... the textbook."

On the other hand, he reflects positively upon his time under his Marlborough biology instructor, [Ashley Gordon Lowndes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashley_Gordon_Lowndes "Ashley Gordon Lowndes"), to whom he has referred as a "very, very good biology teacher". Despite describing him as being "almost incapable of expressing himself", Medawar states that Lowndes, who received a [ScD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ScD "ScD") from [Cambridge University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_University "Cambridge University") while teaching, was "quite good", going on to say,

> He fired me with enthusiasm. He adored his subject, and was so passionate about it that that communicated itself[,]

such that a media profile of him credits this relationship and institution with being the place where Medawar's "scientific career took its start". (Lowndes had also taught professors and Royal Society fellows [J.Z. Young](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Z._Young "John Z. Young") and [R.J. Pumphrey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Julius_Pumphrey?action=edit&redlink=1 "Richard Julius Pumphrey").) Noting the need his family felt that he get a scholarship to Oxford—one of the motivations for Medawar's attending Marlborough—he noted that he did not win such, and that he had "never been any good at competitive exams".

Nevertheless, in 1932, he went on to [Magdalen College, Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magdalen_College,_Oxford "Magdalen College, Oxford"), graduating with a first-class honours degree in zoology in 1935. Medawar was then appointed a Christopher Welch scholar, and then a senior [demy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demyship "Demyship") of Magdalen in 1935. He also worked at the [Sir William Dunn School of Pathology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_William_Dunn_School_of_Pathology "Sir William Dunn School of Pathology") supervised by [Howard Florey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Howard_Florey "Howard Florey") (later Nobel laureate, and who inspired him to take up immunology) and completed his doctoral thesis in 1941. In 1938, he became Fellow of Magdalen through an examination, the position he held until 1944. It was there that he started working with J. Z. Young on the regeneration of nerves. His invention of a nerve glue proved useful in surgical operations of severed nerves during World War II.

The [University of Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Oxford "University of Oxford") approved his [Doctor of Philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Philosophy "Doctor of Philosophy") thesis titled "*Growth promoting and growth inhibiting factors in normal and abnormal development*" in 1941, but because of the prohibitive cost of supplication (the process by which the degree is officially conferred), he spent the money on his urgent [appendicectomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appendicectomy "Appendicectomy") instead. The University of Oxford later awarded him a [Doctor of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Science "Doctor of Science") degree in 1947.

## Career and research

After completing his PhD, Medawar was appointed a Rolleston Prizeman in 1942, senior [research fellow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Research_fellow "Research fellow") of [St John's College, Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_John's_College,_Oxford "St John's College, Oxford"), in 1944, and a university demonstrator in zoology and comparative anatomy, also in 1944. He was re-elected [fellow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow "Fellow") of Magdalen from 1946 to 1947. In 1947, he became Mason Professor of Zoology at the [University of Birmingham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Birmingham "University of Birmingham") and worked there until 1951. He transferred to [University College London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_College_London "University College London") in 1951 as Jodrell Professor of Zoology and Comparative Anatomy.

In 1962, he was appointed director of the [National Institute for Medical Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Institute_for_Medical_Research "National Institute for Medical Research"). His predecessor Sir [Charles Harrington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Harington_(chemist) "Charles Harington (chemist)") was an able administrator such that taking over his post was, as he described, "[N]o more strenuous than ... sliding over into the driving-seat of a Rolls-Royce". He was head of the transplantation section of the [Medical Research Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_Research_Council_(United_Kingdom) "Medical Research Council (United Kingdom)")'s clinical research centre at Harrow from 1971 to 1986. He became professor of experimental medicine at the [Royal Institution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Institution "Royal Institution") (1977–1983), and president of the [Royal Postgraduate Medical School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Postgraduate_Medical_School "Royal Postgraduate Medical School") (1981–1987).

### Immunology

Medawar's first scientific research was on the effect of [malt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malt "Malt") on the development of connective tissue cells ([mesenchyme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesenchyme "Mesenchyme")) in chicken. Reading the draft of the manuscript, Howard Florey commented that it was more philosophical than scientific. It was published in the *[Quarterly Journal of Experimental Physiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quarterly_Journal_of_Experimental_Physiology "Quarterly Journal of Experimental Physiology")* in 1937.

Medawar's involvement with what became [transplant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organ_transplant "Organ transplant") research began during World War II, when he investigated possible improvements in [skin grafts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skin_grafting "Skin grafting"). His first publication on the subject was "Sheets of Pure Epidermal Epithelium from Human Skin", which was published in *[Nature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nature_(journal) "Nature (journal)")* in 1941. His studies particularly concerned solution for skin wounds among soldiers in the war. In 1947, he moved to the University of Birmingham, taking along with him his PhD student Leslie Brent and postdoctoral fellow Rupert Billingham. His research became more focused in 1949, when Australian biologist [Frank Macfarlane Burnet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Macfarlane_Burnet "Frank Macfarlane Burnet"), at the [Walter and Eliza Hall Institute of Medical Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_and_Eliza_Hall_Institute_of_Medical_Research "Walter and Eliza Hall Institute of Medical Research") in Melbourne, advanced the hypothesis that during [embryonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embryo "Embryo") life and immediately after birth, [cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_(biology) "Cell (biology)") gradually acquire the ability to distinguish between their own [tissue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biological_tissue "Biological tissue") substances on the one hand and unwanted cells and foreign material on the other.

With Billingham, he published a seminal paper in 1951 on grafting technique. [Santa J. Ono](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santa_J._Ono "Santa J. Ono"), the American immunologist, has described the enduring impact of this paper to modern science. Based on this technique of grafting, Medawar's team devised a method to test Burnet's hypothesis. They extracted cells from young mouse embryos and injected them into another mouse of different strains. When the mouse developed into adult and skin grafting from that of the original strain was performed, there was no [tissue rejection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transplant_rejection "Transplant rejection"). The mouse had tolerated the foreign tissue, which would normally be rejected. Their experimental proof of Burnet's hypothesis was first published in a brief article in *[Nature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nature_(journal) "Nature (journal)")* in 1953, followed by a series of papers, and a comprehensive description in *[Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophical_Transactions_of_the_Royal_Society_B "Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B")* in 1956, giving the name "actively acquired tolerance".

#### Research outcomes

Medawar was awarded his [Nobel Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize "Nobel Prize") in 1960 with [Burnet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Macfarlane_Burnet "Frank Macfarlane Burnet") for their work in tissue grafting which is the basis of [organ transplants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organ_transplant "Organ transplant"), and their discovery of acquired immunological tolerance. This work was used in dealing with [skin grafts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skin_graft "Skin graft") required after [burns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burn_(injury) "Burn (injury)"). Medawar's work resulted in a shift of emphasis in the science of [immunology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immunology "Immunology") from one that attempts to deal with the fully developed immunity mechanism to one that attempts to alter the immunity mechanism itself, as in the attempt to suppress the body's [rejection of organ transplants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organ_rejection "Organ rejection"). It directly laid the foundation for the first successful organ transplantation in humans, specifically [kidney transplantation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kidney_transplantation "Kidney transplantation"), carried out by an American physician [Joseph Murray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Murray "Joseph Murray"), who eventually received the 1990 [Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physiology_or_Medicine "Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine").

### Theory of senescence

Medawar's 1951 lecture "An Unsolved Problem of Biology" (published 1952) addressed ageing and [senescence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senescence "Senescence"), and he begins by defining both terms as follows:

> We obviously need a word for mere ageing, and I propose to use 'ageing' itself for just that purpose. 'Ageing' hereafter stands for mere ageing, and has no other innuendo. I shall use the word 'senescence' to mean ageing accompanied by that decline of bodily faculties and sensibilities and energies which ageing colloquially entails.

He then tackles the question of why evolution has permitted organisms to senesce, even though (1) senescence lowers individual fitness, and (2) there is no obvious necessity for senescence. In answering this question, Medawar provides two fundamental and interrelated insights. First, there is an inexorable decline in probability of an organism's existence, and, therefore, in what he terms "[reproductive value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reproductive_value_(population_genetics) "Reproductive value (population genetics)")." He suggests that it therefore follows that the force of [natural selection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_selection "Natural selection") weakens progressively with age late in life (because the [fecundity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fecundity "Fecundity") of younger age-groups is overwhelmingly more significant in producing the next generation). What happens to an organism after reproduction is only weakly reflected in natural selection by the effect on its younger relatives. He pointed out that likelihood of death at various times of life, as judged by [life tables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life_tables "Life tables"), was an indirect measure of [fitness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fitness_(biology) "Fitness (biology)"), that is, the capacity of an organism to propagate its genes. Life tables for humans show, for example that the lowest likelihood of death in human females comes at about age 14, which in primitive societies would likely be an age of peak reproduction. This has served as the basis for all three modern theories for the [evolution of senescence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolution_of_ageing "Evolution of ageing").

### Theory on endocrine evolution

Medawar presented a talk on [viviparity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viviparity "Viviparity") in animals (the phenomenon by which some animals give live birth) at a meeting on evolution at Oxford in July 1952. Later published in 1953, he introduced an aphorism:

> Endocrine evolution is not an evolution of hormones but an evolution of the uses to which they are put; an evolution not, to put it crudely, of chemical formulae but of reactivities, reaction patterns and tissue competences.

The notion that evolution and diversity of endocrine function in animals are due to different uses of each hormone rather than different hormones themselves became an established fact. The paper is also regarded as a pioneer in the field of [reproductive immunology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reproductive_immunology "Reproductive immunology").

## Personal life

Medawar never knew the exact meaning of his surname, an [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_language "Arabic language") word, he was told, for "to make round"; but which a friend explained to him as "little round fat man".

On 27 February 1937, Medawar married [Jean Medawar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Medawar "Jean Medawar") ([née](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_name#Maiden_and_married_names "Birth name") Taylor). They met while in graduate class at Oxford, he at Magdalen and Taylor at [Somerville College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somerville_College,_Oxford "Somerville College, Oxford"). Taylor approached him for the meaning of "[heuristic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic "Heuristic")", which she had to ask twice, and he had to finally offer lessons in philosophy. Medawar described her as "the most beautiful woman in Oxford"; but Taylor's impression was he looked "mildly diabolical." Taylor's family objected to their marriage as Medawar had "no background, and no money." Her mother was explicitly afraid of having "black" grandchildren; her aunt disinherited her. The couple had two sons, Charles and Alexander, and two daughters, Caroline and Louise. (Caroline is mother of screenwriter and director [Alex Garland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alex_Garland "Alex Garland").)

Medawar was interested in a wide range of subjects including [opera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opera "Opera"), [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy "Philosophy") and [cricket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cricket "Cricket"). He was exceptionally tall, 6 ft 5 inches (196 cm), and physically robust, with a big voice noted particularly during his lectures. He was renowned for wit and humour, which he claimed he inherited from his "raucous" mother.

As he completed his PhD research in 1941, he did not receive the degree as he could not afford the requisite £25, to which he commented:

> I'm an impostor. I am a doctor, but not a PhD. It was so expensive, I decided not to take one. Morally I'm a PhD, in the sense I could have had one... Anyway, it was unfashionable in my day. [John Young](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Zachary_Young "John Zachary Young") was not a PhD either. A PhD was regarded then as a newfangled German importation, as bizarre and undesirable as having German bands playing on streetcorners...

He was regarded as the philosopher [Karl Popper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Popper "Karl Popper")'s best-known disciple in science.

### Views on religion

Medawar declared:

> I believe that a reasonable case can be made for saying, not that we believe in God because He exists but rather that He exists because we believe in Him... Considered as an element of the world, God has the same degree and kind of objective reality as do other products of mind... I regret my disbelief in God and religious answers generally, for I believe it would give satisfaction and comfort to many in need of it if it were possible to discover and propound good scientific and philosophic reasons to believe in God... To abdicate from the rule of reason and substitute for it an authentication of belief by the intentness and degree of conviction with which we hold it can be perilous and destructive... I am a rationalist—something of a period piece nowadays, I admit...

Although he normally sympathised with Christianity especially on moral teachings, he found the Biblical stories unethical and was "shocked by the way in which [Biblical] characters deceived and defrauded each other." He even asked his wife "to make sure that such a book did not fall into the hands of [their] children."

Nonetheless, he also said the following, which suggests that although religion has good value for humans in aggregate, it does not help them all equally:

> Religion has not sustained me on any of the occasions when the comfort it professes would have been most welcome.

## Later life and death

In 1959 Medawar was invited by the BBC to present the broadcaster's annual [Reith Lectures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reith_Lectures "Reith Lectures")—following in the footsteps of his colleague, J. Z. Young, who was Reith Lecturer in 1950. For his own series of six radio broadcasts, titled *The Future of Man*, Medawar examined how the human race might continue to evolve.

While attending the annual [British Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Association "British Association") meeting in 1969, Medawar suffered a [stroke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stroke "Stroke") when reading the lesson at [Exeter Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exeter_Cathedral "Exeter Cathedral"), a duty which falls on every new President of the [British Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Association "British Association"). It was, as he said, "monstrous bad luck because [Jim Whyte Black](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_W._Black "James W. Black") had not yet devised [beta-blockers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beta-blockers "Beta-blockers"), which slow the heart-beat and could have preserved my health and my career". Before the stroke, Medawar was regarded one of the most influential scientists, as [Kern Wildenthal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kern_Wildenthal "Kern Wildenthal") (president of the [University of Texas Southwestern Medical Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Texas_Southwestern_Medical_Center "University of Texas Southwestern Medical Center")) put it: "He was the most brilliant biologist, the most influentiai scientific thinker, the most lucid writer and the most scintillating conversationist of the 20th century."

After the impairment of his speech and movement, Medawar, with his wife's help, reorganised his life and continued to write and do research though on a greatly restricted scale. However, he suffered further strokes, and in 1987 he died in the [Royal Free Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Free_Hospital "Royal Free Hospital"), London. He is interred with his wife Jean (1913–2005) in the graveyard of [St Andrew's Church in Alfriston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Andrew's_Church,_Alfriston "St Andrew's Church, Alfriston") in East Sussex. In 2024 Medawar's family donated his [Nobel Prize medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_medal "Nobel Prize medal") to the University of Oxford.

## Awards and honours

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/1e/Sir_PETER_MEDAWAR_1915_-_1987_Pioneer_of_Transplantation_Immunology_lived_here.jpg/250px-Sir_PETER_MEDAWAR_1915_-_1987_Pioneer_of_Transplantation_Immunology_lived_here.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Sir_PETER_MEDAWAR_1915_-_1987_Pioneer_of_Transplantation_Immunology_lived_here.jpg)

Blue plaque erected on 14 July 2014 by English Heritage at 25 Downshire Hill, Hampstead

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/07/At_University_of_Birmingham_2026_010.jpg/250px-At_University_of_Birmingham_2026_010.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:At_University_of_Birmingham_2026_010.jpg)

Plaque at the University of Birmingham

Medawar was elected a [Fellow of the Royal Society (FRS) in 1949](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Fellows_of_the_Royal_Society_elected_in_1949 "List of Fellows of the Royal Society elected in 1949"). With [Frank Macfarlane Burnet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Macfarlane_Burnet "Frank Macfarlane Burnet") he shared the 1960 [Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physiology_or_Medicine "Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine") "for discovery of acquired immunological tolerance". The British government conferred him a [CBE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CBE "CBE") in 1958, [knighted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knight_bachelor "Knight bachelor") him in 1965, and appointed him to the [Order of the Companions of Honour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_the_Companions_of_Honour "Order of the Companions of Honour") in 1972, and [Order of Merit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_Merit_(Commonwealth) "Order of Merit (Commonwealth)") in 1981. He was elected an [EMBO Member](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EMBO_Member "EMBO Member") in 1964 and received the [Royal Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Medal "Royal Medal") in 1959, and the [Copley Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copley_Medal "Copley Medal") in 1969 both from the [Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society "Royal Society"). He was President of the [British Association for the Advancement of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Association_for_the_Advancement_of_Science "British Association for the Advancement of Science") during 1968–1969. He was awarded the [UNESCO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UNESCO "UNESCO")  [Kalinga Prize for the Popularization of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalinga_Prize "Kalinga Prize") in 1985. He was awarded an Honorary [Doctor of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Science "Doctor of Science") Degree in 1961 by the University of Birmingham. He was elected a member of the American Society of Immunologists in 1971, and elected foreign member of the [American Academy of Arts and Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Academy_of_Arts_and_Sciences "American Academy of Arts and Sciences") in 1959, the [American Philosophical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Philosophical_Society "American Philosophical Society") in 1961, and the US [National Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Academy_of_Sciences "National Academy of Sciences") in 1965.

Medawar was elected President of the Royal Society for the term 1970–1975, but a severe stroke in 1969 prevented him from taking up the office.

Medawar was awarded the 1987 [Michael Faraday Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Faraday_Prize "Michael Faraday Prize") "*for the contribution his books had made in presenting to the public, and to scientists themselves, the intellectual nature and the essential humanity of pursuing science at the highest level and the part it played in our modern culture*".

Medawar has three awards named after him:

1. The [Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society "Royal Society") created the [Medawar Lecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medawar_Lecture "Medawar Lecture") in 1986 in a subject relating to the history of science, philosophy of science or the social function of science. Since 2007, the lecture has been merged with two older ones honouring [John Wilkins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Wilkins "John Wilkins") and [John Desmond Bernal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Desmond_Bernal "John Desmond Bernal"), and became [Wilkins-Bernal-Medawar Lecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilkins-Bernal-Medawar_Lecture "Wilkins-Bernal-Medawar Lecture") and the accompanying award, [Wilkins-Bernal-Medawar Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilkins-Bernal-Medawar_Lecture "Wilkins-Bernal-Medawar Lecture").
2. [Medawar Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medawar_Medal "Medawar Medal"), awarded by the [British Transplant Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Transplant_Society "British Transplant Society") in recognition of significant research in [organ transplantation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organ_transplantation "Organ transplantation").
3. Peter Brian Medawar Medal, awarded by the State Medical Academy of Rio de Janeiro.

The University of Oxford has established a research consortium named the Peter Medawar Building for Pathogen Research.

The Department of Science and Technology Studies of the University College London has STS Peter Medawar Prize for undergraduate students.

The University of Birmingham Public Engagement with Research (PER) Team established an annual Light of Understanding Award to individuals and groups who accomplished public engagement with research work.

## Publications

Medawar was recognised as a brilliant author. [Richard Dawkins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Dawkins "Richard Dawkins") called him "the wittiest of all scientific writers", and *[New Scientist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Scientist "New Scientist")* magazine's obituary called him "perhaps the best science writer of his generation".

One of his best-known essays is his 1961 criticism of [Pierre Teilhard de Chardin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Teilhard_de_Chardin "Pierre Teilhard de Chardin")'s *[The Phenomenon of Man](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Phenomenon_of_Man "The Phenomenon of Man")*, of which he said: "Its author can be excused of dishonesty only on the grounds that before deceiving others he has taken great pains to deceive himself".

His books include

- *The Uniqueness of the Individual*, which includes essays on immunology, graft rejection and acquired immune tolerance. Basic Books, New York, 1957
- *The Future of Man: the BBC [Reith Lectures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reith_Lectures "Reith Lectures") 1959*, Methuen, London, 1960
- [*Is the scientific paper a fraud?*, *The Listener*, 12 September 1963 pp. 377-8](https://www.weizmann.ac.il/mcb/alon/sites/mcb.UriAlon/files/uploads/medawar.pdf)
- *The Art of the Soluble*, Methuen & Co., London/ Barnes and Noble, New York, 1967
- *Induction and Intuition in Scientific Thought*, American Philosophical Society. Philadelphia/Methuen & Co., London, 1969
- *The Life Science*, Harper & Row, 1978
- *Advice to a Young Scientist*, Harper & Row, 1979
- *[Pluto's Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Best_science_book_ever#Other_nominees "Best science book ever")*, incorporating an earlier book *The Art of the Soluble*, Oxford University Press, 1982
- *Aristotle to Zoos* (with his wife [Jean Shinglewood Taylor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Shinglewood_Taylor "Jean Shinglewood Taylor")), Harvard University Press, 1983
- *The Limits of Science*, Oxford University Press, 1988
- *The Hope of Progress: A Scientist looks at Problems in Philosophy, Literature and Science*, Anchor Press / Doubleday, Garden City, 1973
- *Memoirs of a Thinking Radish: An Autobiography*, Oxford University Press, 1986
- *The Threat and the Glory: Reflections on Science and Scientists* (ed.: David Pyke), a posthumously collected volume of essays, HarperCollins, 1990

Apart from his books on science and philosophy, he wrote a short feature article on "Some Meistersinger Records" in the issue of *[The Gramophone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Gramophone "The Gramophone")* for November 1930. The author was a P. B. Medawar. The evidence that this was indeed the future Sir Peter Medawar—then a schoolboy of 15—was discussed in "Gramophone" in 1995 ("'Gramophone', *[Die Meistersinger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Die_Meistersinger "Die Meistersinger")* and immunology", by John E. Havard, December 1995).
