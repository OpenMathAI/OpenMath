**Susumu Tonegawa** (利根川 進, *Tonegawa Susumu*; September 5, 1939 – July 11, 2026) was a Japanese scientist who was the sole recipient of the [Nobel Prize for Physiology or Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_for_Physiology_or_Medicine "Nobel Prize for Physiology or Medicine") in 1987 for his discovery of [V(D)J recombination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V(D)J_recombination "V(D)J recombination"), the [genetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetics "Genetics") mechanism which produces [antibody](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antibody "Antibody") diversity. Although he won the Nobel Prize for his work in [immunology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immunology "Immunology"), Tonegawa was a [molecular biologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_biology "Molecular biology") by training and, following his Nobel Prize win, changed fields to [neuroscience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neuroscience "Neuroscience"), examining the molecular, cellular and neuronal basis of memory formation and retrieval.

## Early life and education

Tonegawa was born in [Nagoya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nagoya "Nagoya"), Japan, and attended [Hibiya High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hibiya_High_School "Hibiya High School") in Tokyo. While a student at [Kyoto University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyoto_University "Kyoto University"), Tonegawa became fascinated with [operon theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operon_theory "Operon theory") after reading papers by [François Jacob](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/François_Jacob "François Jacob") and [Jacques Monod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques_Monod "Jacques Monod"), whom he credits in part with inspiring his interest in molecular biology. Tonegawa graduated from Kyoto University in 1963 and, due to limited options for molecular biology study in Japan at the time, moved to the [University of California, San Diego](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_San_Diego "University of California, San Diego"), to do his doctorate study under Dr. Masaki Hayashi. He received his Ph.D. in 1968.

## Career

Tonegawa conducted post-doctoral work at the [Salk Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salk_Institute "Salk Institute") in [San Diego](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Diego "San Diego") in the laboratory of [Renato Dulbecco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renato_Dulbecco "Renato Dulbecco"). With encouragement from Dr. Dulbecco, Tonegawa moved to the [Basel Institute for Immunology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basel_Institute_for_Immunology "Basel Institute for Immunology") in Basel, Switzerland, in 1971, where he transitioned from molecular biology into immunology studies and carried out his landmark immunology studies.

In 1981, Tonegawa became a professor at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology. In 1994, he was appointed the first Director of the MIT Center for Learning and Memory, which developed under his guidance into The [Picower Institute for Learning and Memory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Picower_Institute_for_Learning_and_Memory "Picower Institute for Learning and Memory"). Tonegawa resigned his directorship in 2006 and served as a Picower Professor of Neuroscience and Biology and a [Howard Hughes Medical Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Howard_Hughes_Medical_Institute "Howard Hughes Medical Institute") Investigator.

He also served as Director of the [RIKEN Brain Science Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RIKEN_Brain_Science_Institute "RIKEN Brain Science Institute") from 2009 to 2017.

## Research

### Immunology

Tonegawa's Nobel Prize work elucidated the genetic mechanism of the adaptive [immune system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immune_system "Immune system"), which had been the central question of immunology for over 100 years. Prior to Tonegawa's discovery, one early idea to explain the adaptive immune system suggested that each gene produces one protein; however, there are under 19,000 genes in the human body which nonetheless can produce millions of antibodies. In experiments beginning in 1976, Tonegawa showed that genetic material rearranges itself to form millions of antibodies. Comparing the [DNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA "DNA") of [B cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B_cells "B cells") (a type of [white blood cell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_blood_cell "White blood cell")) in embryonic and adult [mice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mouse "Mouse"), he observed that genes in the mature B cells of the adult mice are moved around, recombined, and deleted to form the diversity of the variable region of antibodies. This process is known as [V(D)J recombination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V(D)J_recombination "V(D)J recombination").

In 1983, Tonegawa also discovered a transcriptional enhancer element associated with antibody gene complex, the first cellular enhancer element.

### Neuroscience

Shortly following his Nobel Prize, Tonegawa again changed fields from immunology to neuroscience, where he focused his research in the ensuing years.

Tonegawa's lab pioneered introductory [transgenic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transgenesis "Transgenesis") and [gene-knockout](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gene_knockout "Gene knockout") technologies in mammalian systems. He was involved in early work demonstrating the importance of [CaMKII-](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CAMK "CAMK") (1992) and the [NMDA receptor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NMDA_receptor "NMDA receptor")-dependent [synaptic plasticity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synaptic_plasticity "Synaptic plasticity") (1996) in memory formation.

His lab discovered that [dendritic neuronal spines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dendritic_spine "Dendritic spine") in the [temporal cortex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temporal_lobe "Temporal lobe") are a likely target for treatment of [Fragile X Syndrome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fragile_X_syndrome "Fragile X syndrome"). With one dosage of the inhibitor drug FRAX586, Tonegawa showed a marked reduction of FXS symptoms in the mouse model.

Tonegawa was an early adopter of [optogenetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optogenetics "Optogenetics") and [biotechnology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biotechnology "Biotechnology") in neuroscience research, leading to his groundbreaking work identifying and manipulating [memory engram cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engram_(neuropsychology) "Engram (neuropsychology)"). In 2012, his lab demonstrated that the activation of a specific sub-population of mouse [hippocampal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hippocampus "Hippocampus") neurons, labelled during a [fear conditioning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fear_conditioning "Fear conditioning") paradigm, is sufficient to evoke a behavioral response correlated with a precise memory trace. This demonstrated for the first time that memory information is stored in specific cellular ensembles in the hippocampus, now frequently called memory engram cells.

More recently, his lab continues to employ optogenetic technology and virus injection techniques to expand their findings on the engram cell ensemble. Notably, Tonegawa uncovered the role of memory engram cell ensembles in memory [valence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valence_(psychology) "Valence (psychology)"), social memory, as well as their role in brain disorders such as [depression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Major_depressive_disorder "Major depressive disorder"), [amnesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amnesia "Amnesia"), and [Alzheimer's disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alzheimer's_disease "Alzheimer's disease"). These works provide proofs of concept for future medical treatments in humans through the manipulation of memory engram ensembles.

## Personal life and death

Tonegawa resided in the Boston area with his wife, Mayumi Yoshinari Tonegawa, who worked as an [NHK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NHK "NHK") (Japan Broadcasting Corporation) director/interviewer and is now a freelance science writer. The Tonegawas have three children, Hidde Tonegawa, Hanna Tonegawa, and Satto Tonegawa (deceased).

He was a fan of the Boston Red Sox, and threw out an opening pitch during their 2004 World Series championship season.

Tonegawa died at his home in [San Mateo, California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Mateo,_California "San Mateo, California"), on July 11, 2026, at the age of 86.
