**William G. Kaelin Jr.** (born November 23, 1957) is an American Nobel laureate [physician-scientist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physician-scientist "Physician-scientist"). He is a professor of medicine at [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University "Harvard University") and the [Dana–Farber Cancer Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dana–Farber_Cancer_Institute "Dana–Farber Cancer Institute") where his laboratory studies [tumor suppressor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tumor_suppressor "Tumor suppressor") proteins.
He is the recipient of numerous awards including the [Albert Lasker Award for Basic Medical Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Lasker_Award_for_Basic_Medical_Research "Albert Lasker Award for Basic Medical Research") and the [AACR Princess Takamatsu Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AACR_Awards#AACR_Princess_Takamatsu_Memorial_Lectureship "AACR Awards")
. In 2019 he won the [Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physiology_or_Medicine "Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine") in with [Peter J. Ratcliffe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_J._Ratcliffe "Peter J. Ratcliffe") and [Gregg L. Semenza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregg_L._Semenza "Gregg L. Semenza") for their work on how cells sense and adapt to oxygen availability.

## Early life and education

Kaelin was born in [New York City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_City "New York City") on November 23, 1957, to William George and Nancy Priscilla (Horn) Kaelin. Kaelin earned his bachelor's degree in mathematics and chemistry at [Duke University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duke_University "Duke University"), and stayed to attain an MD, graduating in 1982. He did his residency in internal medicine at [Johns Hopkins School of Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johns_Hopkins_School_of_Medicine "Johns Hopkins School of Medicine") and his fellowship in oncology at [Dana–Farber Cancer Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dana–Farber_Cancer_Institute "Dana–Farber Cancer Institute") (DFCI). After deciding as an undergraduate that research was not a strength of his, at DFCI he did research in the lab of [David Livingston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_M._Livingston "David M. Livingston"), where he found success in the study of [retinoblastoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retinoblastoma "Retinoblastoma"). In 1992, he set up his own lab at DFCI down the hall from Livingston's where he investigated hereditary forms of cancer such as [von Hippel–Lindau disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Von_Hippel–Lindau_disease "Von Hippel–Lindau disease"). He became a professor at [Harvard Medical School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_Medical_School "Harvard Medical School") in 2002.

## Career

He became assistant director of Basic Science at the Dana–Farber/Harvard Cancer Center in 2008. His research at Dana–Farber has focused on understanding the role of mutations in tumor suppressor genes in cancer development. His major work has been on the [retinoblastoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retinoblastoma "Retinoblastoma"), von Hippel–Lindau, and [p53](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P53 "P53") tumor suppressor genes.

His work has been funded by the [National Institutes of Health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Institutes_of_Health "National Institutes of Health"), [American Cancer Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Cancer_Society "American Cancer Society"), [Doris Duke Charitable Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doris_Duke_Charitable_Foundation "Doris Duke Charitable Foundation") and others.

He serves as vice-chair of Scientific Programs on the [Damon Runyon Cancer Research Foundation Board](https://www.damonrunyon.org) of Directors and Chair of the Damon Runyon Physician-Scientist Training Award selection committee and is a member of the board of directors at [Eli Lilly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eli_Lilly "Eli Lilly") and the [Stand Up to Cancer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stand_Up_to_Cancer "Stand Up to Cancer") scientific advisory committee.

## Research

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/5b/HIF_Nobel_Prize_Physiology_Medicine_2019_Hegasy_ENG.png/250px-HIF_Nobel_Prize_Physiology_Medicine_2019_Hegasy_ENG.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:HIF_Nobel_Prize_Physiology_Medicine_2019_Hegasy_ENG.png)

Illustration of how cells sense and adapt to oxygen availability

Following his post-doctorate, Kaelin set up a laboratory at Dana-Farber in 1993 to continue his research on tumor suppression. He had become interested in [Von Hippel–Lindau disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Von_Hippel–Lindau_disease "Von Hippel–Lindau disease") (VHL). VHL tumors, caused by gene mutation, were known to be [angiogenic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angiogenesis "Angiogenesis"), creating blood vessels that secreted [erythropoietin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erythropoietin "Erythropoietin") (EPO), a hormone known to be part of the body's mechanic to react to [hypoxia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypoxia_(medical) "Hypoxia (medical)"), or low oxygen levels in the blood. Kaelin hypothesized that there may be a connection between the formation of VHL tumors and the deficiency of the body to detect oxygen. Kaelin's research found that in VHL subjects, there are genes that express the formation of a protein critical in the EPO process, but which the mutation suppressed. Kaelin's work aligned with that of [Peter J. Ratcliffe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_J._Ratcliffe "Peter J. Ratcliffe") and [Gregg Semenza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregg_Semenza "Gregg Semenza") who separately had identified a two-part protein, [hypoxia-inducible factors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypoxia-inducible_factors "Hypoxia-inducible factors") (HIF) that was essential to EPO production and which was triggered by oxygen levels in the blood. Kaelin's work found that the VHL protein would help regulate the HIF, and in subjects where the VHL proteins were not present, the HIF would overproduce EPO and lead to cancer. The combined work of Kaelin, Ratcliffe, and Semenza identified the pathway of how cells detect and react to oxygen levels in the blood, and have led to the development of drugs to help patients with [anaemia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anaemia "Anaemia") and [kidney failure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kidney_failure "Kidney failure").

## Personal life

He was married to breast cancer surgeon [Carolyn Kaelin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carolyn_Kaelin "Carolyn Kaelin") from 1988 until her death from [glioblastoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glioblastoma "Glioblastoma") in 2015. They have two children.
