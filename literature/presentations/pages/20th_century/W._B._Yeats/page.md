**William Butler Yeats** ([/jeɪts/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English") [*YAYTS*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:Pronunciation_respelling_key "Help:Pronunciation respelling key"); 13 June 1865 – 28 January 1939) was an Irish poet, dramatist, writer and literary critic who was one of the foremost figures of [20th-century literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/20th_century_in_literature "20th century in literature"). He was a driving force behind the [Irish Literary Revival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Literary_Revival "Irish Literary Revival") and, along with [John Millington Synge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Millington_Synge "John Millington Synge") and [Lady Gregory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augusta,_Lady_Gregory "Augusta, Lady Gregory"), founded the [Abbey Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbey_Theatre "Abbey Theatre"), serving as its chief during its early years. He was awarded the [1923 Nobel Prize in Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1923_Nobel_Prize_in_Literature "1923 Nobel Prize in Literature") and later served two terms as a [Senator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seanad_Éireann_(Irish_Free_State) "Seanad Éireann (Irish Free State)") of the [Irish Free State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Free_State "Irish Free State").

A [Protestant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestant "Protestant") of [Anglo-Irish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Irish "Anglo-Irish") descent, Yeats was born in [Sandymount](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sandymount "Sandymount"), Ireland. His father practised law and was a portrait painter. He was educated in Dublin and London and spent his childhood holidays in [County Sligo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Sligo "County Sligo"). He studied poetry from an early age, when he became fascinated by [Irish legends](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_mythology "Irish mythology") and the occult. While in London he became part of the Irish literary revival. His early poetry was influenced by [John Keats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Keats "John Keats"), [William Wordsworth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Wordsworth "William Wordsworth"), [William Blake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Blake "William Blake") and many more. These topics feature in the first phase of his work, lasting roughly from his student days at the [Metropolitan School of Art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_College_of_Art_and_Design "National College of Art and Design") in Dublin until the end of the 19th century. His earliest volume of verse was published in 1889, and its slow-paced, [modernist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modernist_poetry "Modernist poetry") and [lyrical poems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyric_poetry "Lyric poetry") display debts to [Edmund Spenser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmund_Spenser "Edmund Spenser"), [Percy Bysshe Shelley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Percy_Bysshe_Shelley "Percy Bysshe Shelley") and the poets of the [Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Raphaelite_Brotherhood "Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood").

From 1900 his poetry became more rooted in the physical world, [realistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literary_realism "Literary realism") and politicised. He moved away from the transcendental beliefs of his youth, though he remained preoccupied with some elements including cyclical theories of life. He had become the chief playwright for the [Irish Literary Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Literary_Theatre "Irish Literary Theatre") in 1897, and early on promoted younger poets such as [Ezra Pound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ezra_Pound "Ezra Pound"). His major works include *[The Land of Heart's Desire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Land_of_Heart's_Desire "The Land of Heart's Desire")* (1894), *[Cathleen ni Houlihan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathleen_ni_Houlihan "Cathleen ni Houlihan")* (1902), *[The Wild Swans at Coole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Wild_Swans_at_Coole "The Wild Swans at Coole")* (1919) and *[The Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Tower_(poetry_collection) "The Tower (poetry collection)")* (1928).

## Early years

### Dublin

William Butler Yeats was born in [Sandymount](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sandymount "Sandymount") in [County Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Dublin "County Dublin"), Ireland. His father [John](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Butler_Yeats "John Butler Yeats") was a descendant of Jervis Yeats, a [Williamite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_III_of_England "William III of England") soldier, linen merchant, and well-known painter, who died in 1712. Benjamin Yeats, Jervis's grandson and William's great-great-grandfather, had in 1773 married Mary Butler of a [landed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landed_gentry "Landed gentry") family in [County Kildare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Kildare "County Kildare"). Following their marriage, they kept the name Butler. Mary was of the Butler of Neigham [Gowran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gowran "Gowran") family, descended from an illegitimate brother of [The 8th Earl of Ormond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piers_Butler,_8th_Earl_of_Ormond "Piers Butler, 8th Earl of Ormond"). At the time of his marriage, his father, John, was studying law but later pursued art studies at [Heatherley School of Fine Art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heatherley_School_of_Fine_Art "Heatherley School of Fine Art"), in London.

William's mother, [Susan Mary Pollexfen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susan_Pollexfen "Susan Pollexfen"), from [Sligo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sligo "Sligo"), came from a wealthy merchant family, who owned a milling and shipping business. Soon after William's birth, the family relocated to the Pollexfen home at Merville, Sligo, to stay with her extended family, and the young poet came to think of the area as his childhood and spiritual home. Its landscape became, over time, both personally and symbolically, his "country of the heart". So too did its location by the sea; John Yeats stated that "by marriage with a Pollexfen, we have given a tongue to the sea cliffs".

The Butler Yeats family were highly artistic; his brother [Jack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jack_Butler_Yeats "Jack Butler Yeats") became an esteemed painter, while his sisters [Elizabeth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_Yeats "Elizabeth Yeats") and [Susan Mary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lily_Yeats "Lily Yeats")—known to family and friends as Lollie and Lily—became involved in the [Arts and Crafts movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arts_and_Crafts_movement "Arts and Crafts movement"). Their cousin [Ruth Pollexfen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruth_Pollexfen "Ruth Pollexfen"), who was raised by the Yeats sisters after her parents' separation, designed the interior of the [Australian prime minister's official residence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Lodge,_Australia "The Lodge, Australia").

Yeats was raised a member of the [Protestant Ascendancy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestant_Ascendancy "Protestant Ascendancy"), which was at the time undergoing a crisis of identity. While his family was supportive of the changes Ireland was experiencing, the nationalist revival of the late 19th century directly disadvantaged his heritage and informed his outlook for the remainder of his life. In 1997, his biographer [R. F. Foster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R._F._Foster_(historian) "R. F. Foster (historian)") observed that [Napoleon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleon "Napoleon")'s dictum that to understand the man you have to know what was happening in the world when he was twenty "is manifestly true of W.B.Y." Yeats's childhood and young adulthood were shadowed by the power-shift away from the minority Protestant Ascendancy. The 1880s saw the rise of [Charles Stewart Parnell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Stewart_Parnell "Charles Stewart Parnell") and the [home rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Home_rule "Home rule") movement; the 1890s saw the momentum of [nationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nationalism "Nationalism"), while the [Irish Catholics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Catholics "Irish Catholics") became prominent around the transition of the 19th into the 20th century. These developments had a profound effect on his poetry, and his subsequent explorations of Irish identity had a significant influence on the creation of his country's biography.

In 1867, the family moved to England to aid their father, John, to further his career as an artist. At first, the Yeats children were educated at home. Their mother entertained them with stories and Irish folktales. John provided an erratic education in geography and chemistry and took William on natural history explorations of the nearby [Slough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slough "Slough") countryside. On 26 January 1877, the young poet entered the [Godolphin School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Godolphin_and_Latymer_School "Godolphin and Latymer School"), which he attended for four years. He did not distinguish himself academically, and an early school report describes his performance as "only fair. Perhaps better in Latin than in any other subject. Very poor in spelling". Though he had difficulty with mathematics and languages (possibly because he was [tone deaf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tone_deafness "Tone deafness") and had [dyslexia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dyslexia "Dyslexia")), he was fascinated by biology and zoology. In 1879 the family moved to [Bedford Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bedford_Park,_London "Bedford Park, London") taking a two-year lease at 8 Woodstock Road. For financial reasons, the family returned to Dublin toward the end of 1880, living at first in the suburbs of [Harold's Cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold's_Cross "Harold's Cross") and later in [Howth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Howth "Howth"). In October 1881, Yeats resumed his education at Dublin's [Erasmus Smith High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_High_School,_Dublin "The High School, Dublin"). His father's studio was nearby and William spent a great deal of time there, where he met many of the city's artists and writers. During this period William began to write poetry, and in 1885 the *Dublin University Review* published his first poems as well as an essay titled "The Poetry of [Sir Samuel Ferguson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Ferguson "Samuel Ferguson")". Between 1884 and 1886, William attended the Metropolitan School of Art—now the [National College of Art and Design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_College_of_Art_and_Design "National College of Art and Design")—in [Thomas Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Street,_Dublin "Thomas Street, Dublin"). In March 1888 the family moved to 3 Blenheim Road in Bedford Park where they would remain until 1902. The rent on the house in 1888 was £50 a year.

## Young poet

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/5d/William_Butler_Yeats_by_John_Butler_Yeats_1900.jpg/250px-William_Butler_Yeats_by_John_Butler_Yeats_1900.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:William_Butler_Yeats_by_John_Butler_Yeats_1900.jpg)

1900 portrait by Yeats's father, [John Butler Yeats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Butler_Yeats "John Butler Yeats")

Yeats began writing his first works when he was seventeen; these included a poem—heavily influenced by [Percy Bysshe Shelley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Percy_Bysshe_Shelley "Percy Bysshe Shelley")—that describes a magician who set up a throne in central Asia. Other pieces from this period include a draft of a play about a bishop, a monk, and a woman accused of [paganism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paganism "Paganism") by local shepherds, as well as love-poems and narrative lyrics on German knights. The early works were both conventional and, according to the critic Charles Johnston, "utterly unIrish", seeming to come out of a "vast murmurous gloom of dreams". Although Yeats's early works drew heavily on Shelley, [Edmund Spenser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmund_Spenser "Edmund Spenser"), and on the diction and colouring of [pre-Raphaelite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Raphaelite "Pre-Raphaelite") verse, he soon turned to [Irish mythology and folklore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_mythology "Irish mythology") and the writings of [William Blake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Blake "William Blake"). In later life, Yeats paid tribute to Blake by describing him as one of the "great artificers of God who uttered great truths to a little clan". In 1891, Yeats published *John Sherman* and "Dhoya", one a novella, the other a story. The influence of [Oscar Wilde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oscar_Wilde "Oscar Wilde") is evident in Yeats's theory of aesthetics, especially in his stage plays, and runs like a motif through his early works. The theory of masks developed by Wilde in his polemic *[The Decay of Lying](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Decay_of_Lying "The Decay of Lying")* can clearly be seen in Yeats's play *The Player Queen*, while the more sensual characterisation of Salomé, in Wilde's [play of the same name](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salome_(play) "Salome (play)"), provides the template for the changes Yeats made in his later plays, especially in *[On Baile's Strand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_Baile's_Strand "On Baile's Strand")* (1904), *Deirdre* (1907), and his dance play *The King of the Great Clock Tower* (1934).

### Mysticism and occult

Yeats had a lifelong interest in mysticism, [spiritualism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spiritualism_(beliefs) "Spiritualism (beliefs)"), [occultism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occultism "Occultism") and [astrology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrology "Astrology"). He read extensively on the subjects throughout his life, became a member of the [paranormal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paranormal "Paranormal") research organisation "[The Ghost Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Ghost_Club "The Ghost Club")" (in 1911) and was influenced by the writings of [Emanuel Swedenborg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emanuel_Swedenborg "Emanuel Swedenborg"). In 1892 Yeats wrote: "If I had not made magic my constant study I could not have written a single word of my Blake book, nor would *The Countess Kathleen* ever have come to exist. The mystical life is the centre of all that I do and all that I think and all that I write." His mystical interests—also inspired by a study of Hinduism, under the [Theosophist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theosophy_(Blavatskian) "Theosophy (Blavatskian)") [Mohini Chatterjee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mohini_Mohun_Chatterji "Mohini Mohun Chatterji"), and the occult—formed much of the basis of his late poetry. Some critics disparaged this aspect of Yeats's work.

During 1885, Yeats was involved in the formation of the Dublin Hermetic Order. That year the Dublin Theosophical lodge was opened in conjunction with Brahmin [Mohini Chatterjee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mohini_Mohun_Chatterji "Mohini Mohun Chatterji"), who travelled from the [Theosophical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theosophy_(Blavatskian) "Theosophy (Blavatskian)") in London to lecture. Yeats attended his first [séance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Séance "Séance") the following year.

Yeats was admitted into the [Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermetic_Order_of_the_Golden_Dawn "Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn") in 1890 and took the [magical motto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magical_motto "Magical motto") **Daemon est Deus inversus** (abbreviated *D.E.D.I.)*—translated as 'Devil is God inverted'. He was an active recruiter for the sect's [Isis-Urania Temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isis-Urania_Temple "Isis-Urania Temple"), and brought in his uncle George Pollexfen, [Maud Gonne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maud_Gonne "Maud Gonne"), and [Florence Farr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florence_Farr "Florence Farr"). Although he reserved a distaste for abstract and dogmatic religions founded around personality cults, he was attracted to the type of people he met at the Golden Dawn. He became heavily involved with Theosophy and with the eclectic [Rosicrucianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosicrucianism "Rosicrucianism") of the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn. He was involved in the Order's power struggles, both with Farr and [Macgregor Mathers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macgregor_Mathers "Macgregor Mathers"), and was involved when Mathers sent [Aleister Crowley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aleister_Crowley "Aleister Crowley") to repossess Golden Dawn paraphernalia during the "Battle of Blythe Road". After the Golden Dawn ceased and splintered into various offshoots, Yeats remained with the [Stella Matutina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stella_Matutina "Stella Matutina") until 1921.

During séances held from 1912, a spirit calling itself "Leo Africanus" apparently claimed it was Yeats's *[Daemon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daemon_(mythology) "Daemon (mythology)")* or anti-self, inspiring some of the speculations in *Per Amica Silentia Lunae*.

### Early poems

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/62/Yeats_Boughton.jpg/250px-Yeats_Boughton.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Yeats_Boughton.jpg)

Yeats in 1903

Yeats first significant poem is "The Island of Statues", a fantasy work that takes [Edmund Spenser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmund_Spenser "Edmund Spenser") and Shelley as its poetic models. It was serialized in the *Dublin University Review*. Yeats wanted to include it in his first collection, but it was deemed too long and was never republished in his lifetime. Quinx Books first published the poem in its complete form in 2014. Yeats first solo publication was the pamphlet *Mosada: A Dramatic Poem* (1886), in a print run of 100 copies paid for by his father. This was followed by the collection *[The Wanderings of Oisin and Other Poems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Wanderings_of_Oisin_and_Other_Poems "The Wanderings of Oisin and Other Poems")* (1889), which arranged a series of verse that dated as far back as the mid-1880s. The long title poem contains, in the words of his biographer [R. F. Foster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R._F._Foster_(historian) "R. F. Foster (historian)"), "obscure Gaelic names, striking repetitions [and] an unremitting rhythm subtly varied as the poem proceeded through its three sections":

We rode in sorrow, with strong hounds three,
Bran, Sceolan, and Lomair,
On a morning misty and mild and fair.
The mist-drops hung on the fragrant trees,
And in the blossoms hung the bees.
We rode in sadness above Lough Lean,
For our best were dead on Gavra's green.

"The Wanderings of Oisin" is based on lyrics from the [Fenian Cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fenian_Cycle "Fenian Cycle") of [Irish mythology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_mythology "Irish mythology") and displays the influence of both Sir Samuel Ferguson and the [Pre-Raphaelite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Raphaelite "Pre-Raphaelite") poets. The poem took two years to complete and was one of the few works from this period that he did not disown in his maturity. *Oisin* introduces what was to become one of his most important themes: the appeal of a life of contemplation over a life of action. Following the work, Yeats never again attempted another long poem. His other early poems are meditations on the themes of love or mystical and esoteric subjects, and include *Poems* (1895), *The Secret Rose* (1897), and *The Wind Among the Reeds* (1899). The covers of these volumes were illustrated by Yeats's friend [Althea Gyles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Althea_Gyles "Althea Gyles").

In 1890 Yeats and [Ernest Rhys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_Rhys "Ernest Rhys") co-founded the [Rhymers' Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhymers'_Club "Rhymers' Club"), a group of London-based poets who met regularly in a Fleet Street tavern to recite their verse. Yeats later sought to mythologize the collective, calling it the "Tragic Generation" in his autobiography, and published two anthologies of the Rhymers' work, the first one in 1892 and the second one in 1894. He collaborated with [Edwin Ellis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edwin_Ellis_(poet) "Edwin Ellis (poet)") on the first complete edition of William Blake's works, in the process rediscovering a forgotten poem, "Vala, or, the Four Zoas".

## Maud Gonne

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/02/Maudgonne.jpg/250px-Maudgonne.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Maudgonne.jpg)

[Maud Gonne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maud_Gonne "Maud Gonne") (c. 1900)

In 1889, when he was 24, Yeats met Maud Gonne, a 23-year-old English heiress and ardent Irish nationalist. She was eighteen months younger than Yeats and later claimed she met the poet as a "paint-stained art student." Gonne admired "The Island of Statues" and sought out his acquaintance. Yeats began an obsessive infatuation, and she had a significant and lasting effect on his poetry and his life thereafter. In later years he admitted, "it seems to me that she [Gonne] brought into my life those days—for as yet I saw only what lay upon the surface—the middle of the tint, a sound as of a Burmese gong, an over-powering tumult that had yet many pleasant secondary notes." Yeats's love was unrequited, in part due to his reluctance to participate in her nationalist activism.

In 1891 he visited Gonne in Ireland and proposed marriage, but was rejected. He later admitted that from that point "the troubling of my life began". Yeats proposed to Gonne three more times: in 1899, 1900 and 1901. She refused each proposal, and in 1903, to his dismay, married the Irish nationalist [Major John MacBride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_MacBride "John MacBride"). His only other love affair during this period was with [Olivia Shakespear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olivia_Shakespear "Olivia Shakespear"), whom he first met in 1894, and parted from in 1897.

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/24/WB_Yeats_nd.jpg/250px-WB_Yeats_nd.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:WB_Yeats_nd.jpg)

W. B. Yeats (no date)

Yeats derided MacBride in letters and in poetry. He was horrified by Gonne's marriage, at losing his muse to another man; in addition, her conversion to Catholicism before marriage offended the Protestant/agnostic Yeats. He worried his muse would come under the influence of the priests and do their bidding.

Gonne's marriage to MacBride was a disaster. This pleased Yeats, as Gonne began to visit him in London. After the birth of her son, [Seán MacBride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seán_MacBride "Seán MacBride"), in 1904, Gonne and MacBride agreed to end the marriage, although they were unable to agree on the child's welfare. Despite the use of intermediaries, a divorce case ensued in Paris in 1905. Gonne made a series of allegations against her husband with Yeats as her main 'second', though he did not attend court or travel to France. A divorce was not granted, for the only accusation that held up in court was that MacBride had been drunk once during the marriage. A separation was granted, with Gonne having custody of the baby and MacBride having visiting rights.

In 1895, Yeats moved into number 5 [Woburn Walk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woburn_Walk "Woburn Walk") and resided there until 1919.

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/81/Portrait_drawing_of_WB_Yeats_by_JS_Sargent.jpg/250px-Portrait_drawing_of_WB_Yeats_by_JS_Sargent.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Portrait_drawing_of_WB_Yeats_by_JS_Sargent.jpg)

Charcoal portrait of Yeats by [John Singer Sargent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Singer_Sargent "John Singer Sargent") (1908)

Yeats's friendship with Gonne ended, yet, in Paris in 1908, they finally consummated their relationship. "The long years of fidelity rewarded at last" was how another of his lovers described the event. Yeats was less sentimental and later remarked that "the tragedy of sexual intercourse is the perpetual virginity of the soul." The relationship did not develop into a new phase after their night together, and soon afterwards Gonne wrote to the poet indicating that despite the physical consummation, they could not continue as they had been: "I have prayed so hard to have all earthly desire taken from my love for you and dearest, loving you as I do, I have prayed and I am praying still that the bodily desire for me may be taken from you too." By January 1909, Gonne was sending Yeats letters praising the advantage given to artists who abstain from sex. Nearly twenty years later, Yeats recalled their night in [Helenic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helen_of_Troy "Helen of Troy") terms in "A Man Young and Old":

My arms are like the twisted thorn
And yet there beauty lay;
The first of all the tribe lay there
And did such pleasure take;
She who had brought great [Hector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hector "Hector") down
And put all [Troy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Troy "Troy") to wreck.

In 1896, Yeats was introduced to [Lady Gregory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lady_Gregory "Lady Gregory") by their mutual friend [Edward Martyn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Martyn "Edward Martyn"). Gregory encouraged Yeats's nationalism and convinced him to continue focusing on writing drama. Although he was influenced by French [Symbolism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbolism_(arts) "Symbolism (arts)"), Yeats concentrated on an identifiably Irish content and this inclination was reinforced by his involvement with a new generation of younger and emerging Irish authors. Together with Lady Gregory, Martyn, and other writers including [J. M. Synge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Millington_Synge "John Millington Synge"), [Seán O'Casey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seán_O'Casey "Seán O'Casey"), and [Padraic Colum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Padraic_Colum "Padraic Colum"), Yeats was a founding member of the [Irish Literary Revival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Literary_Revival "Irish Literary Revival") movement. Apart from these creative writers, much of the impetus for the Revival came from the work of scholarly translators who were aiding in the discovery of both the ancient sagas and Ossianic poetry and the more recent folk song tradition in Irish. One of the most significant of these was [Douglas Hyde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Douglas_Hyde "Douglas Hyde"), later the first President of Ireland, whose *Love Songs of Connacht* was widely admired.

## Abbey Theatre

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/ae/WBYeats1908.jpg/250px-WBYeats1908.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:WBYeats1908.jpg)

Yeats photographed in 1908 by [Alvin Langdon Coburn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alvin_Langdon_Coburn "Alvin Langdon Coburn")

In 1899, Yeats, Lady Gregory, Edward Martyn and [George Moore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Moore_(novelist) "George Moore (novelist)") founded the [Irish Literary Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Literary_Theatre "Irish Literary Theatre") to promote Irish plays. The ideals of the Abbey were derived from the avant-garde French theatre, which sought to express the "ascendancy of the playwright rather than the actor-manager *à l'anglais*". The group's manifesto, which Yeats wrote, declared, "We hope to find in Ireland an uncorrupted & imaginative audience trained to listen by its passion for oratory ... & that freedom to experiment which is not found in the theatres of England, & without which no new movement in art or literature can succeed." Yeats's interest in the classics and his defiance of English censorship were also fueled by a tour of America he took between 1903 and 1904. Stopping to deliver a lecture at the [University of Notre Dame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Notre_Dame "University of Notre Dame"), he learned about the student production of the *[Oedipus Rex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oedipus_Rex "Oedipus Rex")*. This play was banned in England, an act he viewed as hypocritical and denounced as part of 'British Puritanism'. He contrasted this with the artistic freedom of the Catholicism found at Notre Dame, which had allowed such a play with themes such as incest and patricide. He desired to stage a production of the *Oedipus Rex* in Dublin.

The collective survived for about two years but was unsuccessful. Working with the Irish brothers with theatrical experience, [William](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Fay "William Fay") and [Frank Fay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Fay_(Irish_actor) "Frank Fay (Irish actor)"), Yeats's unpaid but independently wealthy secretary [Annie Horniman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annie_Horniman "Annie Horniman"), and the leading [West End](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_End_theatre "West End theatre") actress [Florence Farr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florence_Farr "Florence Farr"), the group established the [Irish National Theatre Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_National_Theatre_Society "Irish National Theatre Society"). Along with Synge, they acquired property in Dublin and on 27 December 1904 opened the [Abbey Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbey_Theatre "Abbey Theatre"). Yeats's play *[Cathleen ni Houlihan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathleen_ni_Houlihan_(play) "Cathleen ni Houlihan (play)")* and Lady Gregory's *Spreading the News* were featured on the opening night. Yeats remained involved with the Abbey until his death, both as a member of the board and a prolific playwright. In 1902, he helped set up the [Dun Emer Press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dun_Emer_Press "Dun Emer Press") to publish work by writers associated with the Revival. This became the [Cuala Press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuala_Press "Cuala Press") in 1904, and inspired by the Arts and Crafts Movement, sought to "find work for Irish hands in the making of beautiful things." From then until its closure in 1946, the press—which was run by the poet's sisters—produced over 70 titles; 48 of them books by Yeats himself.

Yeats met the American poet [Ezra Pound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ezra_Pound "Ezra Pound") in 1909. Pound had travelled to London at least partly to meet the older man, whom he considered "the only poet worthy of serious study." From 1913 until 1916, the two men wintered in the Stone Cottage at [Ashdown Forest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashdown_Forest "Ashdown Forest"), with Pound nominally acting as Yeats's secretary. The relationship got off to a rocky start when Pound arranged for the publication in the magazine *[Poetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poetry_(magazine) "Poetry (magazine)")* of some of Yeats's verse with Pound's own unauthorised alterations. These changes reflected Pound's distaste for Victorian prosody. A more indirect influence was the scholarship on Japanese [Noh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noh "Noh") plays that Pound had obtained from [Ernest Fenollosa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_Fenollosa "Ernest Fenollosa")'s widow, which provided Yeats with a model for the aristocratic drama he intended to write. The first of his plays modelled on Noh was *At the Hawk's Well*, the first draft of which he dictated to Pound in January 1916.

The emergence of a nationalist revolutionary movement by the Catholic lower-middle and working class made Yeats reassess some of his attitudes. In the refrain of "[Easter, 1916](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Easter,_1916 "Easter, 1916")" ("All changed, changed utterly / A terrible beauty is born"), he faces his own failure to recognise the merits of the leaders of the [Easter Rising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Easter_Rising "Easter Rising"), due to his attitude towards their lowly backgrounds. Yeats was close to Lady Gregory and her home place of [Coole Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coole_Park "Coole Park"), County Galway. He would often visit and stay there as it was a central meeting place for people who supported the resurgence of Irish literature and cultural traditions. His poem "[The Wild Swans at Coole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Wild_Swans_at_Coole_(poem) "The Wild Swans at Coole (poem)")" was written there, between 1916 and 1917.

He wrote prefaces for two books of Irish mythological tales, compiled by Lady Gregory: *[Cuchulain of Muirthemne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuchulain_of_Muirthemne "Cuchulain of Muirthemne")* (1902), and *[Gods and Fighting Men](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gods_and_Fighting_Men "Gods and Fighting Men")* (1904). In the preface of the latter, he wrote: "One must not expect in these stories the epic lineaments, the many incidents, woven into one great event of, let us say the War for the [Brown Bull of Cuailgne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donn_Cuailnge "Donn Cuailnge") or that of the last gathering at Muirthemne."

## Politics

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e5/W._B._Yeats_1922.jpg/250px-W._B._Yeats_1922.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:W._B._Yeats_1922.jpg)

Yeats in Dublin on 12 December 1922, at the start of his term as member of the [Seanad Eireann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seanad_Éireann_(Irish_Free_State) "Seanad Éireann (Irish Free State)")

Yeats was an [Irish nationalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_nationalist "Irish nationalist"), who sought a kind of traditional lifestyle articulated through poems such as "The Fisherman". In the earlier part of his life, Yeats was a member of the [Irish Republican Brotherhood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Republican_Brotherhood "Irish Republican Brotherhood"). As his life progressed, he distanced himself from the [intense political landscape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_War_of_Independence "Irish War of Independence") until [1922](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Civil_War "Irish Civil War"), when he was appointed Senator for the [Irish Free State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Free_State "Irish Free State").

In the 1930s, Yeats was fascinated with the authoritarian, anti-democratic, nationalist movements of Europe, and he composed several marching songs for the [Blueshirts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blueshirts "Blueshirts"), although they were never used. He was a fierce opponent of individualism and political liberalism and saw the fascist movements as a triumph of public order and the needs of the national collective over petty individualism. He was an elitist who abhorred the idea of mob-rule, and saw democracy as a threat to good governance and public order. After the Blueshirt movement began to falter in Ireland, he distanced himself somewhat from his previous views, but maintained a preference for authoritarian and nationalist leadership.

## Later years

### Marriage to Georgie Hyde-Lees

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/43/Walter_de_la_Mare%2C_Bertha_Georgie_Yeats_%28n%C3%A9e_Hyde-Lees%29%2C_William_Butler_Yeats%2C_unknown_woman_by_Lady_Ottoline_Morrell.jpg/330px-Walter_de_la_Mare%2C_Bertha_Georgie_Yeats_%28n%C3%A9e_Hyde-Lees%29%2C_William_Butler_Yeats%2C_unknown_woman_by_Lady_Ottoline_Morrell.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Walter_de_la_Mare,_Bertha_Georgie_Yeats_(née_Hyde-Lees),_William_Butler_Yeats,_unknown_woman_by_Lady_Ottoline_Morrell.jpg)

[Walter de la Mare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_de_la_Mare "Walter de la Mare"), Bertha Georgie Yeats (née Hyde-Lees), William Butler Yeats, unknown woman, summer 1930; photo by [Lady Ottoline Morrell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lady_Ottoline_Morrell "Lady Ottoline Morrell")

By 1916, Yeats was 51 years old and determined to marry and produce an heir. His rival, [John MacBride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_MacBride "John MacBride"), had been executed for his role in the 1916 [Easter Rising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Easter_Rising "Easter Rising"), so Yeats hoped that his widow, [Maud Gonne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maud_Gonne "Maud Gonne"), might remarry. His final proposal to Gonne took place in mid-1916. Gonne's history of revolutionary political activism, as well as a series of personal catastrophes in the previous few years of her life—including [chloroform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloroform "Chloroform") addiction and her troubled marriage to MacBride—made her a potentially unsuitable wife; biographer R. F. Foster has observed that Yeats's last offer was motivated more by a sense of duty than by a genuine desire to marry her.

Yeats proposed in an indifferent manner, with conditions attached, and he both expected and hoped she would turn him down. According to Foster, "when he duly asked Maud to marry him and was duly refused, his thoughts shifted with surprising speed to her daughter." [Iseult Gonne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iseult_Gonne "Iseult Gonne") was Maud's second child with [Lucien Millevoye](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucien_Millevoye "Lucien Millevoye"), and at the time was twenty-one years old. She had lived a sad life to this point; conceived as an attempt to reincarnate her short-lived brother, for the first few years of her life she was presented as her mother's adopted niece. When Maud told her that she was going to marry, Iseult cried and told her mother that she hated MacBride. When Gonne took action to divorce MacBride in 1905, the court heard allegations that he had sexually assaulted Iseult, then eleven. At fifteen, she proposed to Yeats. In 1917, he proposed to Iseult but was rejected.

That September, Yeats proposed to 25-year-old [Georgie Hyde-Lees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georgie_Hyde-Lees "Georgie Hyde-Lees") (1892–1968), known as George, whom he had met through [Olivia Shakespear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olivia_Shakespear "Olivia Shakespear"). Despite warnings from her friends—"George ... you can't. He must be dead"—Hyde-Lees accepted, and the two were married on 20 October 1917. Their marriage was a success, in spite of the age difference, and in spite of Yeats's feelings of remorse and regret during their honeymoon. The couple went on to have two children, [Anne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anne_Yeats "Anne Yeats") and [Michael](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Yeats "Michael Yeats"). Although in later years he had romantic relationships with other women, Georgie herself wrote to her husband, "When you are dead, people will talk about your love affairs, but I shall say nothing, for I will remember how proud you were."

During the first years of marriage, they experimented with [automatic writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automatic_writing "Automatic writing"); she contacted a variety of spirits and guides they called "Instructors" while in a trance. The spirits allegedly communicated a complex and esoteric system of philosophy and history, which the couple developed into an exposition using geometrical shapes: phases, cones, and gyres. Yeats devoted much time to preparing this material for publication as *[A Vision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Vision "A Vision")* (1925). In 1924, he wrote to his publisher T. Werner Laurie, admitting, "I dare say I delude myself in thinking this book my book of books."

### Nobel Prize

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4a/Yeats1923.jpg/250px-Yeats1923.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Yeats1923.jpg)

Yeats photographed in 1923

In December 1923, Yeats was awarded the [Nobel Prize in Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Literature "Nobel Prize in Literature") "for his always inspired poetry, which in a highly artistic form gives expression to the spirit of a whole nation". Politically aware, he knew the symbolic value of an Irish winner so soon after Ireland had gained independence, and highlighted the fact at each available opportunity. His reply to many of the letters of congratulations sent to him contained the words: "I consider that this honour has come to me less as an individual than as a representative of Irish literature, it is part of Europe's welcome to the Free State."

Yeats used the occasion of his acceptance lecture at the [Royal Academy of Sweden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swedish_Academy "Swedish Academy") to present himself as a standard-bearer of Irish nationalism and Irish cultural independence. As he remarked, "The theatres of Dublin were empty buildings hired by the English travelling companies, and we wanted Irish plays and Irish players. When we thought of these plays we thought of everything that was romantic and poetical because the nationalism we had called up—the nationalism every generation had called up in moments of discouragement—was romantic and poetical." The prize led to a significant increase in the sales of his books, as his publishers [Macmillan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macmillan_Publishers "Macmillan Publishers") sought to capitalise on the publicity. For the first time he had money, and he was able to repay not only his own debts but those of his father.

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/df/Bust_of_William_Butler_Yeats_at_Sandymount_Green_Dublin_-129268_%2835015415746%29.jpg/250px-Bust_of_William_Butler_Yeats_at_Sandymount_Green_Dublin_-129268_%2835015415746%29.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Bust_of_William_Butler_Yeats_at_Sandymount_Green_Dublin_-129268_(35015415746).jpg)

Bust of Yeats at [Sandymount Green](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sandymount "Sandymount"), Dublin

## Later life and death

By early 1925, Yeats's health had stabilised and he had completed most of the writing for *[A Vision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Vision "A Vision")*. Although it was dated 1925, it was first published in January 1926, when he almost immediately started rewriting it for a second version. He had been appointed to the first [Irish Senate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seanad_Éireann "Seanad Éireann") in 1922, and re-appointed for a second term in 1925. Early in his tenure, a debate on divorce arose, and Yeats viewed the issue as primarily a confrontation between the emerging Catholic ethos and the Protestant minority. When the Catholic Church weighed in with a blanket refusal to consider their anti position, *[The Irish Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Irish_Times "The Irish Times")* countered that a measure to outlaw divorce would alienate Protestants and "crystallise" the [partition of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partition_of_Ireland "Partition of Ireland"). In response, Yeats delivered a series of speeches that attacked the "quixotically impressive" ambitions of the government and clergy, likening their campaign tactics to those of "medieval Spain". "Marriage is not to us a Sacrament, but, upon the other hand, the love of a man and woman, and the inseparable physical desire, are sacred. This conviction has come to us through ancient philosophy and modern literature, and it seems to us a most sacrilegious thing to persuade two people who hate each other... to live together, and it is to us no remedy to permit them to part if neither can re-marry." The resulting debate has been described as one of Yeats's "supreme public moments", and began his ideological move away from [pluralism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pluralism_(political_philosophy) "Pluralism (political philosophy)") towards religious confrontation.

His language became more forceful; the Jesuit Father Peter Finlay was described by Yeats as a man of "monstrous discourtesy", and he lamented that "It is one of the glories of the Church in which I was born that we have put our Bishops in their place in discussions requiring legislation." During his time in the Senate, Yeats further warned his colleagues, "If you show that this country, southern Ireland, is going to be governed by Roman Catholic ideas and by Catholic ideas alone, you will never get the [North](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Ireland "Northern Ireland")... You will put a wedge in the midst of this nation." He memorably said of his fellow Irish Protestants, "we are no petty people".

In 1924 he chaired a coinage committee charged with selecting designs for the [first currency of the Irish Free State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coins_of_the_Republic_of_Ireland#Pre-decimal_coins "Coins of the Republic of Ireland"). Aware of the symbolic power latent in the imagery of a young state's currency, he sought a form that was "elegant, racy of the soil, and utterly unpolitical". When the house finally decided on the artwork of [Percy Metcalfe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Percy_Metcalfe "Percy Metcalfe"), Yeats was pleased, though he regretted that compromise had led to "lost muscular tension" in the finally depicted images. He retired from the Senate in 1928 because of ill health.

Towards the end of his life—and especially after the [Wall Street crash of 1929](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wall_Street_crash_of_1929 "Wall Street crash of 1929") and [Great Depression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Depression "Great Depression"), which led some to question whether democracy could cope with deep economic difficulty—Yeats seems to have returned to his aristocratic sympathies. During the aftermath of the First World War, he became sceptical about the efficacy of democratic government, and anticipated political reconstruction in Europe through totalitarian rule. His later association with Pound drew him towards [Benito Mussolini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benito_Mussolini "Benito Mussolini"), for whom he expressed admiration on a number of occasions.

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/bb/William_Butler_Yeats.jpg/250px-William_Butler_Yeats.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:William_Butler_Yeats.jpg)

William Butler Yeats, 1933; photo by [Pirie MacDonald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pirie_MacDonald "Pirie MacDonald") ([Library of Congress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Library_of_Congress "Library of Congress"))

At the age of 68 he was 'rejuvenated' by the [Steinach operation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugen_Steinach "Eugen Steinach") which was performed on 6 April 1934 by [Norman Haire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_Haire "Norman Haire"). For the last five years of his life Yeats found a new vigour evident from both his poetry and his intimate relations with younger women. During this time, Yeats was involved in a number of romantic affairs with, among others, the poet and actress [Margot Ruddock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Margot_Ruddock "Margot Ruddock") and the novelist, journalist and sexual radical [Ethel Mannin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethel_Mannin "Ethel Mannin"). As in his earlier life, Yeats found erotic adventure conducive to his creative energy, and despite age and ill-health he remained a prolific writer. In a 1935 letter he noted: "I find my present weakness made worse by the strange second puberty the operation has given me, the ferment that has come upon my imagination. If I write poetry it will be unlike anything I have done." In 1936, he undertook editorship of the *[Oxford Book of Modern Verse, 1892–1935](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford_Book_of_Modern_Verse_1892–1935 "Oxford Book of Modern Verse 1892–1935")*. From 1935 to 1936 he travelled to the [Western Mediterranean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mediterranean_Sea "Mediterranean Sea") island of [Mallorca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mallorca "Mallorca") with [Indian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_people "Indian people")-born [Shri Purohit Swami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shri_Purohit_Swami "Shri Purohit Swami") and from there the two of them performed the majority of the work in translating the [principal Upanishads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principal_Upanishads "Principal Upanishads") from [Sanskrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit "Sanskrit") into common English; the resulting work, *[The Ten Principal Upanishads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Ten_Principal_Upanishads "The Ten Principal Upanishads")*, was published in 1938.

### Death

Yeats died at the Hôtel Idéal Beauséjour in [Roquebrune-Cap-Martin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roquebrune-Cap-Martin "Roquebrune-Cap-Martin"), near [Menton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Menton "Menton"), France, on 28 January 1939, aged 73. The cause of his death was heart failure. He was buried after a discreet and private funeral at Roquebrune. Attempts had been made at Roquebrune to dissuade the family from proceeding with the removal of the remains to Ireland due to the uncertainty of their identity. His body had earlier been exhumed and transferred to the [ossuary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ossuary "Ossuary"). Yeats and his wife, George, had often discussed his death, and his express wish was that he be buried quickly in France with a minimum of fuss. According to George, "His actual words were 'If I die, bury me up there [at Roquebrune] and then in a year's time when the newspapers have forgotten me, dig me up and plant me in Sligo.'" In September 1948, Yeats's body was moved to the churchyard of [St Columba's Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Columba's_Church,_Drumcliff "St Columba's Church, Drumcliff"), [Drumcliff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drumcliff "Drumcliff"), County Sligo, on the Irish Naval Service [corvette](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flower-class_corvette "Flower-class corvette") [*LÉ Macha*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LÉ_Macha_(01) "LÉ Macha (01)"). The person in charge of this operation for the Irish Government was [Seán MacBride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seán_MacBride "Seán MacBride"), son of Maud Gonne MacBride, and then [Minister of External Affairs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_for_Foreign_Affairs_and_Trade "Minister for Foreign Affairs and Trade").

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/0b/WB_Yeats_Grave_Drumcliffe.jpg/250px-WB_Yeats_Grave_Drumcliffe.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:WB_Yeats_Grave_Drumcliffe.jpg)

Yeats's final resting place in the shadow of the [Dartry Mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dartry_Mountains "Dartry Mountains"), [County Sligo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Sligo "County Sligo")

His epitaph is taken from the last lines of "[Under Ben Bulben](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Under_Ben_Bulben "Under Ben Bulben")", one of his final poems:

Cast a cold Eye
On Life, on Death.
Horseman, pass by!

The French ambassador [Stanislas Ostroróg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanislas_Ostroróg "Stanislas Ostroróg") was involved in returning Yeats's remains to Ireland in 1948; in a letter to the European director of the Foreign Ministry in Paris, "Ostrorog tells how Yeats's son Michael sought official help in locating the poet's remains. Neither Michael Yeats nor Seán MacBride, the Irish foreign minister who organised the ceremony, wanted to know the details of how the remains were collected, Ostrorog notes. He repeatedly urges caution and discretion and says the Irish ambassador in Paris should not be informed." Yeats's body was exhumed in 1946 and the remains were moved to an ossuary and mixed with other remains. The French Foreign Ministry authorized Ostrorog to secretly cover the cost of repatriation from his slush fund. Authorities were worried about the fact that the much-loved poet's remains were thrown into a communal grave, causing embarrassment for both Ireland and France. Per a letter from Ostroróg to his superiors, "Mr Rebouillat, (a) forensic doctor in Roquebrune would be able to reconstitute a skeleton presenting all the characteristics of the deceased."

## Style

Yeats is considered one of the key 20th-century English-language poets. He is a [Symbolist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbolism_(movement) "Symbolism (movement)") poet, using allusive imagery and symbolic structures throughout his career. He chose words and assembled them so that, in addition to a particular meaning, they suggest abstract thoughts that may seem more significant and resonant. His use of symbols is usually something physical that is both itself and a suggestion of other, perhaps immaterial, timeless qualities.

Unlike the [modernists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modernism "Modernism") who experimented with [free verse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_verse "Free verse"), Yeats was a master of the traditional forms. The impact of modernism on his work can be seen in the increasing abandonment of the more conventionally poetic diction of his early work in favour of the more austere language and more direct approach to his themes that increasingly characterises the poetry and plays of his middle period, comprising the volumes *In the Seven Woods*, *Responsibilities* and *The Green Helmet*. His later poetry and plays are written in a more personal vein, and the works written in the last twenty years of his life include mention of his son and daughter, as well as meditations on the experience of growing old. In his poem "[The Circus Animals' Desertion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Circus_Animals'_Desertion "The Circus Animals' Desertion")", he describes the inspiration for these late works:

Now that my ladder's gone
I must lie down where all the ladders start
In the foul [rag and bone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rag_and_bone "Rag and bone") shop of the heart.

During 1929, he stayed at [Thoor Ballylee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thoor_Ballylee "Thoor Ballylee") near [Gort](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gort "Gort") in [County Galway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Galway "County Galway") (where Yeats had his summer home since 1919) for the last time. Much of the remainder of his life was lived outside Ireland, although he did lease [Riversdale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riversdale,_Rathfarnham "Riversdale, Rathfarnham") house in the Dublin suburb of [Rathfarnham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rathfarnham "Rathfarnham") in 1932. He wrote prolifically through his final years, and published poetry, plays, and prose. In 1938, he attended the Abbey for the final time to see the premiere of his play *[Purgatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purgatory_(drama) "Purgatory (drama)")*. His *Autobiographies of William Butler Yeats* was published that same year. The preface for the English translation of [Rabindranath Tagore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rabindranath_Tagore "Rabindranath Tagore")'s *[Gitanjali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gitanjali "Gitanjali")* (*[Song Offering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Song_Offering "Song Offering")*) (for which Tagore won the Nobel prize in Literature) was written by Yeats in 1913.

While Yeats's early poetry drew heavily on [Irish myth and folklore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_mythology "Irish mythology"), his later work was engaged with more contemporary issues, and his style underwent a dramatic transformation. His work can be divided into three general periods. The early poems are lushly pre-Raphaelite in tone, self-consciously ornate, and, at times, according to unsympathetic critics, stilted. Yeats began by writing epic poems such as *The Isle of Statues* and *[The Wanderings of Oisin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Wanderings_of_Oisin "The Wanderings of Oisin")*. His other early poems are lyrics on the themes of love or mystical and esoteric subjects. Yeats's middle period saw him abandon the pre-Raphaelite character of his early work and attempt to turn himself into a [Landor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Savage_Landor "Walter Savage Landor")-style social ironist.

Yeats's late work was informed by his later interest in spiritualism. In many ways, this poetry is a return to the vision of his earlier work. The opposition between the worldly-minded man of the sword and the spiritually minded man of God, the theme of *The Wanderings of Oisin*, is reproduced in *A Dialogue Between Self and Soul*.

Some critics hold that Yeats spanned the transition from the 19th century into 20th-century modernism in poetry much as [Pablo Picasso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pablo_Picasso "Pablo Picasso") did in painting; others question whether late Yeats has much in common with modernists of the [Ezra Pound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ezra_Pound "Ezra Pound") and [T. S. Eliot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T._S._Eliot "T. S. Eliot") variety.

Modernists read the well-known poem "[The Second Coming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Second_Coming_(poem) "The Second Coming (poem)")" as a dirge for the decline of European civilisation, but it also expresses Yeats's apocalyptic mystical theories and is shaped by the 1890s. His most important collections of poetry started with *The Green Helmet* (1910) and *Responsibilities* (1914). In imagery, Yeats's poetry became sparer and more powerful as he grew older. *The Tower* (1928), *The Winding Stair* (1933), and *New Poems* (1938) contained some of the most potent images in 20th-century poetry.

Yeats's mystical inclinations towards Hinduism, [theosophical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theosophy_(Blavatskian) "Theosophy (Blavatskian)") and the [occult](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occult "Occult") informed his late poetry, which some critics have judged as lacking in intellectual credibility. The metaphysics of Yeats's late works must be read in relation to his system of esoteric fundamentals in *[A Vision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Vision "A Vision")* (1925).

## Legacy

Yeats is commemorated in Sligo town by a statue, sculpted by [Rowan Gillespie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rowan_Gillespie "Rowan Gillespie") in 1989. On the 50th anniversary of the poet's death, it was erected outside the [Ulster Bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulster_Bank "Ulster Bank"). When receiving his Nobel Prize in Stockholm, Yeats had remarked on the similarities between that city's [Royal Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stockholm_Palace "Stockholm Palace") and the Ulster Bank. Across the river is the Yeats Memorial Building, which houses the Sligo Yeats Society. *[Standing Figure: Knife Edge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standing_Figure:_Knife_Edge "Standing Figure: Knife Edge")* by [Henry Moore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Moore "Henry Moore") is displayed in the W. B. Yeats Memorial Garden at [St Stephen's Green](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Stephen's_Green "St Stephen's Green") in Dublin.

A [blue plaque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_plaque "Blue plaque") dedicated to Yeats is at Balscadden House on the Balscadden Road in [Howth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Howth "Howth"), which was his cottage home from 1880 to 1883. In 1957 the [London County Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_County_Council "London County Council") erected a plaque at his former residence on 23 [Fitzroy Road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fitzroy_Road "Fitzroy Road"), Primrose Hill, London.
