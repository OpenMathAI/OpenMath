**Eugenio Montale** (Italian: [[euˈdʒɛːnjo monˈtaːle]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Italian "Help:IPA/Italian"); 12 October 1896 – 12 September 1981) was an Italian poet, prose writer, editor and translator. In 1975, he was awarded the [Nobel Prize in Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Literature "Nobel Prize in Literature") "for his distinctive poetry which, with great artistic sensitivity, has interpreted human values under the sign of an outlook on life with no illusions."

## Life and works

### Early years

Montale was born in [Genoa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genoa "Genoa"), the son of Giuseppina Ricci and Domenico Montale, a businessman who ran a chemical products company. Montale was the youngest of six children, including five brothers and a sister.

Montale attended elementary school in Genoa. The Montale family spent their summers at their villa in [Monterosso al Mare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monterosso_al_Mare "Monterosso al Mare"), and the landscapes of the [Ligurian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liguria "Liguria") region would go on to inspire his poetry. In 1911, he was enrolled at a technical college and graduated with a diploma in accountancy in 1915. In the same year, he began taking music lessons with baritone Ernesto Sivori. However, his time as an infantryman in World War I and the death of Sivori in 1923 caused him to abandon his career in music and turn to poetry instead.

Montale was largely self-taught. Growing up, his imagination was caught by several writers, including [Dante Alighieri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dante_Alighieri "Dante Alighieri"), and by the study of foreign languages (especially English).

### Poetic works

Montale wrote more than ten anthologies of short lyrics, a journal of poetry translation, plus several books of prose translations, two books of literary criticism, and one of fantasy prose. Alongside his imaginative work he was a constant contributor to Italy's most important newspaper, the [Corriere della Sera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corriere_della_Sera "Corriere della Sera"), for which he wrote a huge number of articles on literature, music, and art. He also wrote a foreword to Dante's "The Divine Comedy", in which he mentions the credibility of Dante, and his insight and unbiased imagination. In 1925 he was a signatory to the [Manifesto of the Anti-Fascist Intellectuals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manifesto_of_the_Anti-Fascist_Intellectuals "Manifesto of the Anti-Fascist Intellectuals"). Montale's own politics inclined toward the liberalism of [Piero Gobetti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piero_Gobetti "Piero Gobetti") and [Benedetto Croce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benedetto_Croce "Benedetto Croce"). He contributed to Gobetti's literary magazine *[Il Baretti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Il_Baretti "Il Baretti")*.

Montale's work, especially his first poetry collection *[Ossi di seppia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ossi_di_seppia?action=edit&redlink=1 "Ossi di seppia")* ("Cuttlefish Bones"), which appeared in 1925, shows him as an antifascist who felt detached from contemporary life and found solace and refuge in the solitude of nature.

### Anticonformism of the new poetry

Montale moved to [Florence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florence "Florence") in 1927 to work as an editor for the publisher [Bemporad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bemporad?action=edit&redlink=1 "Bemporad"). Florence was the cradle of Italian poetry of that age, with works like the *Canti orfici* by [Dino Campana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dino_Campana "Dino Campana") (1914) and the first lyrics by [Ungaretti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giuseppe_Ungaretti "Giuseppe Ungaretti") for the review *[Lacerba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lacerba "Lacerba")*. Other poets like [Umberto Saba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umberto_Saba "Umberto Saba") and [Vincenzo Cardarelli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vincenzo_Cardarelli "Vincenzo Cardarelli") had been highly praised. In 1929 Montale was asked to be chairman of the [Gabinetto Vieusseux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gabinetto_Vieusseux "Gabinetto Vieusseux") Library, a post from which he was expelled in 1938 by the fascist government. By this time Montale's poetry was a reaction against the literary style of the fascist regime. He collaborated with the magazine *[Solaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solaria_(magazine) "Solaria (magazine)")*, and (starting in 1927) frequented the literary café Le [Giubbe Rosse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giubbe_Rosse "Giubbe Rosse") ("Red Jackets") on the [Piazza Vittoria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piazza_della_Repubblica,_Florence "Piazza della Repubblica, Florence") (now Piazza della Repubblica). Visiting the café often several times a day, he became a central figure among a group of writers there, including [Carlo Emilio Gadda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carlo_Emilio_Gadda "Carlo Emilio Gadda"), [Arturo Loria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arturo_Loria?action=edit&redlink=1 "Arturo Loria") and [Elio Vittorini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elio_Vittorini "Elio Vittorini") (all founders of the magazine). He wrote for almost all the important literary magazines of the time.

Though hindered by financial problems and the literary and social conformism imposed by the authorities, in Florence, Montale published his finest anthology, *[Le occasioni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Le_occasioni?action=edit&redlink=1 "Le occasioni")* ("Occasions", 1939). From 1933 to 1938 he had a love relationship with [Irma Brandeis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irma_Brandeis "Irma Brandeis"), a Jewish-American scholar of Dante who occasionally visited Italy for short periods. After falling in love with Brandeis, Montale represented her as a [mediatrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mediatrix "Mediatrix") figure like Dante's [Beatrice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beatrice_Portinari "Beatrice Portinari"). *Le occasioni* contains numerous allusions to Brandeis, here called Clizia (a [senhal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senhal "Senhal")). [Franco Fortini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franco_Fortini "Franco Fortini") judged Montale's *Ossi di seppia* and *Le occasioni* the high-water mark of 20th century [Italian poetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_literature "Italian literature").

[T.S. Eliot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T.S._Eliot "T.S. Eliot"), who shared Montale's admiration for Dante, was an important influence on his poetry at this time; in fact, the new poems of Eliot were shown to Montale by [Mario Praz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mario_Praz "Mario Praz"), then teaching in Manchester. The concept of the [objective correlative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Objective_correlative "Objective correlative") used by Montale in his poetry, was probably influenced by T. S. Eliot. In 1948, for Eliot's sixtieth birthday, Montale contributed a celebratory essay titled "Eliot and Ourselves" to a collection published to mark the occasion.

### Disharmony with the world

From 1948 to his death, Montale lived in Milan. After the war, he was a member of the liberal [Partito d'Azione](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partito_d'Azione "Partito d'Azione"). As a contributor to the *Corriere della Sera* he was music editor and also reported from abroad, including Israel, where he went as a reporter to follow [Pope Paul VI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Paul_VI "Pope Paul VI")'s visit there. His works as a journalist are collected in *Fuori di casa* ("Out of Home", 1969).

*[La bufera e altro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_bufera_e_altro?action=edit&redlink=1 "La bufera e altro")* ("The Storm and Other Things") was published in 1956 and marks the end of Montale's most acclaimed poetry. Here his figure Clizia is joined by La Volpe ("the Fox"), based on the young poet [Maria Luisa Spaziani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maria_Luisa_Spaziani "Maria Luisa Spaziani") with whom Montale had an affair during the 1950s. However, this volume also features Clizia, treated in a variety of poems as a kind of bird-goddess who defies Hitler. These are some of his greatest poems.

His later works are *[Xenia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xenia_(poetry)?action=edit&redlink=1 "Xenia (poetry)")* (1966), *Satura* (1971) and *[Diario del '71 e del '72](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diario_del_'71_e_del_'72?action=edit&redlink=1 "Diario del '71 e del '72")* (1973). Montale's later poetry is wry and ironic, musing on the critical reaction to his earlier work and on the constantly changing world around him. *Satura* contains a poignant elegy to his wife [Drusilla Tanzi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drusilla_Tanzi "Drusilla Tanzi"). He also wrote a series of poignant poems about Clizia shortly before his death. Montale's fame at that point had extended throughout the world. He had received honorary degrees from the Universities of Milan (1961), [Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Cambridge "University of Cambridge") (1967), [Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Università_degli_Studi_di_Roma_La_Sapienza "Università degli Studi di Roma La Sapienza") (1974), and had been named [Senator-for-Life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senator_for_life "Senator for life") in the Italian Senate. In 1973 he was awarded the [Golden Wreath of the Struga Poetry Evenings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Struga_Poetry_Evenings "Struga Poetry Evenings") in [Struga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Struga "Struga"), [SR Macedonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialist_Republic_of_Macedonia "Socialist Republic of Macedonia"). In 1975 he received the Nobel Prize for Literature.

Montale died in Milan in 1981.

In 1996, a work appeared called *[Posthumous Diary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Posthumous_Diary "Posthumous Diary")* (*Diario postumo*) that purported to have been 'compiled' by Montale before his death, with the help of the young poet Annalisa Cima; the critic [Dante Isella](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dante_Isella?action=edit&redlink=1 "Dante Isella") thinks that this work is not authentic. [Joseph Brodsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Brodsky "Joseph Brodsky") dedicated his essay "In the Shadow of Dante" to Eugenio Montale's lyric poetry.
