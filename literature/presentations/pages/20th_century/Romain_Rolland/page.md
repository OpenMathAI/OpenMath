**Romain Rolland** (French: [[ʁɔmɛ̃ ʁɔlɑ̃]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/French "Help:IPA/French"); 29 January 1866 – 30 December 1944) was a French dramatist, novelist, essayist, [art historian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_historian "Art historian") and [mystic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mysticism "Mysticism") who was awarded the [Nobel Prize for Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_for_Literature "Nobel Prize for Literature") in 1915 "as a tribute to the lofty [idealism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idealism "Idealism") of his literary production and to the [sympathy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sympathy "Sympathy") and love of [truth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Truth "Truth") with which he has described different types of human beings".

He was an admirer of [Mahatma Gandhi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahatma_Gandhi "Mahatma Gandhi") and [Rabindranath Tagore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rabindranath_Tagore "Rabindranath Tagore"), wrote a still relevant biography of Gandhi, and is also noted for his correspondence with numerous writers and thinkers across the globe including [Maxim Gorki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxim_Gorki "Maxim Gorki"), [Rabindranath Tagore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rabindranath_Tagore "Rabindranath Tagore") and [Sigmund Freud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigmund_Freud "Sigmund Freud").

## Biography

Rolland was born in [Clamecy, Nièvre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clamecy,_Nièvre "Clamecy, Nièvre") into a family that had both wealthy townspeople and farmers in its lineage. Writing introspectively in his *Voyage intérieur* (1942), he sees himself as a representative of an "antique species". He would cast these ancestors in *Colas Breugnon* (1919).

Accepted to the [École normale supérieure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/École_normale_supérieure "École normale supérieure") in 1886, he first studied philosophy, but his independence of spirit led him to abandon that so as not to submit to the dominant [ideology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ideology "Ideology"). He received his degree in history in 1889 and spent two years in Rome, where his encounter with [Malwida von Meysenbug](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malwida_von_Meysenbug "Malwida von Meysenbug")–who had been a friend of [Nietzsche](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Nietzsche "Friedrich Nietzsche") and of [Wagner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Wagner "Richard Wagner")–and his discovery of Italian masterpieces were decisive for the development of his thought. When he returned to France in 1895, he received his doctoral degree with his thesis *Les origines du théâtre lyrique moderne. Histoire de l'opéra en Europe avant Lulli et Scarlatti* (*The origins of modern lyric theatre. A History of Opera in Europe before Lully and Scarlatti*). For the next two decades, he taught at various lycées in Paris before directing the newly established music school of the École des Hautes Études Sociales from 1902 to 1911. In 1903 he was appointed to the first chair of [music history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_history "Music history") at the [Sorbonne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Paris "University of Paris"), he also directed briefly in 1911 the musical section at the [French Institute in Florence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Institute_in_Florence "French Institute in Florence").

His first book was published in 1902 when he was 36 years old. Through his advocacy for a 'people's theatre', he made a significant contribution towards the [democratisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratisation "Democratisation") of the theatre. As a [humanist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humanist "Humanist"), he embraced the work of the [Indian philosophers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_philosophers "Indian philosophers") ("Conversations with [Rabindranath Tagore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rabindranath_Tagore "Rabindranath Tagore")" and [Mohandas Gandhi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mohandas_Gandhi "Mohandas Gandhi")). Rolland was strongly influenced by the [Vedanta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedanta "Vedanta") philosophy of India, primarily through the works of [Swami Vivekananda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swami_Vivekananda "Swami Vivekananda").

A demanding, yet timid, young man, he did not like teaching. He was not indifferent to youth: Jean-Christophe, Olivier and their friends, the heroes of his novels, are young people. But with real-life persons, youths as well as adults, Rolland maintained only a distant relationship. He was first and foremost a writer. Assured that literature would provide him with a modest income, he resigned from the university in 1912. In 1920, Rolland used the phrase, "Pessimism of the intellect, optimism of the will" in a review, which [Antonio Gramsci](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonio_Gramsci "Antonio Gramsci") adopted from him as a formula for intellectual perseverance during hard times.

Romain Rolland was a lifelong pacifist. He was one of the few major French writers to retain his [pacifist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pacifist "Pacifist") internationalist values; he moved to Switzerland. He protested against the first World War in *[Au-dessus de la mêlée](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Au-dessus_de_la_mêlée?action=edit&redlink=1 "Au-dessus de la mêlée")* (1915), *[Above the Battle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Above_the_Battle "Above the Battle")* (Chicago, 1916). In 1924, his book on [Gandhi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahatma_Gandhi "Mahatma Gandhi") contributed to the Indian [nonviolent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonviolent "Nonviolent") leader's reputation and the two men met in 1931. Rolland was a [vegetarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vegetarianism "Vegetarianism").

In May 1922 he attended the [International Congress of Progressive Artists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Congress_of_Progressive_Artists "International Congress of Progressive Artists") and signed the "Founding Proclamation of the Union of Progressive International Artists".

In 1928 Rolland and Hungarian scholar, philosopher and natural living experimenter [Edmund Bordeaux Szekely](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmund_Bordeaux_Szekely "Edmund Bordeaux Szekely") founded the International Biogenic Society to promote and expand on their ideas of the integration of mind, body and spirit.
In 1932 Rolland was among the first members of the [World Committee Against War and Fascism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Committee_Against_War_and_Fascism "World Committee Against War and Fascism"), organized by [Willi Münzenberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Willi_Münzenberg "Willi Münzenberg"). Rolland criticized the control Münzenberg assumed over the committee and was against it being based in [Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berlin "Berlin").

Rolland moved to [Villeneuve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Villeneuve,_Vaud "Villeneuve, Vaud"), on the shores of [Lake Geneva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Geneva "Lake Geneva") to devote himself to writing. His life was interrupted by health problems, and by travels to art exhibitions. His visit to Moscow (1935), on the invitation of [Maxim Gorky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxim_Gorky "Maxim Gorky"), was an opportunity to meet [Joseph Stalin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Stalin "Joseph Stalin"), whom he considered the greatest man of his time. Rolland served unofficially as ambassador of French artists to the [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union "Soviet Union"). Although he admired Stalin, he attempted to intervene against the persecution of his friends. He attempted to discuss his concerns with Stalin, and was involved in the campaign for the release of the [Left Opposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Left_Opposition "Left Opposition") activist and writer [Victor Serge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victor_Serge "Victor Serge") and wrote to Stalin begging clemency for [Nikolai Bukharin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nikolai_Bukharin "Nikolai Bukharin"). During Serge's imprisonment (1933–1936), Rolland had agreed to handle the publications of Serge's writings in France, despite their political disagreements.

In 1937, he came back to live in [Vézelay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vézelay "Vézelay"), which, in 1940, was occupied by the Germans. During the occupation, he isolated himself in complete solitude. Never stopping his work, in 1940, he finished his memoirs. He also placed the finishing touches on his musical research on the life of [Ludwig van Beethoven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludwig_van_Beethoven "Ludwig van Beethoven"). Shortly before his death, he wrote *[Péguy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Péguy "Péguy")* (1944), in which he examines religion and socialism through the context of his memories. He died on 30 December 1944 in Vézelay.

In 1921, his close friend the Austrian writer [Stefan Zweig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stefan_Zweig "Stefan Zweig") published his biography (in English *Romain Rolland: The Man and His Works*). Zweig profoundly admired Rolland, whom he once described as "the moral consciousness of Europe" during the years of turmoil and War in Europe. Zweig wrote at length about his friendship with Rolland in his own autobiography (in English *[The World of Yesterday](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_World_of_Yesterday "The World of Yesterday")*), discussing, for example, their failed efforts to organize a conference of antiwar intellectuals from both warring camps in neutral Switzerland.

Victor Serge was appreciative of Rolland's interventions on his behalf but ultimately thoroughly disappointed by Rolland's refusal to break publicly with Stalin and the repressive Soviet regime. The entry for 4 May 1945, a few weeks after Rolland's death, in Serge's *Notebooks: 1936-1947* notes acidly that "At age seventy the author of *Jean-Christophe* allowed himself to be covered with the blood spilled by a tyranny of which he was a faithful adulator." However, this is completely denied by Romain Rolland's biographer Bernard Duchatelet in his French biography *[Romain Rolland: Tel qu'en lui-même](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romain_Rolland:_Tel_qu'en_lui-même?action=edit&redlink=1 "Romain Rolland: Tel qu'en lui-même")*. Duchatelet and other Rollandians believe that Rolland remained faithful to his own well-known integrity.

Rolland's life was 'the story of a conscience', as mentioned in the title of the book on him by [Alex Aronson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alex_Aronson "Alex Aronson").

[Hermann Hesse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann_Hesse "Hermann Hesse") dedicated *[Siddhartha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siddhartha_(novel) "Siddhartha (novel)")* to Romain Rolland "my dear friend".

## People's theatre

Rolland's most significant contribution to the theatre lies in his advocacy for a "popular theatre" in his essay *The People's Theatre* (*Le Théâtre du peuple*, 1902). "There is only one necessary condition for the emergence of a new theatre", he wrote, "that the stage and [auditorium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auditorium "Auditorium") should be open to the masses, should be able to contain a people and the actions of a people". The book was not published until 1913, but most of its contents had appeared in the *Revue d'Art Dramatique* between 1900 and 1903. Rolland attempted to put his theory into practice with his [melodramatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melodrama "Melodrama") dramas about the [French Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution "French Revolution"), *Danton* (1900) and *The Fourteenth of July* (1902), but it was his ideas that formed a major reference point for subsequent [practitioners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_practitioners "Theatre practitioners").

The essay is part of a more general movement around the turn of that century towards the [democratization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratization "Democratization") of the theatre. The *Revue* had held a competition and tried to organize a "World Congress on People's Theatre", and a number of People's Theatres had opened across Europe, including the [Freie Volksbühne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volksbühne "Volksbühne") movement ('Free People's Theatre') in Germany and Maurice Pottecher's [Théâtre du Peuple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Théâtre_du_Peuple "Théâtre du Peuple") in France. Rolland was a disciple of Pottecher and dedicated *The People's Theatre* to him.

Rolland's approach is more aggressive, though, than Pottecher's poetic vision of theatre as a substitute 'social religion' bringing unity to the nation. Rolland indicts the [bourgeoisie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bourgeoisie "Bourgeoisie") for its [appropriation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultural_appropriation "Cultural appropriation") of the theatre, causing it to slide into [decadence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decadence "Decadence"), and the deleterious effects of its [ideological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ideology "Ideology") dominance. In proposing a suitable [repertoire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Repertory "Repertory") for his people's theatre, Rolland rejects classical drama in the belief that it is either too difficult or too static to be of interest to the masses. Drawing on the ideas of [Jean-Jacques Rousseau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Jacques_Rousseau "Jean-Jacques Rousseau"), he proposes instead "an epic historical theatre of 'joy, force and intelligence' which will remind the people of its revolutionary heritage and revitalize the forces working for a new society" (in the words of Bradby and McCormick, quoting Rolland). Rolland believed that the people would be improved by seeing heroic images of their past. Rousseau's influence may be detected in Rolland's conception of theatre-as-[festivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Festival "Festival"), an emphasis that reveals a fundamental [anti-theatrical prejudice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-theatrical_prejudice "Anti-theatrical prejudice"): "Theatre supposes lives that are poor and agitated, a people searching in dreams for a refuge from thought. If we were happier and freer we should not feel hungry for theatre. [...] A people that is happy and free has need of festivities more than of theatres; it will always see in itself the finest [spectacle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectacle "Spectacle")".

Programme sheet for [Piscator's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erwin_Piscator "Erwin Piscator") 1922 production of Rolland's drama *The Time Will Come* (1903), at the Central-Theater in [Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berlin "Berlin").

Rolland's dramas have been staged by some of the most influential theatre directors of the twentieth century, including [Max Reinhardt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Reinhardt "Max Reinhardt") and [Erwin Piscator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erwin_Piscator "Erwin Piscator"). Piscator directed the world première of Rolland's pacifist drama *The Time Will Come* (*Le Temps viendra*, written in 1903) at [Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berlin "Berlin")'s Central-Theater, which opened on 17 November 1922 with music by K Pringsheim and [scenic design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scenic_design "Scenic design") by O Schmalhausen and M Meier. The play addresses the connections between [imperialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperialism "Imperialism") and capitalism, the treatment of enemy civilians, and the use of [concentration camps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concentration_camp "Concentration camp"), all of which are dramatised via an episode in the [Boer War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boer_War "Boer War"). Piscator described his treatment of the play as "thoroughly [naturalistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naturalism_(theatre) "Naturalism (theatre)")", whereby he sought "to achieve the greatest possible [realism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Realism_(theatre) "Realism (theatre)") in acting and decor". Despite the play's overly-[rhetorical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhetoric "Rhetoric") style, the production was reviewed positively.

## Novels

Rolland's most famous novel is the 10-volume [novel sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Novel_sequence "Novel sequence") *[Jean-Christophe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Christophe "Jean-Christophe")* (1904–1912), which brings "together his interests and ideals in the story of a German musical genius who makes France his second home and becomes a vehicle for Rolland's views on music, social matters and understanding between nations". His other novels are *Colas Breugnon* (1919), *[Clérambault](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clérambault_(novel) "Clérambault (novel)")* (1920), *[Pierre et Luce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_et_luce "Pierre et luce")* (1920) and his second roman-fleuve, the 7-volume *L'âme enchantée* (1922–1933).

He became a history teacher at [Lycée Henri IV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lycée_Henri_IV "Lycée Henri IV"), then at the [Lycée Louis le Grand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lycée_Louis_le_Grand "Lycée Louis le Grand"), and a member of the [École française de Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/École_française_de_Rome "École française de Rome"), then a professor of the History of Music at the [Sorbonne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Paris "University of Paris"), and History Professor at the École Normale Supérieure.

## Correspondence with Richard Strauss

In 1899 Rolland started what became a voluminous correspondence with the German composer [Richard Strauss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Strauss "Richard Strauss") (the English translation, by [Rollo Myers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rollo_Myers "Rollo Myers"), runs to 239 pages, including some diary entries). At the time, Strauss was a celebrated conductor of works by [Wagner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wagner "Wagner"), [Liszt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liszt "Liszt"), [Mozart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mozart "Mozart"), and of his own [tone poems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symphonic_poem "Symphonic poem"). In 1905, Strauss completed his opera [*Salome*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salome_(opera) "Salome (opera)"), based on the verse play by [Oscar Wilde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oscar_Wilde "Oscar Wilde"), originally written in French. Strauss based his version of [*Salome*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salome_(opera) "Salome (opera)") on [Hedwig Lachmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hedwig_Lachmann "Hedwig Lachmann")'s German translation which he had seen performed in [Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berlin "Berlin") in 1902. Out of respect for Wilde, Strauss wanted to create a parallel French version, to be as close as possible to Wilde's original text, and he wrote to Rolland requesting his help on this project.

Rolland was initially reluctant, but a lengthy exchange ensued, occupying 50 pages of the Myers edition, and in the end Rolland made 191 suggestions for improving the Strauss/Wilde libretto. The resulting French version of *Salome* received its first performance in [Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paris "Paris") in 1907, two years after the German premiere. Thereafter, Rolland's letters regularly discussed Strauss's operas, including the occasional criticism of Strauss's librettist, [Hugo von Hofmannsthal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugo_von_Hofmannsthal "Hugo von Hofmannsthal"): "I only regret that the great writer who gives you such brilliant libretti too often lacks a sense of the theatre."

Rolland was a pacifist and concurred with Strauss when the latter refused to sign the [Manifesto of German artists and intellectuals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manifesto_of_the_Ninety-Three "Manifesto of the Ninety-Three") supporting the German role in World War I. Rolland noted Strauss's response in his diary entry for October 1914: "Declarations about war and politics are not fitting for an artist, who must give his attention to his creations and his works."(*Myers p. 160*)

## Correspondence with Freud

1923 saw the beginning of a correspondence between psychoanalyst [Sigmund Freud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigmund_Freud "Sigmund Freud") and Rolland, who found that the admiration that he showed for Freud was reciprocated in equal measures (Freud proclaiming in a letter to him: "That I have been allowed to exchange a greeting with you will remain a happy memory to the end of my days."). This correspondence introduced Freud to the concept of the "[oceanic feeling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oceanic_feeling "Oceanic feeling")" that Rolland had developed through his study of [Eastern mysticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_mysticism "Eastern mysticism"). Freud opened his next book *[Civilization and its Discontents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civilization_and_its_Discontents "Civilization and its Discontents")* (1929) with a debate on the nature of this feeling, which he mentioned had been noted to him by an anonymous "friend". This friend was Rolland. Rolland would remain a major influence on Freud's work, continuing their correspondence right up to Freud's death in 1939.
