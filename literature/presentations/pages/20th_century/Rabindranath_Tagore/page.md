**Rabindranath Tagore** [FRAS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow_of_the_Royal_Asiatic_Society "Fellow of the Royal Asiatic Society") (Bengali: [[ˈrobind̪ɾonat̪ʰ ˈʈʰakuɾ]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Bengali "Help:IPA/Bengali") [ⓘ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:LL-Q9610_(ben)-Bodhisattwa-%E0%A6%B0%E0%A6%AC%E0%A7%80%E0%A6%A8%E0%A7%8D%E0%A6%A6%E0%A7%8D%E0%A6%B0%E0%A6%A8%E0%A6%BE%E0%A6%A5_%E0%A6%A0%E0%A6%BE%E0%A6%95%E0%A7%81%E0%A6%B0.wav "File:LL-Q9610 (ben)-Bodhisattwa-রবীন্দ্রনাথ ঠাকুর.wav"); 7 May 1861 – 7 August 1941) was a [Bengali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bengalis "Bengalis") poet, author, playwright, musician, composer, philosopher, social reformer, painter, and one of the foremost figures of the [Bengal Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bengal_Renaissance "Bengal Renaissance"). In 1913, Tagore became the first [Indian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Indian_Nobel_laureates "List of Indian Nobel laureates") and non-[European](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_people "European people") to win a Nobel Prize in any category, and also the first lyricist to win the [Nobel Prize in Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1913_Nobel_Prize_in_Literature "1913 Nobel Prize in Literature"). A significant moulder of culture within [Bengal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bengal "Bengal") and the [Indian subcontinent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_subcontinent "Indian subcontinent"), he wrote and composed the national anthems of [India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/India "India") and [Bangladesh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bangladesh "Bangladesh").

He reshaped [Bengali literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bengali_literature "Bengali literature") and [music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_Bengal "Music of Bengal") as well as [Indian art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_art "Indian art") with [Contextual Modernism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contextual_Modernism "Contextual Modernism") in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. He was the author of the "profoundly sensitive, fresh and beautiful" poetry of *[Gitanjali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gitanjali "Gitanjali").* Tagore's poetic songs were viewed as spiritual and mercurial; his elegant [prose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prose "Prose") and magical poetry were widely popular in the [Indian subcontinent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_subcontinent "Indian subcontinent"). He was a fellow of the [Royal Asiatic Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Asiatic_Society_of_Great_Britain_and_Ireland "Royal Asiatic Society of Great Britain and Ireland"). Referred to as "the [Bard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bard "Bard") of Bengal", Tagore was known by the [sobriquets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sobriquets "Sobriquets") **Gurudeb**, **Kobiguru**, and **Bishwokobi**.

A [Bengali Brahmin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bengali_Brahmin "Bengali Brahmin") from [Calcutta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calcutta "Calcutta") with ancestral [gentry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zamindar "Zamindar") roots in [Jessore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jessore_District "Jessore District") and [Bardhaman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bardhaman_district "Bardhaman district") districts, Tagore wrote poetry as an eight-year-old. At the age of sixteen, he released [his first substantial poems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhānusiṃha_Ṭhākurer_Paḍāvalī "Bhānusiṃha Ṭhākurer Paḍāvalī") under the pseudonym *Bhānusiṃha* ("Sun Lion"), which were seized upon by literary authorities as long-lost classics. By 1877 he graduated to his first short stories and dramas, published under his real name. As a [humanist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humanist "Humanist"), [universalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universalist "Universalist"), [internationalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internationalism_(politics) "Internationalism (politics)"), and ardent critic of [nationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nationalism "Nationalism"), he denounced the [British Raj](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Raj "British Raj") and advocated independence from Britain. As an exponent of the Bengal Renaissance, he advanced a vast canon that comprised paintings, sketches and doodles, hundreds of texts, and some two thousand songs; his legacy also endures in his founding of [Visva-Bharati University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visva-Bharati_University "Visva-Bharati University").

Tagore modernised Bengali art by spurning rigid classical forms and resisting linguistic strictures. His novels, stories, songs, dance dramas, and essays spoke to topics political and personal. *[Gitanjali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gitanjali "Gitanjali")* (*Song Offerings*), *[Gora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gora_(novel) "Gora (novel)")* (*Fair-Faced*), and *Ghare-Baire* (*[The Home and the World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Home_and_the_World "The Home and the World")*) are his best-known works. His poetry, short stories, and novels were both praised and criticised for their lyricism, colloquial tone, naturalism, and philosophical introspection. His compositions were chosen by two nations as national anthems: India's "[Jana Gana Mana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jana_Gana_Mana "Jana Gana Mana")" and [Bangladesh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bangladesh "Bangladesh")'s "[Amar Sonar Bangla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amar_Sonar_Bangla "Amar Sonar Bangla")". The [Sri Lankan national anthem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sri_Lanka_Matha "Sri Lanka Matha") was also inspired by his work. His song "[Banglar Mati Banglar Jol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banglar_Mati_Banglar_Jol "Banglar Mati Banglar Jol")" has been adopted as the state anthem of [West Bengal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Bengal "West Bengal").

## Family background

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/15/Genealogy_of_the_Tagore_Family_of_India.png/500px-Genealogy_of_the_Tagore_Family_of_India.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Genealogy_of_the_Tagore_Family_of_India.png)

Genealogy chart of the Tagore family, illustrating his ancestors, descendents and extended family.

The name Tagore is the anglicised transliteration of [Thakur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thakur_(title) "Thakur (title)"). The original surname of the [Tagores](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tagore_family "Tagore family") was Kushari. They were [Pirali Brahmin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pirali_Brahmin "Pirali Brahmin") ('Pirali' historically carried a stigmatised and pejorative connotation; an insult that ostracised Piralis from other Brahmin sub-castes) who originally belonged to a village named *Kush* in the district named [Burdwan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bardhaman_district "Bardhaman district") in West Bengal. The biographer of Rabindranath Tagore, [Prabhat Kumar Mukhopadhyaya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prabhat_Kumar_Mukhopadhyaya "Prabhat Kumar Mukhopadhyaya") wrote in the first volume of his book *Rabindrajibani O Rabindra Sahitya Prabeshak* that

> The Kusharis were the descendants of Deen Kushari, the son of [Bhatta Narayana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhatta_Narayana "Bhatta Narayana"); Deen was granted a village named Kush (in [Burdwan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bardhaman_district "Bardhaman district") zilla) by [Maharaja](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maharaja "Maharaja") Kshitisura, he became its chief and came to be known as Kushari.

## Life and events

### Early life: 1861–1878

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/8b/Tagore_in_London_1879.jpg/250px-Tagore_in_London_1879.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Tagore_in_London_1879.jpg)

Young Tagore in London, 1879

> The last two days a storm has been raging, similar to the description in my song—*Jhauro jhauro borishe baridhara*  [... amidst it] a hapless, homeless man drenched from top to toe standing on the roof of his steamer [...] the last two days I have been singing this song over and over [...] as a result the pelting sound of the intense rain, the wail of the wind, the sound of the heaving Gorai River, [...] have assumed a fresh life and found a new language and I have felt like a major actor in this new musical drama unfolding before me.

— Letter to [Indira Devi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indira_Devi_Chaudhurani "Indira Devi Chaudhurani").

The youngest of 13 surviving children, Tagore (nicknamed "Rabi") was born on 7 May 1861 in the [Jorasanko mansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jorasanko_Thakur_Bari "Jorasanko Thakur Bari") in [Calcutta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calcutta "Calcutta"), the son of [Debendranath Tagore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Debendranath_Tagore "Debendranath Tagore") (1817–1905) and Sarada Devi (1830–1875).

[![Black-and-white photograph of a finely dressed man and woman: the man, smiling, stands with the hand on the hip and elbow turned outward with a shawl draped over his shoulders and in Bengali formal wear. In front of him, the woman, seated, is in an elaborate dress and shawl; she leans against a carved table supporting a vase and flowing leaves.](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/5/58/Rabindranath-Tagore-Mrinalini-Devi-1883.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail_unscaled)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Rabindranath-Tagore-Mrinalini-Devi-1883.jpg)

Tagore and his wife [Mrinalini Devi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mrinalini_Devi "Mrinalini Devi"), 1883

Tagore was raised mostly by servants; his mother had died in his early childhood, and his father travelled widely. The [Tagore family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tagore_family "Tagore family") was at the forefront of the [Bengal renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bengal_renaissance "Bengal renaissance"). They hosted the publication of literary magazines; theatre and recitals of Bengali and Western classical music featured there regularly. Tagore's father invited several professional [Dhrupad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dhrupad "Dhrupad") musicians to stay in the house and teach [Indian classical music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_classical_music "Indian classical music") to the children. Tagore's oldest brother [Dwijendranath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dwijendranath_Tagore "Dwijendranath Tagore") was a philosopher and poet. Another brother, [Satyendranath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satyendranath_Tagore "Satyendranath Tagore"), was the first Indian appointed to the elite and formerly all-European [Indian Civil Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Civil_Service "Indian Civil Service"). Yet another brother, [Jyotirindranath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jyotirindranath_Tagore "Jyotirindranath Tagore"), was a musician, composer, and playwright. His sister [Swarnakumari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swarnakumari_Devi "Swarnakumari Devi") became a novelist. Jyotirindranath's wife [Kadambari Devi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kadambari_Devi "Kadambari Devi"), slightly older than Tagore, was a dear friend and powerful influence. Her abrupt suicide in 1884, soon after he married, left him profoundly distraught for years.

Tagore largely avoided classroom schooling and preferred to roam the manor or nearby [Bolpur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bolpur "Bolpur") and [Panihati](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panihati "Panihati"), which the family visited. His brother [Hemendranath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hemendranath_Tagore "Hemendranath Tagore") tutored and physically conditioned him by having him swim the Ganges or trek through hills, by gymnastics, and by practising [judo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judo "Judo") and [wrestling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_wrestling "Indian wrestling"). He learned drawing, anatomy, geography and history, literature, mathematics, Sanskrit, and English—his least favourite subject. Tagore loathed formal education—his scholarly travails at the local [Presidency College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presidency_University,_Kolkata "Presidency University, Kolkata") spanned a single day. Years later, he held that proper teaching does not explain things; proper teaching stokes curiosity.

After his *[upanayan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upanayan "Upanayan")* (coming-of-age rite) at age eleven, Tagore and his father left Calcutta in February 1873 to tour India for several months, visiting his father's [Shantiniketan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santiniketan "Santiniketan") estate and [Amritsar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amritsar "Amritsar") before reaching the [Himalayan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Himalayas "Himalayas") [hill station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hill_station "Hill station") of [Dalhousie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dalhousie,_India "Dalhousie, India"). There Tagore read biographies, studied history, astronomy, modern science, and [Sanskrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit "Sanskrit"), and examined the classical poetry of [Kālidāsa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kālidāsa "Kālidāsa"). During his 1-month stay at Amritsar in 1873 he was greatly influenced by melodious [gurbani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gurbani "Gurbani") and Nanak bani being sung at Golden Temple, for which both father and son were regular visitors. He writes in his *My Reminiscences* (1912):

> The golden temple of Amritsar comes back to me like a dream. Many a morning have I accompanied my father to this Gurudarbar of the Sikhs in the middle of the lake. There the sacred chanting resounds continually. My father, seated amidst the throng of worshippers, would sometimes add his voice to the hymn of praise, and finding a stranger joining in their devotions they would wax enthusiastically cordial, and we would return loaded with the sanctified offerings of sugar crystals and other sweets.

He wrote 6 poems relating to Sikhism and several articles in Bengali children's magazine about Sikhism.

- **Poems on Guru Gobind Singh:** নিষ্ফল উপহার Nishfal-upahaar (1888, translated as "Futile Gift"), গুরু গোবিন্দ Guru Gobinda (1899) and শেষ শিক্ষা Shesh Shiksha (1899, translated as "Last Teachings")
- **Poem on Banda Bahadur:** বন্দী বীর Bandi-bir (The Prisoner Warrior, written in 1888 or 1898)
- **Poem on Bhai Torusingh:** প্রার্থনাতীত দান (prarthonatit dan – Unsolicited gift) written in 1888 or 1898
- **Poem on Nehal Singh:** নীহাল সিংহ (Nihal Singh) written in 1935.

Tagore returned to Jorosanko and completed a set of major works by 1877, one of them a long poem in the [Maithili](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maithili_language "Maithili language") style of [Vidyapati](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vidyapati "Vidyapati"). As a joke, he claimed that these were the lost works of newly discovered 17th-century [Vaiṣṇava](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaishnavism "Vaishnavism") poet Bhānusiṃha. Regional experts accepted them as the lost works of the fictitious poet. He debuted in the short-story genre in Bengali with "[Bhikharini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhikharini "Bhikharini")" ("The Beggar Woman"). Published in the same year, *Sandhya Sangit* (1882) includes the poem "Nirjharer Swapnabhanga" ("The Rousing of the Waterfall").

### Shilaidaha: 1878–1901

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/42/%E0%A6%B0%E0%A6%AC%E0%A7%80%E0%A6%A8%E0%A7%8D%E0%A6%A6%E0%A7%8D%E0%A6%B0_%E0%A6%95%E0%A7%81%E0%A6%A0%E0%A6%BF%E0%A6%AC%E0%A6%BE%E0%A6%A1%E0%A6%BC%E0%A6%BF_%E0%A6%B6%E0%A6%BF%E0%A6%B2%E0%A6%BE%E0%A6%87%E0%A6%A6%E0%A6%B9_%E0%A6%95%E0%A7%81%E0%A6%B7%E0%A7%8D%E0%A6%9F%E0%A6%BF%E0%A6%AF%E0%A6%BC%E0%A6%BE.jpg/250px-%E0%A6%B0%E0%A6%AC%E0%A7%80%E0%A6%A8%E0%A7%8D%E0%A6%A6%E0%A7%8D%E0%A6%B0_%E0%A6%95%E0%A7%81%E0%A6%A0%E0%A6%BF%E0%A6%AC%E0%A6%BE%E0%A6%A1%E0%A6%BC%E0%A6%BF_%E0%A6%B6%E0%A6%BF%E0%A6%B2%E0%A6%BE%E0%A6%87%E0%A6%A6%E0%A6%B9_%E0%A6%95%E0%A7%81%E0%A6%B7%E0%A7%8D%E0%A6%9F%E0%A6%BF%E0%A6%AF%E0%A6%BC%E0%A6%BE.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:রবীন্দ্র_কুঠিবাড়ি_শিলাইদহ_কুষ্টিয়া.jpg)

Tagore's house in [Shilaidaha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shilaidaha "Shilaidaha"), [Bangladesh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bangladesh "Bangladesh")

Because Debendranath wanted his son to become a barrister, Tagore enrolled at a public school in Brighton, East Sussex, England in 1878. He stayed for several months at a house that the Tagore family owned near [Brighton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brighton "Brighton") and [Hove](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hove "Hove"), in Medina Villas; in 1877 his nephew and niece—Suren and [Indira Devi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indira_Devi_Chaudhurani "Indira Devi Chaudhurani"), the children of Tagore's brother [Satyendranath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satyendranath_Tagore "Satyendranath Tagore")—were sent together with their mother, Tagore's sister-in-law, to live with him. He briefly read law at [University College London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_College_London "University College London"), but again left, opting instead for independent study of [Shakespeare's plays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakespeare's_plays "Shakespeare's plays") *[Coriolanus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coriolanus "Coriolanus")*, and *[Antony and Cleopatra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antony_and_Cleopatra "Antony and Cleopatra")* and the *[Religio Medici](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religio_Medici "Religio Medici")* of [Thomas Browne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Browne "Thomas Browne"). Lively English, Irish, and Scottish folk tunes impressed Tagore, whose own tradition of [Nidhubabu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nidhu_Babu "Nidhu Babu")-authored *[kirtans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kirtans "Kirtans")* and *[tappas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tappa "Tappa")* and [Brahmo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahmo "Brahmo") hymnody was subdued. In 1880, he returned to Bengal degree-less, resolving to reconcile European novelty with Brahmo traditions, taking the best from each. After returning to Bengal, Tagore regularly published poems, stories, and novels. These had a profound influence within Bengal itself but received little national attention. In 1883, he married 9-year-old [Mrinalini Devi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mrinalini_Devi "Mrinalini Devi"), born Bhabatarini, 1874–1902 (this was a common practice at the time). They had five children, two of whom died in childhood.

Tagore family boat (bajra or [budgerow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Budgerow "Budgerow")), the "Padma".

In 1890, Tagore began managing his vast ancestral estates in [Shelaidaha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shelaidaha "Shelaidaha") (today a region of Bangladesh); he was joined there by his wife and children in 1898. Tagore released his *Manasi* poems (1890), among his best-known work. As *[Zamindar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zamindar "Zamindar") Babu*, Tagore criss-crossed the [Padma River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Padma_River "Padma River") in command of the *Padma*, the luxurious family barge (also known as "[budgerow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Budgerow "Budgerow")"). He collected mostly token rents and blessed villagers, who in turn honoured him with banquets—occasionally of dried rice and sour milk. He met [Gagan Harkara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gagan_Harkara "Gagan Harkara"), through whom he became familiar with [Baul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baul "Baul") [Lalon Shah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lalon_Shah "Lalon Shah"), whose folk songs greatly influenced Tagore. Tagore worked to popularise Lalon's songs. The period 1891–1895, Tagore's *Sadhana* period, named after one of his magazines, was his most productive; in these years he wrote more than half the stories of the three-volume, 84-story *Galpaguchchha*. Its ironic and grave tales examined the voluptuous poverty of an idealised rural Bengal.

### Santiniketan: 1901–1932

[![Posed group black-and-white photograph of seven Chinese men, possibly academics, in formal wear: two wear European-style suits, the five others wear Chinese traditional dress; four of the seven sit on the floor in the foreground; another sits on a chair behind them at centre-left; two others stand in the background. They surround an eighth man who is robed, bearded, and sitting in a chair placed at centre-left. Four elegant windows are behind them in a line.](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/bd/Tagore-THU.jpg/330px-Tagore-THU.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Tagore-THU.jpg)

[Tsinghua University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsinghua_University "Tsinghua University"), 1924

In 1901 Tagore moved to [Santiniketan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santiniketan "Santiniketan") to found an [ashram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashram "Ashram") with a marble-floored prayer hall—*The [Mandir](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Mandir "wikt:Mandir")*—an experimental school, groves of trees, gardens, a library. There his wife and two of his children died. His father died in 1905. He received monthly payments as part of his inheritance and income from the Maharaja of [Tripura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tripura "Tripura"), sales of his family's jewellery, his seaside bungalow in [Puri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puri_(city) "Puri (city)"), and a derisory 2,000 rupees in book royalties. He gained Bengali and foreign readers alike; he published *[Naivedya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naivedya "Naivedya")* (1901) and *Kheya* (1906) and translated poems into free verse.

In 1912, Tagore translated his 1910 work *[Gitanjali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gitanjali "Gitanjali")* into English. While on a trip to London, he shared these poems with admirers, including [William Butler Yeats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Butler_Yeats "William Butler Yeats") and [Ezra Pound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ezra_Pound "Ezra Pound"). London's [India Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/India_Society "India Society") published the work in a limited edition, and the American magazine *[Poetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poetry_(magazine) "Poetry (magazine)")* published a selection from *Gitanjali*. In November 1913, Tagore learned he had won that year's [Nobel Prize in Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Literature "Nobel Prize in Literature"): the [Swedish Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swedish_Academy "Swedish Academy") appreciated the idealistic—and for Westerners—accessible nature of a small body of his translated material focused on the 1912 *Gitanjali: Song Offerings*. He was awarded a knighthood by King George V in the [1915 Birthday Honours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1915_Birthday_Honours "1915 Birthday Honours"), but Tagore renounced it after the 1919 [Jallianwala Bagh massacre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jallianwala_Bagh_massacre "Jallianwala Bagh massacre"). Renouncing the knighthood, Tagore wrote in a letter addressed to [Lord Chelmsford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederic_Thesiger,_1st_Viscount_Chelmsford "Frederic Thesiger, 1st Viscount Chelmsford"), the then British Viceroy of India, "The disproportionate severity of the punishments inflicted upon the unfortunate people and the methods of carrying them out, we are convinced, are without parallel in the history of civilised governments...The time has come when badges of honour make our shame glaring in their incongruous context of humiliation, and I for my part wish to stand, shorn of all special distinctions, by the side of my countrymen."

In 1919, he was invited by the president and chairman of Anjuman-e-Islamia, [Syed Abdul Majid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syed_Abdul_Majid "Syed Abdul Majid") to visit [Sylhet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sylhet "Sylhet") for the first time. The event attracted over 5000 people.

In 1921, Tagore and agricultural economist [Leonard Elmhirst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonard_Knight_Elmhirst "Leonard Knight Elmhirst") set up the "Institute for Rural Reconstruction", later renamed Shriniketan or "Abode of Welfare", in [Surul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surul "Surul"), a village near the *ashram*. With it, Tagore sought to moderate [Gandhi's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mohandas_Karamchand_Gandhi "Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi") *[Swaraj](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swaraj "Swaraj")* protests, which he occasionally blamed for British India's perceived mental – and thus ultimately colonial – decline. He sought aid from donors, officials, and scholars worldwide to "free village[s] from the shackles of helplessness and ignorance" by "vitalis[ing] knowledge". In the early 1930s, he targeted ambient "abnormal caste consciousness" and [untouchability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Untouchability "Untouchability"). He lectured against these, he penned [Dalit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dalit "Dalit") heroes for his poems and his dramas, and he campaigned—successfully—to open [Guruvayoor Temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guruvayoor_Temple "Guruvayoor Temple") to Dalits.

### Twilight years: 1932–1941

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/3b/Bundesarchiv_Bild_102-11643%2C_Rabindranath_Tagore.jpg/250px-Bundesarchiv_Bild_102-11643%2C_Rabindranath_Tagore.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Bundesarchiv_Bild_102-11643,_Rabindranath_Tagore.jpg)

In Germany, 1931

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/14/Last_pic_of_Tagore.jpg/250px-Last_pic_of_Tagore.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Last_pic_of_Tagore.jpg)

Last picture of Rabindranath, 1941

Dutta and Robinson describe this phase of Tagore's life as being one of a "peripatetic [litterateur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Litterateur "Litterateur")". It affirmed his opinion that human divisions were shallow. During a May 1932 visit to a [Bedouin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bedouin "Bedouin") encampment in the Iraqi desert, the tribal chief told him that "Our [Prophet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad "Muhammad") has said that a true Muslim is he by whose words and deeds not the least of his brother-men may ever come to any harm..." Tagore confided in his diary: "I was startled into recognising in his words the voice of essential humanity." To the end, Tagore scrutinised orthodoxy—and in 1934, he struck. That year, [an earthquake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1934_Nepal-Bihar_earthquake "1934 Nepal-Bihar earthquake") hit Bihar and killed thousands. Gandhi hailed it as seismic *[karma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karma "Karma")*, as divine retribution avenging the oppression of Dalits. Tagore rebuked him for his seemingly ignominious implications. He mourned the perennial poverty of Calcutta and the socioeconomic decline of Bengal and detailed this newly plebeian aesthetics in an unrhymed hundred-line poem whose technique of searing double-vision foreshadowed [Satyajit Ray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satyajit_Ray "Satyajit Ray")'s film *[Apur Sansar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apur_Sansar_(The_World_of_Apu) "Apur Sansar (The World of Apu)")*. Fifteen new volumes appeared, among them prose-poem works *Punashcha* (1932), *Shes Saptak* (1935), and *Patraput* (1936). Experimentation continued in his prose-songs and dance-dramas— *[Chitra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chitra_(play) "Chitra (play)")* (1914), *Shyama* (1939), and *Chandalika* (1938)— and in his novels— *Dui Bon* (1933), *Malancha* (1934), and *Char Adhyay* (1934).

> Clouds come floating into my life, no longer to carry rain or usher storm, but to add color to my sunset sky.

— Verse 292, *Stray Birds*, 1916.

Tagore's remit expanded to science in his last years, as hinted in *Visva-Parichay*, a 1937 collection of essays. His respect for scientific laws and his exploration of biology, physics, and astronomy informed his poetry, which exhibited extensive naturalism and verisimilitude. He wove the *process* of science, the narratives of scientists, into stories in *Se* (1937), *Tin Sangi* (1940), and *Galpasalpa* (1941). His last five years were marked by chronic pain and two long periods of illness. These began when Tagore lost consciousness in late 1937; he remained comatose and near death for a time. This was followed in late 1940 by a similar spell, from which he never recovered. Poetry from these valetudinary years is among his finest. A period of prolonged agony ended with Tagore's death on 7 August 1941, aged 80. He was in an upstairs room of the Jorasanko mansion in which he grew up. The date is still mourned. A. K. Sen, brother of the first chief election commissioner, received dictation from Tagore on 30 July 1941, a day before a scheduled operation: his last poem.

> I'm lost in the middle of my birthday. I want my friends, their touch, with the earth's last love. I will take life's final offering, I will take the human's last blessing. Today my sack is empty. I have given completely whatever I had to give. In return, if I receive anything—some love, some forgiveness—then I will take it with me when I step on the boat that crosses to the festival of the wordless end.

## Travels

 [![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/09/Rabindranath_with_Einstein.jpg/250px-Rabindranath_with_Einstein.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Rabindranath_with_Einstein.jpg)

At the [Iranian Majlis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Majlis_of_Iran "Majlis of Iran") (parliament) in [Tehran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tehran "Tehran"), [Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran "Iran"), 1932

Between 1878 and 1932, Tagore set foot in more than thirty countries on five continents. In 1912, he took a sheaf of his translated works to England, where they gained attention from missionary and Gandhi protégé [Charles F. Andrews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_F._Andrews "Charles F. Andrews"), Irish poet [William Butler Yeats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Butler_Yeats "William Butler Yeats"), [Ezra Pound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ezra_Pound "Ezra Pound"), [Robert Bridges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Bridges "Robert Bridges"), [Ernest Rhys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_Rhys "Ernest Rhys"), [Thomas Sturge Moore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Sturge_Moore "Thomas Sturge Moore"), and others. Yeats wrote the preface to the English translation of *Gitanjali*; Andrews joined Tagore at Santiniketan. In November 1912 Tagore began touring the United States and the United Kingdom, staying in [Butterton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butterton "Butterton"), Staffordshire with Andrews's clergymen friends. From May 1916 until April 1917, he lectured in Japan and the United States. He denounced nationalism. His essay "Nationalism in India" was scorned and praised; it was admired by [Romain Rolland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romain_Rolland "Romain Rolland") and other pacifists.

Shortly after returning home, the 63-year-old Tagore accepted an invitation from the Peruvian government. He travelled to Mexico. Each government pledged US$100,000 to his school to commemorate the visits. A week after his 6 November 1924 arrival in Buenos Aires, an ill Tagore shifted to the Villa Miralrío at the behest of [Victoria Ocampo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victoria_Ocampo "Victoria Ocampo"). He left for home in January 1925. In May 1926 Tagore reached Naples; the next day he met [Mussolini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benito_Mussolini "Benito Mussolini") in Rome. Their warm rapport ended when Tagore felt compelled to denounce Fascism in an article published in The Manchester Guardian. He had earlier enthused: "[w]without any doubt he is a great personality. There is such a massive vigor in that head that it reminds one of Michael Angelo's chisel." A "fire-bath" of fascism was to have educed "the immortal soul of Italy ... clothed in quenchless light".

On 1 November 1926 Tagore arrived in Hungary and spent some time on the shore of Lake Balaton in the city of Balatonfüred, recovering from heart problems at a sanitarium. He planted a tree, and a bust statue was placed there in 1956 (a gift from the Indian government, the work of Rasithan Kashar, replaced by a newly gifted statue in 2005) and the lakeside promenade still bears his name since 1957.

> Our passions and desires are unruly, but our character subdues these elements into a harmonious whole. Does something similar to this happen in the physical world? Are the elements rebellious, dynamic with individual impulse? And is there a principle in the physical world that dominates them and puts them into an orderly organization?

— Interviewed by Einstein, 14 April 1930.

In 1927, Tagore travelled for four months to Southeast Asia, stopping in British Malaya, Siam and the Dutch East Indies. Tagore was accompanied by the linguist [Suniti Kumar Chatterji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suniti_Kumar_Chatterji "Suniti Kumar Chatterji") and visited the Buddhist shrine of [Borobudur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borobudur "Borobudur"). The goal of the tour was to raise funds for [Visva-Bharati University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visva-Bharati_University "Visva-Bharati University") and explore the legacies of ancient Indian art, culture and religion in the region. The journey had a profound influence on Tagore's view of Indian history and he became an enthusiastic supporter and promoter of the work of the Calcutta-based Greater India Society, acting as its Purodha or honorary chief advisor. He recorded his impressions in a series of letters, published as [Java-Jatrir Patra](https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.315805/page/7/mode/2up) (Letters from Java).

In early 1930 he left Bengal for a nearly year-long tour of Europe and the United States. Upon returning to Britain—and as his paintings were exhibited in Paris and London—he lodged at a Birmingham Quaker settlement. He wrote his Oxford [Hibbert Lectures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hibbert_Lectures "Hibbert Lectures") and spoke at the annual London Quaker meet. There, addressing relations between the British and the Indians – a topic he would tackle repeatedly over the next two years – Tagore spoke of a "dark chasm of aloofness". He visited [Aga Khan III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aga_Khan_III "Aga Khan III"), stayed at [Dartington Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dartington_Hall "Dartington Hall"), toured Denmark, Switzerland, and Germany from June to mid-September 1930, then went on into the Soviet Union. In April 1932 Tagore, intrigued by the Persian mystic [Hafez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hafez "Hafez"), was hosted by [Reza Shah Pahlavi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reza_Shah_Pahlavi "Reza Shah Pahlavi"). In his other travels, Tagore interacted with [Henri Bergson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_Bergson "Henri Bergson"), [Albert Einstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Einstein "Albert Einstein"), [Robert Frost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Frost "Robert Frost"), [Thomas Mann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Mann "Thomas Mann"), [George Bernard Shaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Bernard_Shaw "George Bernard Shaw"), [H. G. Wells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H._G._Wells "H. G. Wells"), and [Romain Rolland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romain_Rolland "Romain Rolland"). Visits to Persia and Iraq (in 1932) and Sri Lanka (in 1933) composed Tagore's final foreign tour, and his dislike of [communalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communalism_(South_Asia) "Communalism (South Asia)") and nationalism only deepened. Vice-president of India M. [Hamid Ansari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamid_Ansari "Hamid Ansari") has said that Rabindranath Tagore heralded the cultural rapprochement between communities, societies and nations much before it became the liberal norm of conduct. Tagore was a man ahead of his time. He wrote in 1932, while on a visit to Iran, that "each country of Asia will solve its own historical problems according to its strength, nature and needs, but the lamp they will each carry on their path to progress will converge to illuminate the common ray of knowledge."

## Works

Known mostly for his poetry, Tagore wrote novels, essays, short stories, travelogues, dramas, and thousands of songs. Of Tagore's prose, his short stories are perhaps the most highly regarded; he is indeed credited with originating the Bengali-language version of the genre. His works are frequently noted for their rhythmic, optimistic, and lyrical nature. Such stories mostly borrow from the lives of common people. Tagore's non-fiction grappled with history, linguistics, and spirituality. He wrote autobiographies. His travelogues, essays, and lectures were compiled into several volumes, including *Europe Jatrir Patro* (*Letters from Europe*) and *Manusher Dhormo* (*[The Religion of Man](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Religion_of_Man "The Religion of Man")*). His brief chat with [Einstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Einstein "Albert Einstein"), "Note on the Nature of Reality", is included as an appendix to the latter. On the occasion of Tagore's 150th birthday, an anthology (titled *Kalanukromik Rabindra Rachanabali*) of the total body of his works is currently being published in Bengali in chronological order. This includes all versions of each work and fills about eighty volumes. In 2011, [Harvard University Press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University_Press "Harvard University Press") collaborated with [Visva-Bharati University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visva-Bharati_University "Visva-Bharati University") to publish *[The Essential Tagore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Essential_Tagore "The Essential Tagore")*, the largest anthology of Tagore's works available in English; it was edited by [Fakrul Alam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fakrul_Alam "Fakrul Alam") and Radha Chakravarthy and marks the 150th anniversary of Tagore's birth.

### Drama

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/11/Valmiki_Pratibha_Indira_Devi_%26_Rabindranath_Tagore.jpg/250px-Valmiki_Pratibha_Indira_Devi_%26_Rabindranath_Tagore.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Valmiki_Pratibha_Indira_Devi_&_Rabindranath_Tagore.jpg)

Tagore performing the title role in *Valmiki Pratibha* (1881) with his niece [Indira Devi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indira_Devi_Chaudhurani "Indira Devi Chaudhurani") as the goddess [Lakshmi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lakshmi "Lakshmi")

Tagore's experiences with drama began when he was sixteen, with his brother [Jyotirindranath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jyotirindranath_Tagore "Jyotirindranath Tagore"). He wrote his first original dramatic piece when he was twenty – *[Valmiki Pratibha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valmiki_Pratibha "Valmiki Pratibha")* which was shown at the Tagore's mansion. Tagore stated that his works sought to articulate "the play of feeling and not of action". In 1890 he wrote *Visarjan* (an adaptation of his novella *Rajarshi*), which has been regarded as his finest drama. In the original Bengali language, such works included intricate subplots and extended monologues. Later, Tagore's dramas used more philosophical and allegorical themes. The play *[Dak Ghar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Post_Office_(play) "The Post Office (play)")* (*The Post Office*; 1912), describes the child Amal defying his stuffy and puerile confines by ultimately "fall[ing] asleep", hinting his physical death. A story with borderless appeal—gleaning rave reviews in Europe—*Dak Ghar* dealt with death as, in Tagore's words, "spiritual freedom" from "the world of hoarded wealth and certified creeds". Another is Tagore's *Chandalika* (*Untouchable Girl*), which was modelled on an ancient Buddhist legend describing how [Ananda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ananda "Ananda"), the [Gautama Buddha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gautama_Buddha "Gautama Buddha")'s disciple, asks a [tribal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adivasi "Adivasi") girl for water. In *[Raktakarabi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raktakarabi_(play) "Raktakarabi (play)")* ("Red" or "Blood Oleanders") is an allegorical struggle against a kleptocrat king who rules over the residents of *[Yaksha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yaksha "Yaksha") [puri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pur_(Vedic) "Pur (Vedic)")*.

*Chitrangada*, *Chandalika*, and *Shyama* are other key plays that have dance-drama adaptations, which together are known as *[Rabindra Nritya Natya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rabindra_Nritya_Natya "Rabindra Nritya Natya")*.

### Short stories

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/2f/Sabujpatra_logo_%281914-1927%29.jpg/250px-Sabujpatra_logo_%281914-1927%29.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Sabujpatra_logo_(1914-1927).jpg)

Cover of the *[Sabuj Patra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sabuj_Patra "Sabuj Patra")* magazine, edited by [Pramatha Chaudhuri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pramatha_Chaudhuri "Pramatha Chaudhuri")

Tagore began his career in short stories in 1877—when he was only sixteen—with "Bhikharini" ("The Beggar Woman"). With this, Tagore effectively invented the Bengali-language short story genre. The four years from 1891 to 1895 are known as Tagore's "Sadhana" period (named for one of Tagore's magazines). This period was among Tagore's most fecund, yielding more than half the stories contained in the three-volume *Galpaguchchha*, which itself is a collection of eighty-four stories. Such stories usually showcase Tagore's reflections upon his surroundings, on modern and fashionable ideas, and on interesting mind puzzles (which Tagore was fond of testing his intellect with). Tagore typically associated his earliest stories (such as those of the "*Sadhana*" period) with an exuberance of vitality and spontaneity; these characteristics were intimately connected with Tagore's life in the common villages of, among others, [Patisar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patisar "Patisar"), Shajadpur, and [Shilaida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shilaidaha "Shilaidaha") while managing the Tagore family's vast landholdings. There, he beheld the lives of India's poor and common people; Tagore thereby took to examining their lives with a penetrative depth and feeling that was singular in Indian literature up to that point. In particular, such stories as "[Kabuliwala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kabuliwala_(short_story) "Kabuliwala (short story)")" ("The Fruitseller from [Kabul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kabul "Kabul")", published in 1892), "Kshudita Pashan" ("The Hungry Stones") (August 1895), and "Atithi" ("The Runaway", 1895) typified this analytic focus on the downtrodden. Many of the other *Galpaguchchha* stories were written in Tagore's *Sabuj Patra* period from 1914 to 1917, also named after one of the magazines that Tagore edited and heavily contributed to.

### Novels

Tagore wrote eight novels and four novellas, among them *[Nastanirh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nastanirh "Nastanirh")* (1901), *[Noukadubi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noukadubi "Noukadubi")* (1906), *[Chaturanga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chaturanga_(Tagore_novel) "Chaturanga (Tagore novel)")* (1916) and *[Char Adhyay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Char_Adhyay_(novel) "Char Adhyay (novel)")* (1934).

In *[Chokher Bali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chokher_Bali_(novel) "Chokher Bali (novel)")* (1902–1903), Tagore inscribes Bengali society via its heroine: a rebellious widow who would live for herself alone. He pillories the custom of perpetual mourning on the part of widows, who were not allowed to remarry, who were consigned to seclusion and loneliness.

*Ghare Baire* (*[The Home and the World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Home_and_the_World "The Home and the World")*, 1916), through the lens of the idealistic *[zamindar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zamindar "Zamindar")* protagonist Nikhil, excoriates rising Indian nationalism, terrorism, and religious zeal in the [*Swadeshi* movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swadeshi_movement "Swadeshi movement"); a frank expression of Tagore's conflicted sentiments, it emerged from a 1914 bout of depression. The novel ends in Hindu-Muslim violence and Nikhil's likely mortal—wounding.

His longest novel, *[Gora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gora_(novel) "Gora (novel)")* (1907–1910), raises controversial questions regarding the Indian identity. As with *Ghare Baire*, matters of self-identity (*[jāti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jāti "Jāti")*), personal freedom, and religion are developed in the context of a family story and love triangle. In it, an Irish boy orphaned in the [Sepoy Mutiny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sepoy_Mutiny "Sepoy Mutiny") is raised by Hindus as the titular *gora*—"whitey". Ignorant of his foreign origins, he chastises Hindu religious backsliders out of love for the indigenous Indians and solidarity with them against his hegemon-compatriots. He falls for a Brahmo girl, compelling his worried foster father to reveal his lost past and cease his nativist zeal. As a "true dialectic" advancing "arguments for and against strict traditionalism", it tackles the colonial conundrum by "portray[ing] the value of all positions within a particular frame [...] not only syncretism, not only liberal orthodoxy but the extremist reactionary traditionalism he defends by an appeal to what humans share." Among these, Tagore highlights "identity [...] conceived of as *[dharma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dharma "Dharma").*"

In *[Jogajog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jogajog_(novel) "Jogajog (novel)")* (*Yogayog*, *Relationships*, 1929), the heroine Kumudini—bound by the ideals of *[Śiva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Śiva "Śiva")-[Sati](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dakshayani "Dakshayani")*, exemplified by [Dākshāyani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dakshayani "Dakshayani")—is torn between her pity for the sinking fortunes of her progressive and compassionate elder brother and his foil: her roué of a husband. Tagore flaunts his feminist leanings; *pathos* depicts the plight and ultimate demise of women trapped by pregnancy, duty, and family honor; he simultaneously trucks with Bengal's putrescent landed gentry. The story revolves around the underlying rivalry between two families—the Chatterjees, aristocrats now on the decline (Biprodas) and the Ghosals (Madhusudan), representing new money and new arrogance. Kumudini, Biprodas' sister, is caught between the two as she is married off to Madhusudan. She had risen in an observant and sheltered traditional home, as had all her female relations.

Others were uplifting: *[Shesher Kabita](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shesher_Kabita "Shesher Kabita")* (1929) — translated twice as *Last Poem* and *Farewell Song* — is his most lyrical novel, with poems and rhythmic passages written by a poet protagonist. It contains elements of satire and postmodernism and has stock characters who gleefully attack the reputation of an old, outmoded, oppressively renowned poet who, incidentally, goes by a familiar name: "Rabindranath Tagore".

Though his novels remain among the least-appreciated of his works, they have been given renewed attention via film adaptations, by [Satyajit Ray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satyajit_Ray "Satyajit Ray") for *[Charulata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charulata "Charulata")* (based on *Nastanirh*) in 1964 and *[Ghare Baire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghare_Baire_(film) "Ghare Baire (film)")* in 1984, and by several others filmmakers such as Satu Sen for [Chokher Bali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chokher_Bali_(1938_film) "Chokher Bali (1938 film)") already in 1938, when Tagore was still alive.

### Poetry

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/21/Gitanjali_11_title_page.jpg/250px-Gitanjali_11_title_page.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Gitanjali_11_title_page.jpg)

Title page of the 1913 [Macmillan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macmillan_Publishers "Macmillan Publishers") edition of Tagore's *Gitanjali*

[![Three-verse handwritten composition; each verse has original Bengali with English-language translation below: "My fancies are fireflies: specks of living light twinkling in the dark. The same voice murmurs in these desultory lines, which is born in wayside pansies letting hasty glances pass by. The butterfly does not count years but moments, and therefore has enough time."](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/df/Tagore_handwriting_Bengali.jpg/250px-Tagore_handwriting_Bengali.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Tagore_handwriting_Bengali.jpg)

Part of a poem written by Tagore in Hungary, 1926

Internationally, *Gitanjali* ([Bengali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bengali_language "Bengali language"): গীতাঞ্জলি) is Tagore's best-known collection of poetry, for which he was awarded the [Nobel Prize in Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Literature "Nobel Prize in Literature") in 1913. Tagore was the first non-European to receive a Nobel Prize in Literature and the second non-European to receive a Nobel Prize after [Theodore Roosevelt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodore_Roosevelt "Theodore Roosevelt").

Besides *Gitanjali*, other notable works include *Manasi*, *[Sonar Tori](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sonar_Tori "Sonar Tori")* ("Golden Boat"), *Balaka* ("Wild Geese" – the title being a metaphor for migrating souls)

Tagore's poetic style, which proceeds from a lineage established by 15th- and 16th-century Vaishnava poets, ranges from classical formalism to the comic, visionary, and ecstatic. He was influenced by the atavistic mysticism of [Vyasa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vyasa "Vyasa") and other *rishi*-authors of the [Upanishads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upanishad "Upanishad"), the [Bhakti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhakti "Bhakti")-[Sufi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sufism "Sufism") mystic [Kabir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kabir "Kabir"), and [Ramprasad Sen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramprasad_Sen "Ramprasad Sen"). Tagore's most innovative and mature poetry embodies his exposure to Bengali rural folk music, which included mystic [Baul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baul "Baul") ballads such as those of the bard [Lalon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lalon "Lalon"). These, rediscovered and re-popularised by Tagore, resemble 19th-century Kartābhajā hymns that emphasise inward divinity and rebellion against bourgeois *bhadralok* religious and social orthodoxy. During his Shelaidaha years, his poems took on a lyrical voice of the *moner manush*, the Bāuls' "man within the heart" and Tagore's "life force of his deep recesses", or meditating upon the *jeevan devata*—the demiurge or the "living God within". This figure connected with divinity through appeal to nature and the emotional interplay of human drama. Such tools saw use in his Bhānusiṃha poems chronicling the [Radha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radha "Radha")-[Krishna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krishna "Krishna") romance, which were repeatedly revised over seventy years.

Later, with the development of new poetic ideas in Bengal – many originating from younger poets seeking to break with Tagore's style – Tagore absorbed new poetic concepts, which allowed him to further develop a unique identity. Examples of this include *Africa* and *Camalia*, which are among the better-known of his latter poems.

### Songs (Rabindra Sangeet)

Tagore was a prolific composer, with around 2,230 songs to his credit. His songs are known as *[rabindrasangit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rabindra_Sangeet "Rabindra Sangeet")* ("Tagore Song"), which merges fluidly into his literature, most of which—poems or parts of novels, stories, or plays alike—were lyricised. Influenced by the *[thumri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thumri "Thumri")* style of [Hindustani music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindustani_classical_music "Hindustani classical music"), they ran the entire gamut of human emotion, ranging from his early dirge-like Brahmo devotional hymns to quasi-erotic compositions. They emulated the tonal colour of classical *[ragas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raga "Raga")* to varying extents. Some songs mimicked a given raga's melody and rhythm faithfully, others newly blended elements of different *ragas*. Yet about nine-tenths of his work was not *bhanga gaan*, the body of tunes revamped with "fresh value" from select Western, Hindustani, Bengali folk and other regional flavours "external" to Tagore's own ancestral culture.

[![](//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f8/Tagore_singing_Jana_Gana_Mana.webm/250px--Tagore_singing_Jana_Gana_Mana.webm.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser)](//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/f/f8/Tagore_singing_Jana_Gana_Mana.webm?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=rest&utm_content=original)

Rabindranath Tagore reciting Jana Gana Mana

In 1971, *[Amar Shonar Bangla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amar_Shonar_Bangla "Amar Shonar Bangla")* became the national anthem of [Bangladesh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bangladesh "Bangladesh"). It was written – ironically – to protest the [1905 Partition of Bengal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1905_Partition_of_Bengal "1905 Partition of Bengal") along communal lines: cutting off the Muslim-majority East Bengal from Hindu-dominated West Bengal was to avert a regional bloodbath. Tagore saw the partition as a cunning plan to stop the [independence movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_independence_movement "Indian independence movement"), and he aimed to rekindle Bengali unity and tar communalism. *[Jana Gana Mana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jana_Gana_Mana "Jana Gana Mana")* was written in *[shadhu-bhasha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shadhu-bhasha "Shadhu-bhasha")*, a Sanskritised form of Bengali, and is the first of five stanzas of the Brahmo hymn *[Bharot Bhagyo Bidhata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bharot_Bhagyo_Bidhata "Bharot Bhagyo Bidhata")* that Tagore composed. It was first sung in 1911 at a Calcutta session of the [Indian National Congress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_National_Congress "Indian National Congress"), and was adopted in 1950 by the Constituent Assembly of the Republic of India as its national anthem.

[Sri Lanka's National Anthem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sri_Lanka_Matha "Sri Lanka Matha") was inspired by his work.

For Bengalis, the songs' appeal, stemming from the combination of emotive strength and beauty described as surpassing even Tagore's poetry, was such that the *Modern Review* observed that "[t]here is in Bengal no cultured home where Rabindranath's songs are not sung or at least attempted to be sung... Even illiterate villagers sing his songs". Tagore influenced *sitar* maestro [Vilayat Khan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vilayat_Khan "Vilayat Khan") and *[sarodiyas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarod "Sarod")* Buddhadev Dasgupta and [Amjad Ali Khan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amjad_Ali_Khan "Amjad Ali Khan").

### Art works

[![Black-and-white photograph of a stylised sketch depicting a tribal funerary mask.](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/28/Rabindranath_Tagore_Rabindra_Bhavana_collection_2155_pastel_mask.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail_unscaled)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Rabindranath_Tagore_Rabindra_Bhavana_collection_2155_pastel_mask.jpg)

Primitivism: a pastel-coloured rendition of a [Malagan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malagan "Malagan") mask from northern [New Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Ireland_Province "New Ireland Province"), Papua New Guinea

[![Black-and-white close-up photograph of a piece of wood boldly painted in unmixed solid strokes of black and white in a stylised semblance to "ro" and "tho" from the Bengali syllabary.](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/bd/Rabindranath_Tagore_Ra-Tha_seal_initials.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail_unscaled)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Rabindranath_Tagore_Ra-Tha_seal_initials.jpg)

Tagore's Bengali-language initials, the letters র and ঠ, are worked into this "Ro-Tho" (of RAbindranath THAkur) wooden seal, stylistically similar to designs used in traditional [Haida carvings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haida_argillite_carvings "Haida argillite carvings") from the [Pacific Northwest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pacific_Northwest "Pacific Northwest") region of North America. Tagore often embellished his manuscripts with such art.

At sixty, Tagore took up drawing and painting; successful exhibitions of his many works—which made a debut appearance in Paris upon encouragement by artists he met in the south of France—were held throughout Europe. He was likely red, green [colour blind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protanopia "Protanopia"), resulting in works that exhibited strange colour schemes and off-beat aesthetics. Tagore was influenced by numerous styles, including [scrimshaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scrimshaw "Scrimshaw") by the [Malanggan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malanggan "Malanggan") people of northern [New Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Ireland_(island) "New Ireland (island)"), [Papua New Guinea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papua_New_Guinea "Papua New Guinea"), [Haida carvings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haida_argillite_carvings "Haida argillite carvings") from the [Pacific Northwest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pacific_Northwest "Pacific Northwest") region of North America, and woodcuts by the German [Max Pechstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Pechstein "Max Pechstein"). His artist's eye for handwriting was revealed in the simple artistic and rhythmic [leitmotifs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leitmotif "Leitmotif") embellishing the scribbles, cross-outs, and word layouts of his manuscripts. Some of Tagore's lyrics corresponded in a synesthetic sense with particular paintings.

> Surrounded by several painters Rabindranath had always wanted to paint. Writing and music, playwriting and acting came to him naturally and almost without training, as it did to several others in his family, and in even greater measure. But painting eluded him. Yet he tried repeatedly to master the art and there are several references to this in his early letters and reminiscence. In 1900 for instance, when he was nearing forty and already a celebrated writer, he wrote to Jagadish Chandra Bose, "You will be surprised to hear that I am sitting with a sketchbook drawing. Needless to say, the pictures are not intended for any salon in Paris, they cause me not the least suspicion that the national gallery of any country will suddenly decide to raise taxes to acquire them. But, just as a mother lavishes most affection on her ugliest son, so I feel secretly drawn to the very skill that comes to me least easily." He also realized that he was using the eraser more than the pencil, and dissatisfied with the results he finally withdrew, deciding it was not for him to become a painter.

Face of a woman, inspired by [Kadambari Devi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kadambari_Devi "Kadambari Devi"). Ink on paper. [National Gallery of Modern Art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Gallery_of_Modern_Art "National Gallery of Modern Art"), New Delhi

India's [National Gallery of Modern Art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Gallery_of_Modern_Art "National Gallery of Modern Art") lists 102 works by Tagore in its collections.

In 1937, Tagore's paintings were removed from Berlin's baroque [Crown Prince Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kronprinzenpalais "Kronprinzenpalais") by the Nazi regime and five were included in the inventory of "[degenerate art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degenerate_art "Degenerate art")" compiled by the Nazis in 1941–1942.

## Philosophy and religion

An early introduction to Tagore's thought for Western readers was written by [Sarvepalli Radhakrishnan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarvepalli_Radhakrishnan "Sarvepalli Radhakrishnan") in 1918, who analyzed Tagore's views on philosophy, religion, art and poetry, using Tagore's works available in English up to that time. He quotes extensively from Tagore's poetry collections *Gitanjali* (1912), *The Crescent Moon* (1913), *The Gardener* (1913), *Fruit-Gathering* (1916), *Stray Birds* (1916), the essay collections *Sadhana: The Realisation of Life* (1913) and *Personality* (1917), as well as the drama *The King of the Dark Chamber* (1910) and the principal *[Upanishads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upanishads "Upanishads")*. Radhakrishnan interprets Tagore's thought largely through the lens of [Advaita Vedanta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advaita_Vedanta "Advaita Vedanta"), causing differences with Tagore's more poetic, humanistic, and socially critical outlook.

Tagore later elaborated his religious and philosophical ideas in his 1922 lectures published as *Creative Unity*, and his 1931 Hibbert Lectures at Oxford University, published as *The Religion of Man*.

A contemporary analysis was done by
Kalyan Sen Gupta.

## Politics

[![Photo of a formal function, an aged bald man and old woman in simple white robes are seated side-by-side with legs folded on a rug-strewn dais; the man looks at a bearded and garlanded old man seated on another dais at left. In the foreground, various ceremonial objects are arrayed; in the background, dozens of other people observe.](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a1/Gandhi-Tagore-cropped.jpg/250px-Gandhi-Tagore-cropped.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Gandhi-Tagore-cropped.jpg)

Tagore hosts [Gandhi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahatma_Gandhi "Mahatma Gandhi") and wife [Kasturba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kasturba_Gandhi "Kasturba Gandhi") at Santiniketan in 1940.

Tagore opposed [imperialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperialism "Imperialism") and supported Indian nationalists, and these views were first revealed in *Manast*, which was mostly composed in his twenties. Evidence produced during the [Hindu–German Conspiracy Trial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu–German_Conspiracy_Trial "Hindu–German Conspiracy Trial") and latter accounts affirm his awareness of the [Ghadarites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu–German_Conspiracy "Hindu–German Conspiracy") and stated that he sought the support of *[Gensui](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gensui_(Imperial_Japanese_Army) "Gensui (Imperial Japanese Army)")* [Count](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Count "Count") [Terauchi Masatake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terauchi_Masatake "Terauchi Masatake"), the [Prime Minister of Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_Minister_of_Japan "Prime Minister of Japan"), and [Marquess](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marquess "Marquess") [Ōkuma Shigenobu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ōkuma_Shigenobu "Ōkuma Shigenobu"), the former Japanese Prime Minister. Yet he lampooned the [Swadeshi movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swadeshi_movement "Swadeshi movement"); he rebuked it in *[The Cult of the Charkha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Cult_of_the_Charkha "The Cult of the Charkha")*, an acrid 1925 essay. According to [Amartya Sen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amartya_Sen "Amartya Sen"), Tagore rebelled against strongly nationalist forms of the independence movement, and he wanted to assert India's right to be independent without denying the importance of what India could learn from abroad. He urged the masses to avoid victimology and instead seek self-help and education, and he saw the presence of British administration as a "political symptom of our social disease". He maintained that, even for those at the extremes of poverty, "there can be no question of blind revolution"; preferable to it was a "steady and purposeful education".

> So I repeat we never can have a true view of man unless we have a love for him. Civilisation must be judged and prized, not by the amount of power it has developed, but by how much it has evolved and given expression to, by its laws and institutions, the love of humanity.

— *Sādhanā: The Realisation of Life*, 1916.

Such views enraged many. He escaped assassination—and only narrowly—by Indian expatriates during his stay in a San Francisco hotel in late 1916; the plot failed when his would-be assassins fell into an argument. Tagore wrote songs lionising the Indian independence movement. Two of Tagore's more politically charged compositions, "[Chitto Jetha Bhayshunyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chitto_Jetha_Bhayshunyo "Chitto Jetha Bhayshunyo")" ("Where the Mind is Without Fear") and "[Ekla Chalo Re](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ekla_Chalo_Re "Ekla Chalo Re")" ("If They Answer Not to Thy Call, Walk Alone"), gained mass appeal, with the latter favoured by Gandhi. Though somewhat critical of Gandhian activism, Tagore was key in resolving a Gandhi–[Ambedkar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B._R._Ambedkar "B. R. Ambedkar") dispute involving separate electorates for untouchables, thereby mooting at least one of Gandhi's fasts "unto death".

### Repudiation of knighthood

Tagore renounced his knighthood in response to the [Jallianwala Bagh massacre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jallianwala_Bagh_massacre "Jallianwala Bagh massacre") in 1919. In the repudiation letter to the Viceroy, [Lord Chelmsford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederic_Thesiger,_1st_Viscount_Chelmsford "Frederic Thesiger, 1st Viscount Chelmsford"), he wrote

> The time has come when badges of honour make our shame glaring in the incongruous context of humiliation, and I for my part, wish to stand, shorn, of all special distinctions, by the side of those of my countrymen who, for their so-called insignificance, are liable to suffer degradation not fit for human beings.

## Shantiniketan and Visva-Bharati

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/75/Kala_Bhavan%2C_Santiniketan.jpg/250px-Kala_Bhavan%2C_Santiniketan.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Kala_Bhavan,_Santiniketan.jpg)

[Kala Bhavan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kala_Bhavan "Kala Bhavan") (Institute of Fine Arts), [Shantiniketan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shantiniketan "Shantiniketan"), India

Tagore despised rote classroom schooling, as shown in his short story, "The Parrot's Training", wherein a bird is caged and force-fed textbook pages—to death. Visiting Santa Barbara in 1917, Tagore conceived a new type of university: he sought to "make Santiniketan the connecting thread between India and the world [and] a world center for the study of humanity somewhere beyond the limits of nation and geography." The school, which he named [Visva-Bharati](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visva-Bharati_University "Visva-Bharati University"), had its foundation stone laid on 24 December 1918 and was inaugurated precisely three years later. Tagore employed a *[brahmacharya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahmacharya "Brahmacharya")* system: *gurus* gave pupils personal guidance—emotional, intellectual, and spiritual. Teaching was often done under trees. He staffed the school, he contributed his Nobel Prize monies, and his duties as steward-mentor at Shantiniketan kept him busy: mornings he taught classes; afternoons and evenings he wrote the students' textbooks. He fundraised widely for the school in Europe and the United States between 1919 and 1921.

### Theft of Nobel Prize

On 25 March 2004, Tagore's Nobel Prize was stolen from the safety vault of the Visva-Bharati University, along with several other of his belongings. On 7 December 2004, the Swedish Academy decided to present two replicas of Tagore's Nobel Prize, one made of gold and the other made of bronze, to the Visva-Bharati University. It inspired the fictional film *[Nobel Chor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Chor "Nobel Chor")*. In 2016, a baul singer named Pradip Bauri, accused of sheltering the thieves, was arrested.

## Influence and legacy

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/fe/Hungary%2C_Balatonf%C3%BCred%2C_Rabindranath_promenade_in_autumn_-_Tagore_statue.jpg/250px-Hungary%2C_Balatonf%C3%BCred%2C_Rabindranath_promenade_in_autumn_-_Tagore_statue.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Hungary,_Balatonfüred,_Rabindranath_promenade_in_autumn_-_Tagore_statue.jpg)

Bust of Rabindranath in Tagore promenade, [Balatonfüred](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balatonfüred "Balatonfüred"), Hungary

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/23/Rabindranath_Tagore_statue_in_Dublin%2C_Ireland_01.jpg/250px-Rabindranath_Tagore_statue_in_Dublin%2C_Ireland_01.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Rabindranath_Tagore_statue_in_Dublin,_Ireland_01.jpg)

Rabindranath Tagore statue in [Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin "Dublin"), Ireland

Every year, many events pay tribute to Tagore: *Kabipranam*, his birth anniversary, is celebrated by groups scattered across the globe; the annual Tagore Festival held in Urbana, Illinois (US); *Rabindra Path Parikrama* walking pilgrimages from Kolkata to Santiniketan; and recitals of his poetry, which are held on important anniversaries. Bengali culture is fraught with this legacy: from language and arts to history and politics. Amartya Sen deemed Tagore a "towering figure", a "deeply relevant and many-sided contemporary thinker". Tagore's Bengali originals—the 1939 *Rabīndra Rachanāvalī*—is canonised as one of his nation's greatest cultural treasures, and he was roped into a reasonably humble role: "the greatest poet India has produced".

Tagore was renowned throughout much of Europe, North America, and East Asia. He co-founded [Dartington Hall School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dartington_Hall_School "Dartington Hall School"), a progressive coeducational institution; in Japan, he influenced such figures as Nobel laureate [Yasunari Kawabata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yasunari_Kawabata "Yasunari Kawabata"). In [colonial Vietnam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Indochina "French Indochina") Tagore was a guide for the restless spirit of the radical writer and publicist [Nguyen An Ninh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nguyen_An_Ninh "Nguyen An Ninh"). Tagore's works were widely translated into English, Dutch, German, Spanish, and other European languages by Czech Indologist [Vincenc Lesný](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vincenc_Lesný "Vincenc Lesný"), French Nobel laureate [André Gide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/André_Gide "André Gide"), Russian poet [Anna Akhmatova](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anna_Akhmatova "Anna Akhmatova"), former Turkish Prime Minister [Bülent Ecevit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bülent_Ecevit "Bülent Ecevit"), and others. In the United States, Tagore's lecturing circuits, particularly those of 1916–1917, were widely attended and wildly acclaimed. Some controversies involving Tagore, possibly fictive, trashed his popularity and sales in Japan and North America after the late 1920s, concluding with his "near total eclipse" outside Bengal. Yet a latent reverence of Tagore was discovered by an astonished [Salman Rushdie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salman_Rushdie "Salman Rushdie") during a trip to Nicaragua.

By way of translations, Tagore influenced Chileans [Pablo Neruda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pablo_Neruda "Pablo Neruda") and [Gabriela Mistral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gabriela_Mistral "Gabriela Mistral"); Mexican writer [Octavio Paz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octavio_Paz "Octavio Paz"); and Spaniards [José Ortega y Gasset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/José_Ortega_y_Gasset "José Ortega y Gasset"), [Zenobia Camprubí](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zenobia_Camprubí "Zenobia Camprubí"), and [Juan Ramón Jiménez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juan_Ramón_Jiménez "Juan Ramón Jiménez"). In the period 1914–1922, the Jiménez-Camprubí pair produced twenty-two Spanish translations of Tagore's English corpus; they heavily revised *The Crescent Moon* and other key titles. In these years, Jiménez developed "naked poetry". Ortega y Gasset wrote that "Tagore's wide appeal [owes to how] he speaks of longings for perfection that we all have [...] Tagore awakens a dormant sense of childish wonder, and he saturates the air with all kinds of enchanting promises for the reader, who [...] pays little attention to the deeper import of Oriental mysticism". Tagore's works circulated in free editions around 1920—alongside those of [Plato](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plato "Plato"), [Dante](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dante_Alighieri "Dante Alighieri"), [Cervantes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miguel_de_Cervantes "Miguel de Cervantes"), [Goethe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Wolfgang_von_Goethe "Johann Wolfgang von Goethe"), and [Tolstoy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leo_Tolstoy "Leo Tolstoy").

Tagore was deemed over-rated by some. [Graham Greene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graham_Greene "Graham Greene") doubted that "anyone but Mr. Yeats can still take his poems very seriously." Several prominent Western admirers—including Pound and, to a lesser extent, even Yeats—criticised Tagore's work. Yeats, unimpressed with his English translations, railed against that "Damn Tagore [...] We got out three good books, Sturge Moore and I, and then, because he thought it more important to see and know English than to be a great poet, he brought out sentimental rubbish and wrecked his reputation. Tagore does not know English, no Indian knows English." [William Radice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Radice "William Radice"), who "English[ed]" his poems, asked: "What is their place in world literature?" He saw him as "kind of counter-cultur[al]", bearing "a new kind of classicism" that would heal the "collapsed romantic confusion and chaos of the 20th century." The translated Tagore was "almost nonsensical", and subpar English offerings reduced his trans-national appeal:

> Anyone who knows Tagore's poems in their original Bengali cannot feel satisfied with any of the translations (made with or without Yeats's help). Even the translations of his prose works suffer, to some extent, from distortion. E.M. Forster noted [of] *The Home and the World* [that] '[t]he theme is so beautiful,' but the charms have 'vanished in translation,' or perhaps 'in an experiment that has not quite come off.'

— [Amartya Sen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amartya_Sen "Amartya Sen"), "Tagore and His India".

In October 1961 a blue plaque to Tagore was unveiled at Number 3, Villas on the Heath in Hampstead, to mark Tagore's visit in 1912 and to mark the centenary of Tagore's birth. The address was Tagore's home for a few months in the summer of 1912, during his third visit to England. The lodgings were found for him by the artist and writer Sir [William Rothenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Rothenstein "William Rothenstein"), who lived nearby at 11 Oak Hill Park.

In July 2025, a bronze bust of Tagore, sculpted by [Ram V. Sutar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ram_V._Sutar "Ram V. Sutar"), was unveiled in Trunk's Close, [Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edinburgh "Edinburgh"), opposite a bust of [Patrick Geddes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patrick_Geddes "Patrick Geddes") who was a friend and collaborator of Tagore.

- Rabindra Bhavan Museum, in [Santiniketan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santiniketan "Santiniketan"), India
- [Rabindra Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rabindra_Museum "Rabindra Museum"), in Mungpoo, near Kalimpong, India
- Patisar Rabindra Kacharibari, Patisar, [Atrai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atrai_Upazila "Atrai Upazila"), [Naogaon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naogaon_District "Naogaon District"), Bangladesh
- Pithavoge Rabindra Memorial Complex, Pithavoge, [Rupsha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rupsa_Upazila "Rupsa Upazila"), Khulna, Bangladesh
- [Rabindra Complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rabindra_Complex "Rabindra Complex"), Dakkhindihi village, [Phultala Upazila](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phultala_Upazila "Phultala Upazila"), [Khulna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khulna "Khulna"), Bangladesh

Jorasanko Thakur Bari ([Bengali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bengali_language "Bengali language"): *House of the [Thakurs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thakur_(Indian_title) "Thakur (Indian title)")*; anglicised to *Tagore*) in [Jorasanko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jorasanko "Jorasanko"), north of Kolkata, is the ancestral home of the Tagore family. It is currently located on the [Rabindra Bharati University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rabindra_Bharati_University "Rabindra Bharati University") campus at 6/4 Dwarakanath Tagore Lane Jorasanko, Kolkata 700007. It is the house in which Tagore was born, and also the place where he spent most of his childhood and where he died on 7 August 1941.

## In popular culture

- *[Rabindranath Tagore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rabindranath_Tagore_(film) "Rabindranath Tagore (film)")* is a 1961 Indian documentary film written and directed by [Satyajit Ray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satyajit_Ray "Satyajit Ray"), released during the birth centenary of Tagore. It was produced by the [Government of India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_of_India "Government of India")'s [Films Division](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Films_Division "Films Division").
- Serbian composer [Darinka Simic-Mitrovic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darinka_Simic-Mitrovic "Darinka Simic-Mitrovic") used Tagore's text for her song cycle *Gradinar* in 1962.
- In 1969, American composer [E. Anne Schwerdtfeger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E._Anne_Schwerdtfeger "E. Anne Schwerdtfeger") was commissioned to compose *Two Pieces*, a work for women's chorus based on text by Tagore.
- In Sukanta Roy's [Bengali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cinema_of_West_Bengal "Cinema of West Bengal") film *Chhelebela* (2002) [Jisshu Sengupta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jisshu_Sengupta "Jisshu Sengupta") portrayed Tagore.
- In Bandana Mukhopadhyay's Bengali film *Chirosakha He* (2007) Sayandip Bhattacharya played Tagore.
- In [Rituparno Ghosh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rituparno_Ghosh "Rituparno Ghosh")'s Bengali documentary film *Jeevan Smriti* (2011) [Samadarshi Dutta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samadarshi_Dutta "Samadarshi Dutta") played Tagore.
- In [Suman Ghosh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suman_Ghosh_(director) "Suman Ghosh (director)")'s Bengali film *[Kadambari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kadambari_(2015_film) "Kadambari (2015 film)")* (2015) [Parambrata Chatterjee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parambrata_Chatterjee "Parambrata Chatterjee") portrayed Tagore.
