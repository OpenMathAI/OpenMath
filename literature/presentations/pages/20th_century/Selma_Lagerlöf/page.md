**Selma Ottilia Lovisa Lagerlöf** ([/ˈlɑːɡərlɜːf, -lɜːv/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English"), [US](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_English "American English") also [/-lʌv, -ləv/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English"), Swedish: [[ˈsɛ̂lːma ˈlɑ̂ːɡɛˌɭøːv]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Swedish "Help:IPA/Swedish") [ⓘ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Sv-Selma_Lagerl%C3%B6f.ogg "File:Sv-Selma Lagerlöf.ogg"); 20 November 1858 – 16 March 1940) was a Swedish writer. She published her first novel, *[Gösta Berling's Saga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gösta_Berling's_Saga "Gösta Berling's Saga")*, at the age of 33. She was the first woman to win the [Nobel Prize in Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Literature "Nobel Prize in Literature"), which she was [awarded in 1909](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1909_Nobel_Prize_in_Literature "1909 Nobel Prize in Literature"). In 1914, she was the first woman to be granted a membership of the [Swedish Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swedish_Academy "Swedish Academy").

## Life

### Early years

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/04/Selma_Lagerlofs_Marbacka_at_Sunne.jpg/250px-Selma_Lagerlofs_Marbacka_at_Sunne.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Selma_Lagerlofs_Marbacka_at_Sunne.jpg)

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/af/Selma_Lagerl%C3%B6f_in_1881.jpg/250px-Selma_Lagerl%C3%B6f_in_1881.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Selma_Lagerlöf_in_1881.jpg)

Lagerlöf in 1881

Selma Ottilia Lovisa Lagerlöf was born on 20 November 1858 at [Mårbacka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mårbacka "Mårbacka"), [Värmland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Värmland "Värmland"), [Kingdoms of Sweden and Norway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_between_Sweden_and_Norway "Union between Sweden and Norway"). Lagerlöf was the daughter of Erik Gustaf Lagerlöf, a lieutenant in the Royal Värmland Regiment, and Louise Lagerlöf (*née* Wallroth), whose father was a well-to-do merchant and a [foundry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foundry "Foundry") owner (*brukspatron*). Lagerlöf was the couple's fifth child out of six. She was born with a [hip injury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hip_dysplasia_(human) "Hip dysplasia (human)"), which was caused by detachment in the hip joint. At the age of three and a half, a sickness left her lame in both legs, although she later recovered.

She was a quiet, serious child with a deep love of [reading](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reading_(process) "Reading (process)"). She wrote poetry but did not publish anything until later in life. Her grandmother helped raise her, often telling stories of fairytales and fantasy. Growing up, she was plain and slightly lame, and an account stated that the cross-country wanderings of Margarethe and Elisabet in *Gösta Berling's Saga* could be the author's compensatory fantasies. She received her schooling at home since the [*Folkskola*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volksschule "Volksschule") compulsory education system was not [fully developed yet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education_in_Sweden#History "Education in Sweden"). She studied English and French. After reading *Osceola* by [Thomas Mayne Reid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Mayne_Reid "Thomas Mayne Reid") at the age of seven, she decided she would be a writer when she grew up.

In 1875, Lagerlöf lived in the [Karlskoga Church Rectory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karlskoga_Church_Rectory "Karlskoga Church Rectory") alongside [Erik Tullius Hammargren](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erik_Tullius_Hammargren "Erik Tullius Hammargren") and his wife, Ottiliana Lagerlöf, who was her aunt, during which time she was one of Hammargren's confirmation students.

The sale of the Lagerlöf family's Mårbacka estate in 1884 had a serious impact on her development. Selma's father is said to have been an alcoholic, something she rarely discussed. Her father did not want Selma to continue her education or be involved with the women's movement. Later in life, she bought back her father's estate with the money she received for her Nobel Prize. Lagerlöf lived there for the rest of her life.

### Teaching life

Lagerlöf studied at the [Högre lärarinneseminariet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Högre_lärarinneseminariet "Högre lärarinneseminariet") in Stockholm from 1882 to 1885. She worked as a country schoolteacher at a high school for girls in [Landskrona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landskrona "Landskrona") from 1885 to 1895. Through her studies at the Royal Women's Superior Training Academy in [Stockholm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stockholm "Stockholm"), Lagerlöf reacted against the realism of contemporary Swedish-language writers such as [August Strindberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/August_Strindberg "August Strindberg"). She began her first novel, *[Gösta Berling's Saga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gösta_Berling's_Saga "Gösta Berling's Saga"),* while working as a teacher in Landskrona. Her first break as a writer came when she submitted the first chapters to a literary contest in the magazine *[Idun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idun_(magazine) "Idun (magazine)")*, and won a publishing contract for the whole book. At first, her writing only received mild reviews from critics. Once a popular critic, [Georg Brandes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georg_Brandes "Georg Brandes"), had given her positive reviews of the Danish translation, her popularity soared. She received financial support from [Fredrika Limnell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fredrika_Limnell "Fredrika Limnell"), who wished to enable her to concentrate on her writing.

## Literary career

A visit in 1900 to the [American Colony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Colony_(Jerusalem) "American Colony (Jerusalem)") in [Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerusalem "Jerusalem") became the inspiration for Lagerlöf's book by that name. The royal family and the Swedish Academy gave her substantial financial support to continue her passion. *Jerusalem* was also acclaimed by critics, who began comparing her to [Homer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homer "Homer") and [Shakespeare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakespeare "Shakespeare"), so that she became a popular figure both in Sweden and abroad. By 1895, she gave up her teaching to devote herself to her writing. With the help of proceeds from *Gösta Berling's Saga* and a scholarship and grant, she made two journeys, which were largely instrumental in providing material for her next novel. With her close friend [Sophie Elkan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sophie_Elkan "Sophie Elkan"), she traveled to Italy, and also to [Palestine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palestine_(region) "Palestine (region)") and Egypt. In Italy, a legend of a Christ Child figure that had been replaced with a false version inspired Lagerlöf's novel *Antikrists mirakler* (*The Miracles of the Antichrist*). Set in [Sicily](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sicily "Sicily"), the novel explores the interplay between Christian and socialist moral systems. However, most of Lagerlöf's stories were set in Värmland.

In 1902, Lagerlöf was asked by the National Teachers' Association to write a geography book for children. She wrote *Nils Holgerssons underbara resa genom Sverige* (*[The Wonderful Adventures of Nils](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Wonderful_Adventures_of_Nils "The Wonderful Adventures of Nils")*), a novel about a boy from the southernmost part of Sweden, who had been shrunk to the size of a thumb and who travelled on the back of a goose across the country. Lagerlöf mixed historical and geographical facts about the provinces of Sweden with the tale of the boy's adventures, detailing how he managed to return home and was restored to his normal size. The novel is one of Lagerlöf's most well-known books, and it has been translated into more than 30 languages.

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/25/S_Lagerloef-Valborg_Olander.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail_unscaled)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:S_Lagerloef-Valborg_Olander.jpg)

Lagerlöf with her friend and literary assistant [Valborg Olander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valborg_Olander "Valborg Olander"). Elkan was jealous of the relationship.

She moved to [Falun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falun "Falun") in 1897 and met [Valborg Olander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valborg_Olander "Valborg Olander"), who became her literary assistant and friend, but Elkan's jealousy of Olander was a complication in the relationship. Olander, a teacher, was also active in the growing [women's suffrage movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women's_suffrage_movement "Women's suffrage movement") in Sweden. Selma Lagerlöf herself was active as a speaker for the [National Association for Women's Suffrage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Association_for_Women's_Suffrage_(Sweden) "National Association for Women's Suffrage (Sweden)"), which was beneficial for the organisation because of the great respect which surrounded Lagerlöf, and she spoke at the International Suffrage Congress in Stockholm in June 1911, where she gave the opening address, as well as at the victory party of the Swedish suffrage movement after women suffrage had been granted in May 1919.

Selma Lagerlöf was a friend of the German-Jewish writer [Nelly Sachs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nelly_Sachs "Nelly Sachs"). Shortly before her death in 1940, Lagerlöf intervened with the Swedish royal family to secure the release of Sachs and Sachs' aged mother from Nazi Germany, on the last flight from Germany to Sweden, and their lifelong asylum in Stockholm.

## Personal life

### Relationships

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c7/Sophie_Elkan_%26_Selma_Lagerl%C3%B6f.jpg/250px-Sophie_Elkan_%26_Selma_Lagerl%C3%B6f.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Sophie_Elkan_&_Selma_Lagerlöf.jpg)

Lagerlöf with the writer [Sophie Elkan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sophie_Elkan "Sophie Elkan") (right)

In 1894, she met the Swedish writer [Sophie Elkan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sophie_Elkan "Sophie Elkan"), who became her friend and companion. Over many years, Elkan and Lagerlöf critiqued each other's work. Lagerlöf wrote that Elkan strongly influenced her work and that she often disagreed sharply with the direction Lagerlöf wanted to take in her books. Lagerlöf's letters to Elkan were published in 1993, titled *Du lär mig att bli fri* (*You Teach Me to Be Free*).

Beginning in the 1900s, she also had a close relationship with [Valborg Olander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valborg_Olander "Valborg Olander"), who had some influence as a literary adviser, agent, and secretary of sorts as well; their correspondence was published in 2006 as *En riktig författarhustru* (*A Proper Writer's Wife*). There appears to have been a strong rivalry between Elkan and Olander. Both relationships were close, emotional, exclusive and described in terms suggestive of love, the boundary between expressions of friendship and love being somewhat vague at the time. Still, it is primarily the surviving correspondence with Olander that contains passages implying decidedly erotic and physical passion, even though Lagerlöf took care to destroy many of the letters she found too risky. Homosexual relations between women were taboo as well as illegal in Sweden at the time, so none of the women involved ever revealed such a relationship publicly.

## Literary adaptations

In 1919, Lagerlöf sold all the movie rights to all of her as-yet unpublished works to Swedish Cinema Theatre ([Swedish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swedish_language "Swedish language"): *Svenska Biografteatern*), so over the years, many movie versions of her works were made. During the era of Swedish silent cinema, her works were used in film by [Victor Sjöström](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victor_Sjöström "Victor Sjöström"), [Mauritz Stiller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mauritz_Stiller "Mauritz Stiller"), and other Swedish film makers. Sjöström's retelling of Lagerlöf's tales about rural Swedish life, in which his camera recorded the detail of traditional village life and the Swedish landscape, provided the basis of some of the most poetic and memorable products of silent cinema. The [1921 film adaptation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Phantom_Carriage "The Phantom Carriage") of *[Thy Soul Shall Bear Witness!](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thy_Soul_Shall_Bear_Witness! "Thy Soul Shall Bear Witness!")* (*Körkarlen*) was critically acclaimed and influential within cinema. *[Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerusalem_(Lagerlöf_novel) "Jerusalem (Lagerlöf novel)")* was adapted in 1996 into the internationally acclaimed [film of the same name](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerusalem_(1996_film) "Jerusalem (1996 film)").

## Awards

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/88/1959_CPA_2284.jpg/250px-1959_CPA_2284.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:1959_CPA_2284.jpg)

Lagerlöf on a 1959 [postage stamp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postage_stamp "Postage stamp") of the [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union "Soviet Union")

On 10 December 1909, Selma Lagerlöf won the Nobel Prize "in appreciation of the [lofty idealism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lofty_idealism "Lofty idealism"), vivid imagination, and spiritual perception that characterize her writings", but the decision was preceded by harsh internal power struggle within the [Swedish Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swedish_Academy "Swedish Academy"), the body that awards the Nobel Prize in Literature. During her acceptance speech, she told a story of her father, as she 'visited him in heaven'. In the story, she asks her father for help with the debt she owes and her father explains the debt is from all the people who supported her throughout her career. In 1904, the academy had awarded her its great gold medal, and in 1914, she also became a member of the academy. For both the academy membership and her Nobel literature prize, she was the first woman to be so honored. She was awarded the [Litteris et Artibus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Litteris_et_Artibus "Litteris et Artibus") in 1909 and the [Illis quorum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illis_quorum "Illis quorum") in 1926.

In 1907, she received the honorary degree of [doctor of letters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LL.D. "LL.D.") (*filosofie hedersdoktor*) from [Uppsala University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uppsala_University "Uppsala University"). In 1928, she received an honorary doctorate from the [University of Greifswald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Greifswald "University of Greifswald")'s [Faculty of Arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Greifswald_Faculty_of_Arts "University of Greifswald Faculty of Arts"). At the start of [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II"), she sent her Nobel Prize medal and gold medal from the Swedish Academy to the government of Finland to help raise money to fight the [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union "Soviet Union"). The Finnish government was so touched that it raised the necessary money by other means and returned her medal to her.

## Legacy

In 1983, the [Sunne Municipality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunne_Municipality "Sunne Municipality") in [Värmland County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Värmland_County "Värmland County") founded the [Selma Lagerlöf Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selma_Lagerlöf_Prize "Selma Lagerlöf Prize"), awarded to an author writing in the spirit of Lagerlöf. Recipients receive 100,000 [Swedish kronor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swedish_kronor "Swedish kronor").

In 1991, she became the first woman to be depicted on a Swedish banknote, when the first 20-kronor note was released.

A flight of steps connecting two streets is named after her in Jerusalem, Israel.

Two hotels are named after her in Östra Ämtervik in Sunne, and her home, [Mårbacka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mårbacka "Mårbacka"), is preserved as a museum.
