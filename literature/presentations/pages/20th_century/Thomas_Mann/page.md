**Paul Thomas Mann** (6 June 1875 – 12 August 1955) was a German novelist, short story writer, social critic, philanthropist, essayist, and the [1929 Nobel Prize in Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1929_Nobel_Prize_in_Literature "1929 Nobel Prize in Literature") laureate. His highly symbolic and ironic epic novels and novellas are noted for their insight into the psychology of the artist and the intellectual. His analysis and critique of the European and German soul used modern versions of German and Biblical stories, as well as the ideas of [Johann Wolfgang von Goethe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Wolfgang_von_Goethe "Johann Wolfgang von Goethe"), [Friedrich Nietzsche](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Nietzsche "Friedrich Nietzsche"), and [Arthur Schopenhauer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Schopenhauer "Arthur Schopenhauer").

The [Mann family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mann_family "Mann family") was part of the [Hanseatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanseaten_(class) "Hanseaten (class)") class. He portrayed both his family and the influential class in his first novel, *[Buddenbrooks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddenbrooks "Buddenbrooks")* (1901). Other major novels include *[The Magic Mountain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Magic_Mountain "The Magic Mountain")* (1924), the tetralogy *[Joseph and His Brothers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_and_His_Brothers "Joseph and His Brothers")* (1933–1943), and *[Doctor Faustus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_Faustus_(novel) "Doctor Faustus (novel)")* (1947). He also wrote short stories and [novellas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Novella "Novella"), including *[Death in Venice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Death_in_Venice "Death in Venice")* (1912).

His older brother was novelist [Heinrich Mann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heinrich_Mann "Heinrich Mann"). Three of Mann's six children – [Erika Mann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erika_Mann "Erika Mann"), [Klaus Mann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klaus_Mann "Klaus Mann") and [Golo Mann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golo_Mann "Golo Mann") – also became significant German writers.

When [Adolf Hitler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adolf_Hitler "Adolf Hitler") [came to power in 1933](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adolf_Hitler's_rise_to_power "Adolf Hitler's rise to power"), Mann fled to Switzerland. When [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II") broke out in 1939, he moved to the United States, returning to Switzerland after the war in 1952. Mann is one of the best-known exponents of the so-called *[Exilliteratur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exilliteratur "Exilliteratur")*, [German literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contemporary_German_literature "Contemporary German literature") written in exile by those who opposed the Hitler regime.

## Life

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/1e/Buddenbrookhaus_Luebeck_%28Germany%29_by_night.jpg/250px-Buddenbrookhaus_Luebeck_%28Germany%29_by_night.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Buddenbrookhaus_Luebeck_(Germany)_by_night.jpg)

House of the [Mann family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mann_family "Mann family") in Lübeck (*Buddenbrookhaus*), where Thomas Mann grew up; now a family museum

Paul Thomas Mann was born to a [Hanseatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanseaten_(class) "Hanseaten (class)") family in [Lübeck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lübeck "Lübeck"), the second son of Thomas Johann Heinrich Mann (a senator and a [grain merchant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grain_trade "Grain trade")) and his wife [Júlia da Silva Bruhns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Júlia_da_Silva_Bruhns "Júlia da Silva Bruhns"), a Brazilian woman of German, Portuguese and Native Brazilian ancestry, who emigrated to Germany with her family when she was seven years old. His mother was [Roman Catholic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Catholic "Roman Catholic") but Mann was baptised into his father's [Lutheran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lutheranism "Lutheranism") religion. Mann's father died in 1891, and after that his trading firm was liquidated. The family subsequently moved to [Munich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Munich "Munich"). Mann first studied science at a Lübeck *[Gymnasium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gymnasium_(school) "Gymnasium (school)")* (secondary school), then attended the [Ludwig-Maximilians-Universität München](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludwig-Maximilians-Universität_München "Ludwig-Maximilians-Universität München") as well as the [Technische Hochschule München](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technische_Hochschule_München "Technische Hochschule München"), where, in preparation for a journalism career, he studied history, economics, art history and literature.

Mann lived in Munich from 1891 until 1933, with the exception of a year spent in [Palestrina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palestrina,_Lazio "Palestrina, Lazio"), Italy, with his elder brother, the novelist [Heinrich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heinrich_Mann "Heinrich Mann"). Thomas worked at the South German Fire Insurance Company in 1894–95. His career as a writer began when he wrote for the magazine *[Simplicissimus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simplicissimus "Simplicissimus")*. Mann's first short story, "[Little Herr Friedemann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_Herr_Friedemann "Little Herr Friedemann")" (*Der Kleine Herr Friedemann*), was published in 1898.

In 1905, Mann married [Katia Pringsheim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Katia_Mann "Katia Mann"), who came from a wealthy, secular Jewish industrialist family. She later joined the [Lutheran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lutheran "Lutheran") church. The couple had six children: [Erika](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erika_Mann "Erika Mann") (b. 1905), [Klaus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klaus_Mann "Klaus Mann") (b. 1906), [Golo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golo_Mann "Golo Mann") (b. 1909), [Monika](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monika_Mann "Monika Mann") (b. 1910), [Elisabeth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elisabeth_Mann_Borgese "Elisabeth Mann Borgese") (b. 1918) and [Michael](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Mann_(scholar) "Michael Mann (scholar)") (b. 1919).

Due to the [Pringsheim family's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Pringsheim "Alfred Pringsheim") wealth, Katia Mann was able to purchase a summer property in [Bad Tölz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Tölz "Bad Tölz") in 1908, on which they built a country house the following year, which they kept until 1917. In 1914 they also purchased a villa in Munich (at Poschinger Str in the borough of [Bogenhausen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bogenhausen "Bogenhausen"), today 10 Thomas-Mann-Allee) where they lived until 1933.

### Pre-war and Second World War period

In 1912, Katia was treated for what was incorrectly diagnosed as tuberculosis for a few months in a [sanatorium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanatorium "Sanatorium") in [Davos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Davos "Davos"), Switzerland, where Thomas Mann visited her for a few weeks. This inspired him to write his 1924 novel *[The Magic Mountain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Magic_Mountain "The Magic Mountain")*. He was also appalled by the risk of international confrontation between Germany and France, following the [Agadir Crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agadir_Crisis "Agadir Crisis") in Morocco, and later by the outbreak of the [First World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_World_War "First World War"). The novel ends with the outbreak of this war, in which the hero enlists, with his survival uncertain.

As a "German patriot", Mann had the proceeds from their summer house used in 1917 to subscribe to war bonds, which lost their face value after the war was lost. His father-in-law did the same, which caused a loss of a major part of the Pringsheim family's wealth. The disastrous [inflation of 1923 and 1924](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperinflation_in_the_Weimar_Republic "Hyperinflation in the Weimar Republic") resulted in additional high losses. The sales success of his novel *The Magic Mountain*, published in 1924, improved his financial situation again, as did the award of the [Nobel Prize in Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Literature "Nobel Prize in Literature") in 1929. He used the prize money to build a [cottage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:House_of_Thomas_Mann_in_Nida_02.jpg "File:House of Thomas Mann in Nida 02.jpg") in the fishing village of [Nida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nida,_Lithuania "Nida, Lithuania"), Lithuania on the [Curonian Spit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curonian_Spit "Curonian Spit"), where there was a German art colony and where he spent the summers of 1930–1932 working on *[Joseph and His Brothers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_and_His_Brothers "Joseph and His Brothers")*. Today, the cottage is a cultural center dedicated to him, with a small memorial exhibition.

In February 1933, having finished a book tour to Amsterdam, Brussels and Paris, Thomas Mann moved to [Arosa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arosa "Arosa") (Switzerland) when [Hitler took power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi_seizure_of_power "Nazi seizure of power"), and Mann heard from his eldest children, Klaus and Erika in Munich, that it would not be safe for him to return to Germany. His political views (see [chapter below](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Mann#Political_views)) had made him an enemy of the Nazis. He was doubtful at first, because, with a certain naïveté, he could not imagine the violence of the overthrow and the persecution of opponents of the regime, but the children insisted, and their advice later turned out to be accurate when it emerged that even their driver-caretaker had become an [informant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Informant "Informant") and that Mann's immediate arrest would have been very likely. The family (except these two children, who went to Amsterdam) emigrated to [Küsnacht](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Küsnacht "Küsnacht"), near [Zürich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zürich "Zürich"), Switzerland, after a stopover in [Sanary-sur-Mer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanary-sur-Mer "Sanary-sur-Mer"), France. The son Golo managed, at great risk, to smuggle the already completed chapters of the *Joseph* novel and the (sensitive) diaries into Switzerland. The [Bavarian Political Police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bavarian_Political_Police "Bavarian Political Police") searched Mann's house in Munich and confiscated the house, its inventory and the bank accounts. At the same time, an [arrest warrant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protective_custody_(Nazi_Germany) "Protective custody (Nazi Germany)") was issued. Mann was also no longer able to use his holiday home in Lithuania because it was only a few hundred yards from the German border and he seemed to be at risk there. When all members of the Poetry Section at the [Prussian Academy of Arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prussian_Academy_of_Arts "Prussian Academy of Arts") were asked to make a declaration of loyalty to the National Socialist government, Mann declared his resignation on 17 March 1933.

The writer's freedom of movement was reduced when his German passport expired. The Manns traveled to the United States for the first two times in 1934 and 1935. There was great interest in the prominent writer; the authorities allowed him entry without a valid passport. He received [Czechoslovak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Czechoslovakia "Czechoslovakia") citizenship and a passport in 1936, even though he had never lived there. A few weeks later the German citizenship of Mann, his wife Katia, and their children Golo, Elisabeth and Michael were revoked, and the Nazi government expropriated the family home in Munich, which [Reinhard Heydrich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reinhard_Heydrich "Reinhard Heydrich") in particular insisted on. It had already been confiscated and forcibly rented out in 1933. In December 1936, the [University of Bonn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Bonn "University of Bonn") withdrew the honorary doctorate awarded to Mann in 1919; on 13 December 1946, after the defeat of Nazi Germany, it was reinstated.

In 1939, following the [German occupation of Czechoslovakia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_occupation_of_Czechoslovakia "German occupation of Czechoslovakia"), Mann emigrated to the United States, while his in-laws only managed, thanks to high-ranking connections, to leave Germany for Zurich in October 1939. The Manns moved to [Princeton, New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton,_New_Jersey "Princeton, New Jersey"), where they lived on 65 Stockton Street and he began to teach at [Princeton University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University "Princeton University"). In 1941 he was designated consultant in German Literature, later Fellow in Germanic Literature, at the [Library of Congress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Library_of_Congress "Library of Congress"). In 1942, the Mann family moved to [1550 San Remo Drive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Mann_House "Thomas Mann House") in the [Pacific Palisades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pacific_Palisades,_Los_Angeles "Pacific Palisades, Los Angeles") neighborhood of Los Angeles, California. The Manns were prominent members of the German expatriate community of Los Angeles and frequently met other émigrés at [the house of Salka and Bertold Viertel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salka_and_Berthold_Viertel_House "Salka and Berthold Viertel House") in Santa Monica, and at the [Villa Aurora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Villa_Aurora "Villa Aurora"), the home of fellow German exile [Lion Feuchtwanger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lion_Feuchtwanger "Lion Feuchtwanger"). Thomas Mann's always difficult relationship with his brother [Heinrich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heinrich_Mann "Heinrich Mann"), who envied Thomas's success and wealth and also differed politically, hardly improved when the latter arrived in California, poor and sickly, in need of support. On 23 June 1944, Thomas Mann was naturalized as a citizen of the United States. The Manns lived in Los Angeles until 1952.

### Anti-Nazi broadcasts

The outbreak of World War II, on 1 September 1939, prompted Mann to offer anti-Nazi speeches in German to the German people via the [BBC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC "BBC"). In October 1940, he began monthly broadcasts, recorded in the U.S. and flown to London, where the [BBC German Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_German_Service "BBC German Service") broadcast them to Germany on the [longwave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longwave "Longwave") band. In these eight-minute addresses, Mann condemned Hitler and his "paladins" as crude philistines completely out of touch with European culture. In one noted speech, he said: "The war is horrible, but it has the advantage of keeping Hitler from making speeches about culture."

Mann was one of the few publicly active opponents of Nazism among German expatriates in the U.S. In a BBC broadcast of 30 December 1945, after the defeat of Germany, Mann said he understood why those peoples that had suffered from the Nazi regime would embrace the idea of [German collective guilt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_collective_guilt "German collective guilt"). But he also thought that many enemies might now have second thoughts about "revenge". And he expressed regret that such judgement cannot be based on the individual:

> Those, whose world became grey a long time ago when they realized what mountains of hate towered over Germany; those, who a long time ago imagined during sleepless nights how terrible would be the revenge on Germany for the inhuman deeds of the Nazis, cannot help but view with wretchedness all that is being done to Germans by the Russians, Poles, or Czechs as nothing other than a mechanical and inevitable reaction to the crimes that the people have committed as a nation, in which unfortunately individual justice, or the guilt or innocence of the individual, can play no part.

### Last years

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/97/Thomas_Mann_Grave_2005-03-26.jpeg/250px-Thomas_Mann_Grave_2005-03-26.jpeg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Thomas_Mann_Grave_2005-03-26.jpeg)

The grave of Thomas, Katia, Erika, Monika, Michael, and Elisabeth Mann, in [Kilchberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilchberg,_Zürich "Kilchberg, Zürich"), Switzerland. The gravestone is modeled on a Roman [stele](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stele "Stele").

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/fb/Bundesarchiv_Bild_183-32373-0006%2C_Schweiz%2C_Beisetzung_Thomas_Mann.jpg/250px-Bundesarchiv_Bild_183-32373-0006%2C_Schweiz%2C_Beisetzung_Thomas_Mann.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Bundesarchiv_Bild_183-32373-0006,_Schweiz,_Beisetzung_Thomas_Mann.jpg)

Mann's funeral, 1955

With the start of the [Cold War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cold_War "Cold War"), he was increasingly frustrated by rising [McCarthyism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McCarthyism "McCarthyism"). As a "suspected [communist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communist "Communist")", he was required to testify to the [House Un-American Activities Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_Un-American_Activities_Committee "House Un-American Activities Committee"), where he was termed "one of the world's foremost apologists for Stalin and company". He was listed by HUAC as being "affiliated with various peace organizations or Communist fronts". Being in his own words a non-communist, rather than an [anti-communist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-communist "Anti-communist"), Mann openly opposed the allegations: "As an American citizen of German birth, I finally testify that I am painfully familiar with certain political trends. Spiritual intolerance, political inquisitions, and declining legal security, and all this in the name of an alleged 'state of emergency'. ... That is how it started in Germany." As Mann joined protests against the jailing of the [Hollywood Ten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hollywood_Ten "Hollywood Ten") and the firing of schoolteachers suspected of being Communists, he found "the media had been closed to him". Finally, he was forced to quit his position as Consultant in Germanic Literature at the [Library of Congress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Library_of_Congress "Library of Congress"), and in 1952, he returned to Europe, to live in [Kilchberg, near Zürich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilchberg,_Zürich "Kilchberg, Zürich"), Switzerland. Here he initially lived in a rented house and bought his last house there in 1954 (which later his widow and then their son Golo lived in until their deaths). He never again lived in Germany, though he regularly traveled there. His most important German visit was in 1949, at the 200th birthday of [Johann Wolfgang von Goethe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Wolfgang_von_Goethe "Johann Wolfgang von Goethe"), attending celebrations in [Frankfurt am Main](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frankfurt_am_Main "Frankfurt am Main") (then [West Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Germany "West Germany")) and [Weimar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weimar "Weimar") (then [East Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Germany "East Germany")), as a statement that German culture extended beyond the new political borders. He also visited Lübeck, where he saw his parents' house, which was partially destroyed by the [bombing of Lübeck in World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bombing_of_Lübeck_in_World_War_II "Bombing of Lübeck in World War II") (and only later rebuilt). The city welcomed him warmly, but the patrician [hanseatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanseaten_(class) "Hanseaten (class)") families gave him a reserved welcome, since the publication of *Buddenbrooks* they had resented him for daring to describe their caste with some mockery, as they at least felt about it.

Along with [Albert Einstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Einstein "Albert Einstein"), Mann was one of the sponsors of the [Peoples' World Convention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peoples'_World_Convention "Peoples' World Convention") (PWC), also known as Peoples' World Constituent Assembly (PWCA), which took place in 1950–51 at Palais Electoral, [Geneva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geneva "Geneva"), Switzerland.

### Death

Following his 80th birthday, Mann went on vacation to [Noordwijk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noordwijk "Noordwijk") in the Netherlands. On 18 July 1955, he began to experience pain and unilateral swelling in his left leg. The condition of [thrombophlebitis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thrombophlebitis "Thrombophlebitis") was diagnosed by Dr. Mulders from Leiden and confirmed by Dr. [Wilhelm Löffler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilhelm_Löffler_(doctor) "Wilhelm Löffler (doctor)"). Mann was transported to a Zürich hospital, but soon developed a state of [shock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shock_(circulatory) "Shock (circulatory)"). On 12 August 1955, he died. Postmortem, his condition was found to have been misdiagnosed. The pathologic diagnosis, made by Christoph Hedinger, showed he had actually suffered a perforated [iliac artery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iliac_artery "Iliac artery") [aneurysm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aneurysm "Aneurysm") resulting in a [retroperitoneal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retroperitoneal "Retroperitoneal") [hematoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hematoma "Hematoma"), compression and [thrombosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thrombosis "Thrombosis") of the iliac vein. (At that time, lifesaving vascular surgery had not been developed.) On 16 August 1955, Thomas Mann was buried in the Kilchberg village cemetery.

### Legacy

Mann's work influenced many later authors, such as [Yukio Mishima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yukio_Mishima "Yukio Mishima"). [Joseph Campbell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Campbell "Joseph Campbell") also stated in an interview with Bill Moyers that Mann was one of his mentors. Many institutions are named in his honour, for instance the [Thomas Mann Gymnasium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Mann_Gymnasium_(Budapest) "Thomas Mann Gymnasium (Budapest)") of [Budapest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Budapest "Budapest").

[Blanche Knopf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blanche_Knopf "Blanche Knopf") of [Alfred A. Knopf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_A._Knopf "Alfred A. Knopf") publishing house was introduced to Mann by [H.L. Mencken](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H._L._Mencken "H. L. Mencken") while on a book-buying trip to Europe. After *Buddenbrooks* proved successful in its first year, the Knopfs sent him an unexpected bonus. Later in the 1930s, Blanche helped arrange for Mann and his family to emigrate to America.

### Nobel Prize in Literature

Mann was awarded the [Nobel Prize in Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Literature "Nobel Prize in Literature") in 1929, after he had been nominated by [Anders Österling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anders_Österling "Anders Österling"), member of the [Swedish Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swedish_Academy "Swedish Academy"), principally in recognition of his popular achievements with *[Buddenbrooks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddenbrooks "Buddenbrooks")* (1901), *[The Magic Mountain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Magic_Mountain "The Magic Mountain")* (*Der Zauberberg*, 1924), and his numerous short stories. (Due to the personal taste of an influential committee member, only *Buddenbrooks* was cited at any great length.) Based on Mann's own family, *Buddenbrooks* relates the decline of a merchant family in Lübeck over the course of four generations. *The Magic Mountain* (*Der Zauberberg*, 1924) follows an engineering student who, planning to visit his [tubercular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuberculosis "Tuberculosis") cousin at a Swiss [sanatorium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanatorium "Sanatorium") for only three weeks, finds his departure from the sanatorium delayed. During that time, he confronts medicine and the way it looks at the body and encounters a variety of characters, who play out ideological conflicts and discontents of contemporary European civilization. The tetralogy *Joseph and His Brothers* is an epic novel written over a period of sixteen years and is one of the largest and most significant works in Mann's oeuvre. Later novels included *[Lotte in Weimar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lotte_in_Weimar:_The_Beloved_Returns "Lotte in Weimar: The Beloved Returns")* (1939), in which Mann returned to the world of Goethe's novel *[The Sorrows of Young Werther](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Sorrows_of_Young_Werther "The Sorrows of Young Werther")* (1774); *[Doctor Faustus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_Faustus_(novel) "Doctor Faustus (novel)")* (1947), the story of the fictitious composer Adrian Leverkühn and the corruption of [German culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture_of_Germany "Culture of Germany") in the years before and during World War II; and *[Confessions of Felix Krull](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confessions_of_Felix_Krull "Confessions of Felix Krull")* (*Bekenntnisse des Hochstaplers Felix Krull*, 1954), which was unfinished at Mann's death. These later works prompted two members of the [Swedish Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swedish_Academy "Swedish Academy") to nominate Mann for the Nobel Prize in Literature a second time, in 1948.

## Influence

The writer [Theodor Fontane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodor_Fontane "Theodor Fontane"), who died in 1898, had a particular stylistic influence on Thomas Mann. Of course, Mann always admired and emulated [Goethe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goethe "Goethe"), the German "poet prince". The Danish author [Herman Bang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herman_Bang "Herman Bang"), with whom he felt a kindred spirit, had a certain influence, especially on the novellas. The pessimistic philosopher [Arthur Schopenhauer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Schopenhauer "Arthur Schopenhauer") provided philosophical inspiration for the *Buddenbrooks'* narrative of decline, especially with his two-volume work *[The World as Will and Representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_World_as_Will_and_Representation "The World as Will and Representation")*, which Mann studied closely while writing the novel. Russian narrators should also be mentioned, he admired the [Russian Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Literature "Russian Literature")'s ability for self-criticism, at least during the 19th century, in [Nikolai Gogol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nikolai_Gogol "Nikolai Gogol"), [Ivan Goncharov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivan_Goncharov "Ivan Goncharov") and [Ivan Turgenev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivan_Turgenev "Ivan Turgenev"). Mann believed that in order to make a [bourgeois revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Revolution "Russian Revolution"), the Russians had to forget [Dostoevsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dostoevsky "Dostoevsky"). He particularly loved [Leo Tolstoy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leo_Tolstoy "Leo Tolstoy"), whom he considered an anarchist and whom he lovingly and mockingly admired for his "courage to be boring."

Throughout Mann's [Dostoevsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dostoevsky "Dostoevsky") essay, he finds parallels between the Russian and the sufferings of [Friedrich Nietzsche](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Nietzsche "Friedrich Nietzsche"). Speaking of Nietzsche, he says, "his personal feelings initiate him into those of the criminal ... in general all creative originality, all artist nature in the broadest sense of the word, does the same. It was the French painter and sculptor [Degas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edgar_Degas "Edgar Degas") who said that an artist must approach his work in the spirit of the criminal about to commit a crime." Nietzsche's influence on Mann runs deep in his work, especially in Nietzsche's views on decay and the proposed fundamental connection between sickness and creativity. Mann believed that disease should not be regarded as wholly negative. In his essay on Dostoevsky, we find: "but after all and above all it depends on who is diseased, who mad, who epileptic or paralytic: an average dull-witted man, in whose illness any intellectual or cultural aspect is non-existent; or a Nietzsche or Dostoyevsky. In their case something comes out in illness that is more important and conducive to life and growth than any medical guaranteed health or sanity.... [I]n other words: certain conquests made by the soul and the mind are impossible without disease, madness, crime of the spirit."

## Thematic and stylistic focuses

Many of Thomas Mann's works have the following similarities:

- A "gravely-mischievous" style that is very popular with readers, with superficial solemnity and an underlying ironic humor, mostly benevolent, never drastic or bitter and only rarely degenerating into the macabre. Thomas Mann took this "mild irony" from his literary predecessor and role model [Theodor Fontane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodor_Fontane "Theodor Fontane"), at first in *[Buddenbrooks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddenbrooks "Buddenbrooks")* where it is modified into local sedateness through [Low German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low_German "Low German") sprinkles and quotations. He continued this style, with variations, throughout his life (the contemporary, much more avant-garde author [Alfred Döblin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Döblin "Alfred Döblin") mocked that Mann had "elevated the *pressed crease* to a style principle"). In *[Joseph and His Brothers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_and_His_Brothers "Joseph and His Brothers")* the tone takes on something fairytale-biblical, but here too the irony often shines through. In *[Doctor Faustus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_Faustus_(novel) "Doctor Faustus (novel)")*, Thomas Mann adopts a predominantly serious tone in view of the sinister theme, although the critical irony does not completely disappear there either, for which the description of the nationalist milieu in the [Weimar Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weimar_Republic "Weimar Republic") in particular provides ample reason.
- The amusingly entertaining, mostly ironic descriptions of contemporaries as well as people of the past and their views and lifestyles based on his own observations or research, often very detailed, similar to his contemporary [Marcel Proust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcel_Proust "Marcel Proust"), are combined with various profound components: in *Buddenbrooks* with the motif of economic and spiritual decline of a family, in *[The Magic Mountain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Magic_Mountain "The Magic Mountain")* with the philosophical disputes of the time before the First World War, in *[Lotte in Weimar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lotte_in_Weimar:_The_Beloved_Returns "Lotte in Weimar: The Beloved Returns")* with the circumstances of [Goethe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goethe "Goethe")'s [Weimar Classicism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weimar_Classicism "Weimar Classicism") and the complicated effect between real and literary love, in *Joseph and his brothers* with biblical-mythical motifs and the question of origins as well as the eternal recurrence of the same "mythical" stories, in *Doctor Faustus* with the historical-political circumstances of the rise, success and fall of Nazism.
- Attachment to home regions: Lübeck (Buddenbrooks, Tonio Kröger) and Munich (Gladius Dei, At the Prophet, Disorder and Early Suffering) are in the foreground of important works.
- [![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d2/Bundesarchiv_Bild_183-R15883%2C_Thomas_Mann.jpg/250px-Bundesarchiv_Bild_183-R15883%2C_Thomas_Mann.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Bundesarchiv_Bild_183-R15883,_Thomas_Mann.jpg)

  Thomas Mann with his [gramophone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gramophone "Gramophone") in his Munich house (1932)

  Classical music already plays a central role in *Buddenbrooks* and *[Tristan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tristan_(novella) "Tristan (novella)")* (Mann loved [Richard Wagner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Wagner "Richard Wagner")'s operas and, in his 1933 essay *The Suffering and Greatness of Richard Wagner*, defended him against the Nazis' attempt to appropriate Wagner as a nationalist icon) and [Neue Musik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neue_Musik "Neue Musik") plays the main role in *Doctor Faustus* (about which Mann sought advice from [Theodor W. Adorno](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodor_W._Adorno "Theodor W. Adorno") and gave his hero's music features of [Arnold Schoenberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arnold_Schoenberg "Arnold Schoenberg")'s compositional style).
- Central to Thomas Mann's thoughts and work is the mutual relationship between art and life: *Ambiguity as a system* is also the title of an essay.
- Conscientiousness: Thomas Mann always wrote his works after long and thorough research into the facts and the atmospheric circumstances.
- Political commitment (see below: Political views): His – mostly indirect – commitment runs through many of his works, from the *Buddenbrooks* (covering the revolutionary and counter-revolutionary shifts of the entire 19th century) to *[Mario and the Magician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mario_and_the_Magician "Mario and the Magician")* (mocking the atmosphere of 1920s [Italian fascism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_fascism "Italian fascism")) to *Doctor Faustus*. In contrast to his brother Heinrich and his children Erika and Klaus, Thomas Mann at first advocated a rather conservative stance, especially in the *[Reflections of a Nonpolitical Man](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflections_of_a_Nonpolitical_Man "Reflections of a Nonpolitical Man")* (1918), later and in his major works, however, a moderate, centrist and occasionally progressive attitude, while they were more "left-leaning".
- Homoerotic allusions are also common and recur in many works (see below: [Sexuality and literary work](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Mann#Sexuality_and_literary_work)).

During World War I, Mann supported the conservatism of [Kaiser Wilhelm II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaiser_Wilhelm_II "Kaiser Wilhelm II"), attacked liberalism, and supported the war effort, calling the [Great War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_War "Great War") "a purification, a liberation, an enormous hope". In his 600-page-long work *[Reflections of a Nonpolitical Man](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflections_of_a_Nonpolitical_Man "Reflections of a Nonpolitical Man")* (1918), Mann presented his conservative, anti-modernist philosophy: spiritual tradition over material progress, German patriotism over egalitarian internationalism, and rooted culture over rootless civilisation.

In "[On the German Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_the_German_Republic "On the German Republic")" (*Von Deutscher Republik*, 1922), Mann called upon German intellectuals to support the new [Weimar Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weimar_Republic "Weimar Republic"). The work was delivered at the Beethovensaal in Berlin on 13 October 1922, and published in *[Die neue Rundschau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Die_neue_Rundschau "Die neue Rundschau")* in November 1922. In the work, Mann developed his eccentric defence of the Republic based on extensive close readings of [Novalis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Novalis "Novalis") and [Walt Whitman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walt_Whitman "Walt Whitman"). Also in 1921, he wrote an essay *Mind and Money* in which he made a very open assessment of his family background: "In any case, I am personally indebted to the capitalist world order from the past, which is why it will never be appropriate for me to spit on it as it is *à la mode* these days". Thereafter, his political views gradually shifted toward [liberal-left](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberal-left "Liberal-left"). He especially embraced democratic principles when the Weimar Republic was established.

Mann initially gave his support to the left-liberal [German Democratic Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Democratic_Party "German Democratic Party") before urging unity behind the [Social Democrats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_Democratic_Party_of_Germany "Social Democratic Party of Germany"), probably less for ideological reasons, but because he only trusted it to provide sufficient mass and resistance to the growing [Nazism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazism "Nazism"). In 1930, he gave a public address in Berlin titled *An Appeal to Reason*, in which he strongly denounced Nazism and encouraged resistance by the working class. This was followed by numerous essays and lectures in which he attacked the [Nazis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi_Party "Nazi Party"). At the same time, he expressed increasing sympathy for [socialist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialist "Socialist") ideas. When the Nazis came to power in 1933, Mann and his wife were on holiday in Switzerland. Due to his strident denunciations of Nazi policies, his son Klaus advised him not to return. In contrast to those of his brother Heinrich and his son Klaus, Mann's books were not among those burnt publicly by Hitler's regime in May 1933, possibly since he had been the Nobel laureate in literature for 1929. In 1936, the Nazi government officially revoked his German citizenship.

During the war, Mann made a series of anti-Nazi radio-speeches, published as *[Listen, Germany!](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Listen,_Germany! "Listen, Germany!")* in 1943. They were recorded on tape in the United States and then sent to the United Kingdom, where the [British Broadcasting Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Broadcasting_Corporation "British Broadcasting Corporation") transmitted them, hoping to reach German listeners.

### Views on Soviet communism and German Nazism

Mann expressed his belief in the collection of letters written in exile, *Listen, Germany!* (Hör zu, Deutschland!) that equating [Soviet communism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_communism "Soviet communism") with Nazi fascism on the basis that both are [totalitarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Totalitarian "Totalitarian") systems was either superficial or insincere in showing a preference for [Nazism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazism "Nazism"). He clarified this view during a German press interview in July 1949, declaring that he was not a communist but that communism at least had some relation to ideals of humanity and of a better future. He said that the transition of the [communist revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communist_revolution "Communist revolution") into an autocratic regime was a tragedy while Nazism was only "devilish [nihilism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nihilism "Nihilism")".

## Sexuality and literary work

Mann's diaries reveal his struggles with his [sexuality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sexuality "Sexuality"), with his attraction to men finding frequent reflection in his works, most prominently through the obsession of the elderly Aschenbach for the 14-year-old Polish boy Tadzio in the novella *[Death in Venice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Death_in_Venice "Death in Venice")* (*Der Tod in Venedig*, 1912). [Anthony Heilbut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthony_Heilbut "Anthony Heilbut")'s biography *Thomas Mann: Eros and Literature* (1997) uncovered the centrality of Mann's sexuality to his oeuvre. [Gilbert Adair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gilbert_Adair "Gilbert Adair")'s work *The Real Tadzio* (2001) describes how, in 1911, Mann had stayed at the [Grand Hôtel des Bains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Hôtel_des_Bains "Grand Hôtel des Bains") on the [Venice Lido](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venice_Lido "Venice Lido") with his wife and brother, when he became enraptured by the angelic figure of [Władysław (Władzio) Moes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Władysław_Moes "Władysław Moes"), a 10-year-old Polish boy ([the real Tadzio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Death_in_Venice#The_real_Tadzio "Death in Venice")). While the story found acclaim, his wife would later recall that her own uncle was disgusted to hear of this attraction: "I still remember that my uncle, Privy Counsellor Friedberg, a famous professor of canon law in Leipzig, was outraged: 'What a story! And a married man with a family!'"

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/59/Thomas_Mann_1900_cropped.jpg/250px-Thomas_Mann_1900_cropped.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Thomas_Mann_1900_cropped.jpg)

Thomas Mann in 1900 when he completed *Buddenbrooks*

In the autobiographical novella *[Tonio Kröger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tonio_Kröger "Tonio Kröger")* from 1901, the young hero has a crush on a handsome male classmate (modeled after real-life Lübeck classmate Armin Martens). In the novella *With the prophet* (1904) Mann mocks the believing disciples of a neo-Romantic "prophet" who preaches asceticism and has a strong resemblance to the real contemporary poet [Stefan George](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stefan_George "Stefan George") and his *[George-Kreis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George-Kreis "George-Kreis")* ('George-Circle'). In 1902, George had met the 14-year-old boy [Maximilian Kronberger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximilian_Kronberger "Maximilian Kronberger"); he made an idol of him and after his early death in 1904 transfigured him into a kind of [Antinous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antinous "Antinous")-style "god".

Mann had also started planning a novel about [Frederick the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_the_Great "Frederick the Great") in 1905/1906, which ultimately did not come to fruition. The [sexuality of Frederick the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sexuality_of_Frederick_the_Great "Sexuality of Frederick the Great") would have played a significant role in this, its impact on his life, his political decisions and wars. In late 1914, at the start of World War I, Mann used the notes and excerpts already collected for this project to write his essay "Frederick and the grand coalition" in which he contrasted Frederick's soldierly, male drive and his literary, female connotations consisting of "decomposing" skepticism. A similar "decomposing skepticism" had already estranged the barely concealed gay novel characters Tonio Kröger and Hanno [Buddenbrook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddenbrooks "Buddenbrooks") (1901) from their traditional upper class family environments and hometown (which in both cases is Lübeck). The *[Confessions of Felix Krull](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confessions_of_Felix_Krull "Confessions of Felix Krull")*, written from 1910 onwards, describes a self-absorbed young dandyish imposter who, if not explicitly, fits into the gay typology, at one point considering, but ultimately refusing, the advances of a Scottish lord to accept him as his [sugar daddy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sugar_daddy "Sugar daddy") and in return be adopted and named as heir. The 1909 novel *[Royal Highness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Highness_(novel) "Royal Highness (novel)")*, which describes a young unworldly and dreamy prince who forces himself into a marriage of convenience that ultimately becomes happy, was modeled after Mann's own romance and marriage to Katia Mann in February 1905.

In *[The Magic Mountain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Magic_Mountain "The Magic Mountain")*, Hans Castorp, in love with Clawdia Chauchat, recalls his schooltime when, as a fourteen-year-old, he adored a classmate, Pribislav Hippe, whom he asked, with his heart pounding, if he could lend him his pencil, of which he keeps a few scraps like a relic. Borrowing and returning were interpreted as poetic masks for a sexual act. But it is not just a poetic symbol. In his diary entry from 15 September 1950, Mann remembers "Williram Timpe's scraps from his pencil", referring to a classmate from Lübeck. The novella *[Mario and the Magician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mario_and_the_Magician "Mario and the Magician")* (1929) ends with a murder due to a male–male kiss.

Numerous homoerotic crushes are documented in his letters and diaries, both before and after his marriage. Mann's diary records his [incestuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incest "Incest") attraction to his own 13-year-old son, "Eissi" – [Klaus Mann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klaus_Mann "Klaus Mann"): "Klaus to whom recently I feel very drawn" (22 June). In the background conversations about man-to-man eroticism take place; a long letter is written to Carl Maria Weber on this topic, while the diary reveals: "In love with Klaus during these days" (5 June). "Eissi, who enchants me right now" (11 July). "Delight over Eissi, who in his bath is terribly handsome. Find it very natural that I am in love with my son ... Eissi lay reading in bed with his brown torso naked, which disconcerted me" (25 July). "I heard noise in the boys' room and surprised Eissi completely naked in front of Golo's bed acting foolish. Strong impression of his premasculine, gleaming body. Disquiet" (17 October 1920). His younger son [Golo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golo_Mann "Golo Mann"), homosexual like Klaus, suffered lifelong from the lower esteem his father showed him, the less handsome and somewhat clumsy – but ultimately he inherited more of his father's literary talent than the older one.

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b0/Ludwig_von_Hofmann%2C_Die_Quelle_%281913%29.jpg/250px-Ludwig_von_Hofmann%2C_Die_Quelle_%281913%29.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Ludwig_von_Hofmann,_Die_Quelle_(1913).jpg)

[Ludwig von Hofmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludwig_von_Hofmann "Ludwig von Hofmann"): *The Spring* (1913). The picture, purchased in 1914, hung in Mann's study until his death.

Mann was a friend of the painter and violinist [Paul Ehrenberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Ehrenberg "Paul Ehrenberg"), for whom he had feelings as a young man (from 1899 to 1904, at least until around 1903 when there is evidence that those feelings had cooled). The attraction that he felt for Ehrenberg, which is corroborated by notebook entries, caused Mann difficulty and discomfort and may have been an obstacle to his marrying an English woman, Mary Smith, whom he met in 1901. In 1927, while on summer vacation in [Kampen (Sylt)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kampen_(Sylt) "Kampen (Sylt)"), the 52-year-old Mann fell in love with 17-year-old Klaus Heuser, to whom he dedicated the introduction to his essay "[Kleist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heinrich_von_Kleist "Heinrich von Kleist")'s Amphitryon, a Reconquest" in the fall of the same year, which he read publicly in Munich in the presence of Heuser. [Jupiter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jupiter_(god) "Jupiter (god)"), who has transformed himself into the form of the general [Amphitryon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amphitryon "Amphitryon"), tries to seduce his wife Alcmene when the real Amphitryon returns home and Alcmene rejects the god. Mann understands Jupiter as the "lonely artistic spirit" who courts life, is rejected and, "a triumphant renouncer", learns to be content with his divinity. In 1950, Mann met the 19-year-old waiter Franz Westermeier, confiding to his diary "Once again this, once again love". He immediately processed the experience in his essay "[Michelangelo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michelangelo "Michelangelo") in his poems" (1950) and was also inspired to write "[The Black Swan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Black_Swan_(short_story) "The Black Swan (short story)")" (1954). In 1975, when Mann's diaries were published, creating a national sensation in Germany, the retired Westermeier was tracked down in the United States: he was flattered to learn he had been the object of Mann's obsession, but also shocked at its depth.

Mann's infatuations probably remained largely [platonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platonic_love "Platonic love"). Katia Mann tolerated these love affairs, as did the children, because they knew that it did not go too far. He exchanged letters with Klaus Heuser for a while and met him again in 1935. He wrote about the Heuser experience in his diary on 6 May 1934: "In comparison, the early experiences with Armin Martens and Williram Timpe recede far into the childlike, and that with Klaus Heuser was a late happiness with the character of life-relevant fulfillment ... That's probably how it is humanly, and because of this normality I can feel my life is more canonical than through marriage and children." In the entry from 20 February 1942, he spoke again about Klaus Heuser: "Well, yes − lived and loved. Black eyes that shed tears for me, beloved lips that I kissed − it was there, I had it too, I'll be able to tell myself when I die." He was partly delighted, partly ashamed of the depth of his own emotions in these cases and mostly made them productive at some earlier or later date, but the experiences themselves were not yet literary. Only in retrospective, he converted them into literary production and sublimated his shame into the theory that "a writer experiences in order to express himself", that his life is just material. Mann even went so far as to accuse his brother [Heinrich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heinrich_Mann "Heinrich Mann") of his "aestheticism being a gesture-rich, highly gifted impotence for life and love." When Mann met the aging bachelor Heuser, who had worked in China for 18 years, for the last time in 1954, his daughter Erika scoffed: "Since he (Heuser) couldn't have the magician (= Thomas Mann's nickname with his children), he preferred to give it up completely."

Although Mann had always denied his novels had autobiographical components, the unsealing of his diaries revealing how consumed his life had been with unrequited and sublimated passion resulted in a reappraisal of his work. Thomas Mann had burned all of his diaries from before March 1933 in the garden of his home in Pacific Palisades in May 1945. Only the booklets from September 1918 to December 1921 were preserved because the author needed them for his work on *[Doctor Faustus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_Faustus_(novel) "Doctor Faustus (novel)")*. He later decided to have them − and his diaries from 1933 onwards – published 20 years after his death and predicted "surprise and cheerful astonishment". They were published by [Peter von Mendelssohn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_von_Mendelssohn "Peter von Mendelssohn") and [Inge Jens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Jens "Walter Jens") in 10 volumes.

From the very beginning, Thomas' son [Klaus Mann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klaus_Mann "Klaus Mann") openly dealt with his own homosexuality in his literary work and open lifestyle and referred critically to his father's "[sublimation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sublimation_(psychology) "Sublimation (psychology)")" in his diary. On the other hand, Thomas's daughter [Erika Mann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erika_Mann "Erika Mann") and his son [Golo Mann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golo_Mann "Golo Mann") came out only later in their lives. Thomas Mann reacted cautiously to Klaus's first novel *The Pious Dance, Adventure Book of a Youth* (1926), which is openly set in [Berlin's homosexual milieu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LGBT_culture_in_Berlin "LGBT culture in Berlin"). Although he embraced male–male eroticism, he disapproved of gay lifestyle. The [Eulenburg affair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eulenburg_affair "Eulenburg affair"), which broke out two years after Mann's marriage, had strengthened him in his renunciation of a gay life and he supported the journalist [Maximilian Harden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximilian_Harden "Maximilian Harden"), who was friends with Katia Mann's family, in his denunciatory trial against the gay [Prince of Eulenburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philipp,_Prince_of_Eulenburg "Philipp, Prince of Eulenburg"), a close friend of Emperor [Wilhelm II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilhelm_II "Wilhelm II"). Thomas Mann was always concerned about his dignity, reputation and respectability; the "poet king" [Goethe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goethe "Goethe") was his role model. His horror at a possible collapse of these attributes found expression in the character of Aschenbach in *Death in Venice*. Cases like that of the industrialist [Friedrich Alfred Krupp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Alfred_Krupp "Friedrich Alfred Krupp"), who felt driven to suicide after a homosexual affair became public, were not uncommon and had a deterrent effect. But as time went on Mann became more open. He tolerated his son Klaus bringing his various lovers to lunch in Pacific Palisades, and was only appalled when these – navy sailors and the like – had never heard of him or his works, which, unfortunately, was mostly the case, leading to short, surly diary entries about their lack of culture. When the twenty-two-year-old novelist [Gore Vidal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gore_Vidal "Gore Vidal") published his third novel *[The City and the Pillar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_City_and_the_Pillar "The City and the Pillar")* in 1948, a love-story between small-town American boys and a portrait of homosexual life in New York and Hollywood in the forties, a highly controversial book even among the publishers, not to mention the press, he sent a copy to Thomas Mann who responded politely, calling it a "noble work".

When the physician and pioneer of gay liberation [Magnus Hirschfeld](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnus_Hirschfeld "Magnus Hirschfeld") sent another petition to the [Reichstag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reichstag_(Weimar_Republic) "Reichstag (Weimar Republic)") in 1922 to abolish [Section 175](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paragraph_175 "Paragraph 175") of the German Criminal Code, under which many homosexuals were imprisoned simply because of their inclinations, Thomas Mann also signed. However, criminal liability among adults was only abolished through a change in the law on 25 June 1969 − fourteen years after Mann's death and just three days before the [Stonewall riots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stonewall_riots "Stonewall riots"). This legal situation certainly had an impact throughout his life; the man whom the Nazis labeled a traitor never had any desire to be incarcerated for "criminal acts".

## Cultural references

### *The Magic Mountain*

Several literary and other works make reference to Mann's book *[The Magic Mountain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Magic_Mountain "The Magic Mountain")*, including:

- [Frederic Tuten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederic_Tuten "Frederic Tuten")'s 1993 novel *Tintin in the New World* features many characters (such as Clavdia Chauchat, Mynheer Peeperkorn and others) from *The Magic Mountain* interacting with [Tintin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tintin_(character) "Tintin (character)") in Peru.
- [Andrew Crumey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Crumey "Andrew Crumey")'s novel *[Mobius Dick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mobius_Dick "Mobius Dick")* (2004) imagines an alternative universe where an author named Behring has written novels resembling Mann's. These include a version of *The Magic Mountain* with [Erwin Schrödinger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erwin_Schrödinger "Erwin Schrödinger") in place of Castorp.
- [Haruki Murakami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haruki_Murakami "Haruki Murakami")'s novel *[Norwegian Wood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norwegian_Wood_(novel) "Norwegian Wood (novel)")* (1987), in which the main character is criticized for reading *The Magic Mountain* while visiting a friend in a sanatorium.
- The song "Magic Mountain" by the band [Blonde Redhead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blonde_Redhead "Blonde Redhead").
- The painting *Magic Mountain (after Thomas Mann)* by [Christiaan Tonnis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christiaan_Tonnis "Christiaan Tonnis") (1987). "The Magic Mountain" is also a chapter in Tonnis's 2006 book *Krankheit als Symbol* ("Illness as a Symbol").
- The 1941 film *[49th Parallel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/49th_Parallel_(film) "49th Parallel (film)")*, in which the character Philip Armstrong Scott unknowingly praises Mann's work to an escaped World War II Nazi [U-boat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U-boat "U-boat") commander, who later responds by burning Scott's copy of *The Magic Mountain*.
- In [Ken Kesey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ken_Kesey "Ken Kesey")'s novel *[Sometimes a Great Notion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sometimes_a_Great_Notion "Sometimes a Great Notion")* (1964), character Indian Jenny purchases a Thomas Mann novel and tries to find out "just where was this mountain full of magic..." (p. 578).
- [Hayao Miyazaki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hayao_Miyazaki "Hayao Miyazaki")'s 2013 film *[The Wind Rises](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Wind_Rises "The Wind Rises")*, in which an unnamed German man at a mountain resort invokes the novel as cover for furtively condemning the rapidly arming Hitler and Hirohito regimes. After he flees to escape the Japanese secret police, the protagonist, who fears his own mail is being read, refers to him as the novel's Mr. Castorp. The film is partly based on another Japanese novel, set like *The Magic Mountain* in a tuberculosis sanatorium.
- [Father John Misty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Father_John_Misty "Father John Misty")'s 2017 album *[Pure Comedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pure_Comedy "Pure Comedy")* contains a song titled "So I'm Growing Old on Magic Mountain", in which a man, near death, reflects on the passing of time and the disappearance of his Dionysian youth in homage to the themes in Mann's novel.
- [Viktor Frankl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viktor_Frankl "Viktor Frankl")'s book *[Man's Search for Meaning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Man's_Search_for_Meaning "Man's Search for Meaning")* relates the "time-experience" of Holocaust prisoners to TB patients in *The Magic Mountain*: "How paradoxical was our time-experience! In this connection we are reminded of Thomas Mann's *The Magic Mountain*, which contains some very pointed psychological remarks. Mann studies the spiritual development of people who are in an analogous psychological position, i.e., tuberculosis patients in a sanatorium who also know no date for their release. They experience a similar existence—without a future and without a goal."
- The movie [A Cure For Wellness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Cure_For_Wellness "A Cure For Wellness"), directed by [Gore Verbinski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gore_Verbinski "Gore Verbinski"), was inspired by and is somewhat a modernization, somewhat a parody, of *The Magic Mountain*. In one scene, an orderly at the asylum can be seen reading *Der Zauberberg*.
- The album cover for [Peter Schickele](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Schickele "Peter Schickele")'s recording of [P.D.Q. Bach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P.D.Q._Bach "P.D.Q. Bach")'s "[Bluegrass Cantata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluegrass_Cantata?action=edit&redlink=1 "Bluegrass Cantata")" shows an illustration of the 18th Century German bluegrass ensemble Tommy Mann and his Magic Mountain Boys.
- The 2022 novel [The Empusium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Empusium "The Empusium") by [Olga Tokarczuk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olga_Tokarczuk "Olga Tokarczuk") reprises key plot elements from The Magic Mountain, including an alpine sanatorium for the treatment of tuberculosis, a time setting of 1913 which precedes World War I, a protagonist who is a young engineer and an isolated health resort as a [microcosm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microcosm–macrocosm_analogy "Microcosm–macrocosm analogy") of society.
- The 2025 song "Magic Mountain" by the band [Japanese Breakfast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_Breakfast "Japanese Breakfast").

- Mann's 1896 short story "Disillusionment" is the basis for the [Leiber and Stoller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leiber_and_Stoller "Leiber and Stoller") song "[Is That All There Is?](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Is_That_All_There_Is%3F "Is That All There Is?")", famously recorded in 1969 by [Peggy Lee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peggy_Lee "Peggy Lee").
- In a 1994 essay, [Umberto Eco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umberto_Eco "Umberto Eco") suggests that the media discuss "Whether reading Thomas Mann gives one erections" as an alternative to "Whether [Joyce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Joyce "James Joyce") is boring".
- Mann's life in California during World War II, including his relationships with his older brother [Heinrich Mann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heinrich_Mann "Heinrich Mann") and [Bertolt Brecht](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bertolt_Brecht "Bertolt Brecht") is a subject of [Christopher Hampton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Hampton "Christopher Hampton")'s play *Tales from Hollywood*.
- In 2001, the three-part miniseries *[Die Manns – Ein Jahrhundertroman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Die_Manns_–_Ein_Jahrhundertroman "Die Manns – Ein Jahrhundertroman")* aired on German television, telling the story of the Mann family between 1922 and 1955. In the award-winning series, Thomas Mann is portrayed by [Armin Mueller-Stahl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armin_Mueller-Stahl "Armin Mueller-Stahl").
- Hans Pleschinski's 2013 novel *Königsallee* fictionalizes an actual visit by Thomas Mann to Klaus Heuser's parents in Düsseldorf in 1954. He lets Mann's former lover come along with his boy named Anwar, as well as Mann's children Erika and Golo. Everything gets complicated.
- [Colm Tóibín](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colm_Tóibín "Colm Tóibín")'s 2021 fictionalised biography *[The Magician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Magician_(Tóibín_novel) "The Magician (Tóibín novel)")* is a portrait of Mann in the context of his family and political events.
- *[Fatherland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fatherland_(2026_film) "Fatherland (2026 film)")*, a 2026 film written and directed by [Paweł Pawlikowski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paweł_Pawlikowski "Paweł Pawlikowski"), is based on an episode from Tóibín's *The Magician* where Mann and his daughter [Erika](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erika_Mann "Erika Mann") embark on a road trip across Cold War-era Germany, culminating in Mann's visits to [Frankfurt am Main](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frankfurt_am_Main "Frankfurt am Main") and [Weimar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weimar "Weimar") to commemorate the 200th birthday of [Johann Wolfgang von Goethe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Wolfgang_von_Goethe "Johann Wolfgang von Goethe"). Mann is portrayed by [Hanns Zischler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanns_Zischler "Hanns Zischler"), and [Sandra Hüller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sandra_Hüller "Sandra Hüller") portrays Erika Mann. The film premiered to critical acclaim at the [2026 Cannes Film Festival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Cannes_Film_Festival "2026 Cannes Film Festival"), where Pawlikowski won the prize for [Best Director](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannes_Film_Festival_Award_for_Best_Director "Cannes Film Festival Award for Best Director").

The book was published in 2025 to mark the 150th anniversary of Mann's birth.

## Research

### Databases

The metadatabase TMI-Research brings together archival materials and library holdings of the network "Thomas Mann International". The network was founded in 2017 by the five houses Buddenbrookhaus/Heinrich-und-Thomas-Mann-Zentrum (Lübeck), the [Monacensia im Hildebrandhaus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monacensia "Monacensia") (Munich), the Thomas Mann Archive of the ETH Zurich (Zurich/Switzerland), the [Thomas Mann House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Mann_House "Thomas Mann House") (Los Angeles/USA) and the Thomo Manno kultūros centras/Thomas Mann Culture Centre (Nida/Lithuania). The houses stand for the main stations of Thomas Mann's life. The platform, which is hosted by [ETH Zurich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ETH_Zurich "ETH Zurich"), allows research in the collections of the network partners across all houses. The database is freely accessible and contains over 165,000 records on letters, original editions, photographs, monographs and essays on Thomas Mann and the Mann family. Further links take you to the respective source databases with contact options and further information.
