**William Cuthbert Faulkner** ([/ˈfɔːknər/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English") [*FAWK-nər*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:Pronunciation_respelling_key "Help:Pronunciation respelling key"); [né](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Né "Né") **Falkner**; September 25, 1897 – July 6, 1962) was an American writer. He is best known for [his novels and short stories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Faulkner_bibliography "William Faulkner bibliography") set in the fictional [Yoknapatawpha County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoknapatawpha_County "Yoknapatawpha County"), [Mississippi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mississippi "Mississippi"), a stand-in for [Lafayette County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lafayette_County,_Mississippi "Lafayette County, Mississippi") where he spent most of his life. Winner of the [1949 Nobel Prize in Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1949_Nobel_Prize_in_Literature "1949 Nobel Prize in Literature"), Faulkner is one of the most celebrated writers of [American literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_literature "American literature"), often considered the greatest writer of [Southern literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_United_States_literature "Southern United States literature") and regarded as one of the most influential and important writers of the 20th century.

Faulkner was born in [New Albany, Mississippi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Albany,_Mississippi "New Albany, Mississippi"), and raised in [Oxford, Mississippi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford,_Mississippi "Oxford, Mississippi"). During [World War I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I "World War I"), he joined the [Royal Canadian Air Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Canadian_Air_Force "Royal Canadian Air Force"), but did not serve in combat. Returning to Oxford, he attended the [University of Mississippi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Mississippi "University of Mississippi") for three semesters before dropping out. He moved to [New Orleans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Orleans "New Orleans"), where he wrote his first novel *[Soldiers' Pay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soldiers'_Pay "Soldiers' Pay")* (1925). He went back to Oxford and wrote *[Sartoris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sartoris "Sartoris")* (1927), his first work set in Yoknapatawpha County. In 1929, he published *[The Sound and the Fury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Sound_and_the_Fury "The Sound and the Fury")*. The following year, he wrote *[As I Lay Dying](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/As_I_Lay_Dying "As I Lay Dying")*. Later that decade, he wrote *[Light in August](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light_in_August "Light in August")*; *[Absalom, Absalom!](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absalom,_Absalom! "Absalom, Absalom!");* and *[The Wild Palms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Wild_Palms "The Wild Palms")*. He also worked as a screenwriter, contributing to [Howard Hawks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Howard_Hawks "Howard Hawks")'s *[To Have and Have Not](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/To_Have_and_Have_Not_(film) "To Have and Have Not (film)")* and [*The Big Sleep*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Big_Sleep_(1946_film) "The Big Sleep (1946 film)"), adapted from [Raymond Chandler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raymond_Chandler "Raymond Chandler")'s [novel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Big_Sleep "The Big Sleep").

Faulkner's reputation grew following publication of [Malcolm Cowley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malcolm_Cowley "Malcolm Cowley")'s *The Portable Faulkner* (1946), and he was awarded the [1949 Nobel Prize in Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1949_Nobel_Prize_in_Literature "1949 Nobel Prize in Literature") for "his powerful and unique contribution to the modern American novel." He is the only Mississippi-born Nobel laureate. Two of his works, *[A Fable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Fable "A Fable")* (1954) and *[The Reivers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Reivers "The Reivers")* (1962), won the [Pulitzer Prize for Fiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulitzer_Prize_for_Fiction "Pulitzer Prize for Fiction"). Faulkner died from a [heart attack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heart_attack "Heart attack") on July 6, 1962, following a fall from his horse the month before. [Ralph Ellison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ralph_Ellison "Ralph Ellison") called him "the greatest artist the South has produced".

## Life

### Childhood and heritage

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e8/William_Clark_Falkner.jpg/250px-William_Clark_Falkner.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:William_Clark_Falkner.jpg)

Faulkner was influenced by stories of [William Clark Falkner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Clark_Falkner "William Clark Falkner"), his paternal great-grandfather and namesake.

Faulkner was born on September 25, 1897, in [New Albany, Mississippi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Albany,_Mississippi "New Albany, Mississippi"), the first of four sons of Murry Cuthbert Falkner and Maud Butler. His family was upper middle-class, but "not quite of [the old feudal cotton aristocracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planter_class "Planter class")". After Maud rejected Murry's plan to become a rancher in Texas, the family moved to [Oxford, Mississippi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford,_Mississippi "Oxford, Mississippi"), in 1902, where Faulkner's father established a livery stable and hardware store before becoming the [University of Mississippi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Mississippi "University of Mississippi")'s business manager. Except for short periods elsewhere, Faulkner lived in Oxford for the rest of his life.

Faulkner spent his boyhood listening to stories told to him by his elders – stories that spanned the [Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Civil_War "American Civil War"), slavery, the [Ku Klux Klan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ku_Klux_Klan "Ku Klux Klan"), and the Faulkner family. Young William was greatly influenced by the history of his family and the region in which he lived. Mississippi marked his sense of humor, his sense of the tragic position of "[black](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_Americans "African Americans") and [white](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_Americans "White Americans")" [Americans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Americans "Americans"), his characterization of Southern characters, and his timeless themes, including fiercely intelligent people who are dwelling behind the façades of good ol' boys and simpletons. He was particularly influenced by stories of his great-grandfather [William Clark Falkner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Clark_Falkner "William Clark Falkner"), who had become a near legendary figure in North Mississippi. Born into poverty, the elder Falkner was a strict disciplinarian and was a Confederate colonel. Tried and acquitted twice on charges of murder, he became a member of the [Mississippi House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mississippi_House_of_Representatives "Mississippi House of Representatives") and became a part-owner of a railroad before being murdered by a co-owner. Faulkner incorporated many aspects of his great-grandfather's biography into his later works.

Faulkner initially excelled in school and skipped the second grade. However, beginning somewhere in the fourth and fifth grades, he became a quieter and more withdrawn child. He occasionally played truant and became indifferent about schoolwork. Instead, he took an interest in studying the [history of Mississippi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Mississippi "History of Mississippi"). The decline of his performance in school continued, and Faulkner wound up repeating the eleventh and twelfth grades, never graduating from high school. As a teenager in Oxford, Faulkner dated Estelle Oldham (1897–1972), the popular daughter of Major Lemuel and Lida Oldham, and he also believed he would marry her. However, Estelle dated other boys during their romance, and, in 1918, [Cornell Franklin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornell_Franklin "Cornell Franklin") (five years Faulkner's senior) proposed marriage to her before Faulkner did. She accepted.

### Trip to the North and early writings

When he was 17, Faulkner met [Phil Stone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phil_Stone "Phil Stone"), who became an important early influence on his writing. Stone was four years his senior and came from one of Oxford's older families; he was passionate about literature and had bachelor's degrees from [Yale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yale_University "Yale University") and the University of Mississippi. Stone read and was impressed by some of Faulkner's early poetry, becoming one of the first to recognize and encourage Faulkner's talent. Stone mentored the young Faulkner, introducing him to the works of writers like [James Joyce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Joyce "James Joyce"), who influenced Faulkner's own writing. In his early 20s, Faulkner gave poems and short stories he had written to Stone in hopes of them being published. Stone sent these to publishers, but they were uniformly rejected. In spring 1918, Faulkner traveled to live with Stone at Yale, his first trip to the North. Through Stone, Faulkner met writers like [Sherwood Anderson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sherwood_Anderson "Sherwood Anderson"), [Robert Frost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Frost "Robert Frost"), and [Ezra Pound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ezra_Pound "Ezra Pound").

During the [First World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_World_War "First World War"), Faulkner attempted to join the US Army. There are accounts of this that indicate he was rejected for being under weight and his short stature of 5'5". Other accounts purport to prove that the aforementioned accounts are false. Although he initially planned to join the [British Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Army "British Army") in hopes of being commissioned as an officer, Faulkner instead joined the [Royal Air Force (Canada)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Flying_Corps_(Canada) "Royal Flying Corps (Canada)") with a forged letter of reference and left Yale to receive training in [Toronto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toronto "Toronto"). He enlisted in Toronto on July 10, 1918, as a Private (II Class), No.173799, in the [RAF (C)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Flying_Corps_(Canada) "Royal Flying Corps (Canada)") but never saw active service overseas during the First World War, only training at the recruit depot in Toronto.

[![Faulkner is pictured in a military uniform and cap, leaning on a cane. A caption reads "Royal Flying Corps".](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/12/William_Faulkner_in_Toronto%2C_Canada_%281918%29.jpg/250px-William_Faulkner_in_Toronto%2C_Canada_%281918%29.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:William_Faulkner_in_Toronto,_Canada_(1918).jpg)

Faulkner as a cadet in the Canadian RAF, 1918

On January 4, 1919, he was discharged as a Private (II Class) due to the end of the War, having served 179 days. Despite claiming so in his letters, Faulkner did not receive cockpit training or ever fly. Returning to Oxford in December 1918, Faulkner told acquaintances false war-stories and even faked a war wound.

In 1918, Faulkner's surname changed from "Falkner" to "Faulkner". According to one story, a careless typesetter made an error. When the misprint appeared on the title page of his first book, Faulkner was asked whether he wanted the change. He supposedly replied, "Either way suits me." His 1918 Attestation Papers for the RAF (C) note his name as "Faulkner". In adolescence, Faulkner began writing poetry almost exclusively. He did not write his first novel until 1925. His literary influences are deep and wide. He once stated that he modeled his early writing on the [Romantic era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanticism "Romanticism") in late 18th- and early 19th-century England.

He attended the University of Mississippi, enrolling in 1919, studying for three semesters before dropping out in November 1920. Faulkner joined the [Sigma Alpha Epsilon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigma_Alpha_Epsilon "Sigma Alpha Epsilon") fraternity, and pursued his dream to become a writer. He skipped classes often and received a "D" grade in English. However, some of his poems were published in campus publications. In 1922, his poem "Portrait" was published in the New Orleans literary magazine *Double Dealer*. The magazine published his "New Orleans" short story collection three years later. After dropping out, he took a series of odd jobs: at a New York City bookstore, as a carpenter in Oxford, and as the Ole Miss postmaster. He resigned from the post office with the declaration: "I will be damned if I propose to be at the beck and call of every itinerant scoundrel who has two cents to invest in a postage stamp."

### New Orleans and early novels

[![Photographic portrait of Faulkner at bust length, in profile facing right, smoking a pipe, with short hair and a mustache.](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/14/William_Faulkner_%281924_publicity_photo_-_pipe%29.jpg/250px-William_Faulkner_%281924_publicity_photo_-_pipe%29.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:William_Faulkner_(1924_publicity_photo_-_pipe).jpg)

[![Photographic portrait of Faulkner seated in a chair.](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d4/William_Faulkner_%281924_publicity_photo_-_chair%29.jpg/250px-William_Faulkner_%281924_publicity_photo_-_chair%29.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:William_Faulkner_(1924_publicity_photo_-_chair).jpg)

Publicity photographs of Faulkner, summer 1924

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/8a/FQFauknerPirates.jpg/250px-FQFauknerPirates.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:FQFauknerPirates.jpg)

During part of his time in New Orleans, Faulkner lived in a house in the [French Quarter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Quarter "French Quarter") (pictured center yellow).

While most writers of Faulkner's [generation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lost_Generation "Lost Generation") traveled to and lived in Europe, Faulkner remained writing in the United States. Faulkner spent the first half of 1925 in [New Orleans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Orleans "New Orleans"), Louisiana, where many [bohemian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bohemianism "Bohemianism") artists and writers lived, specifically in the [French Quarter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Quarter "French Quarter") where Faulkner lived beginning in March. During his time in New Orleans, Faulkner's focus drifted from poetry to prose and his literary style made a marked transition from [Victorian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victorian_literature "Victorian literature") to [modernist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literary_modernism "Literary modernism"). *[The Times-Picayune](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Times-Picayune/The_New_Orleans_Advocate "The Times-Picayune/The New Orleans Advocate")* published several of his short works of prose.

Beginning in 1925, Faulkner shared a St. Peter Street apartment with the architect and artist [William Spratling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Spratling "William Spratling"). The following year they privately published *Sherwood Anderson and Other Famous Creoles*, a parody of [Miguel Covarrubias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miguel_Covarrubias "Miguel Covarrubias")'s *The Prince of Wales and Other Famous Americans*, combining Spratling's caricatures of the French Quarter literary circle with captions and an introduction by Faulkner.

During the same period, Faulkner wrote his first novel, *[Soldiers' Pay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soldiers'_Pay "Soldiers' Pay")*, in New Orleans. Anderson helped secure its publication, as well as that of *[Mosquitoes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mosquitoes_(novel) "Mosquitoes (novel)")*, by recommending both works to his publisher. These and Faulkner's other early works resembled those of contemporaries such as [Ernest Hemingway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_Hemingway "Ernest Hemingway") and [F. Scott Fitzgerald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F._Scott_Fitzgerald "F. Scott Fitzgerald"), at times nearly appropriating phrases.

The miniature house at 624 Pirate's Alley, just around the corner from [St. Louis Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Louis_Cathedral_(New_Orleans) "St. Louis Cathedral (New Orleans)") in New Orleans, is now the site of Faulkner House Books, where it also serves as the headquarters of the Pirate's Alley Faulkner Society.

During the summer of 1927, Faulkner wrote his first novel set in his fictional Yoknapatawpha County, *[Flags in the Dust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flags_in_the_Dust "Flags in the Dust")*. This novel drew heavily from the traditions and history of the South, in which Faulkner had been engrossed in his youth. He was extremely proud of the novel upon its completion and he believed it a significant step up from his previous two novels—however, when submitted for publication to [Boni & Liveright](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boni_&_Liveright "Boni & Liveright"), it was rejected. Faulkner was devastated by this rejection but he eventually allowed his literary agent, Ben Wasson, to edit the text, and the novel was published in 1929 as *[Sartoris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sartoris "Sartoris").* The work was notable in that it was his first novel that dealt with the Civil War rather than the contemporary emphasis on World War I and its legacy. Eventually Faulkner's daughter, Jill, would approach University of Virginia professor [Douglas Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Douglas_Day "Douglas Day") about restoring the text. Almost a fourth of the original manuscript had been cut by Wasson to meet the demands of publishers Harcourt, Brace in 1929. Working from a surviving typescript, Day reinstated cut passages but also included at least one added section from the published text. This new edition, published in 1973, also restored Faulkner's original title, *Flags in the Dust.* A third version by Noel Polk has since replaced Day's and is considered the definitive text by Random House, the current publishers of Faulkner's fiction.

### *The Sound and the Fury*

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7c/The_Sound_and_the_Fury_%281929_1st_ed_dust_jacket%29.jpg/250px-The_Sound_and_the_Fury_%281929_1st_ed_dust_jacket%29.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:The_Sound_and_the_Fury_(1929_1st_ed_dust_jacket).jpg)

*[The Sound and the Fury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Sound_and_the_Fury "The Sound and the Fury")* (1929)

In autumn 1928, just after his 31st birthday, Faulkner began working on *[The Sound and the Fury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Sound_and_the_Fury "The Sound and the Fury")*. He started by writing three short stories about a group of children with the last name Compson, but soon began to feel that the characters he had created might be better suited for a full-length novel. Perhaps as a result of disappointment in the initial rejection of *Flags in the Dust*, Faulkner had now become indifferent to his publishers and wrote this novel in a much more experimental style. In describing the writing process for this work, Faulkner later said, "One day I seemed to shut the door between me and all publisher's addresses and book lists. I said to myself, 'Now I can write.'" After its completion, Faulkner insisted that Wasson not do any editing or add any punctuation for clarity.

### 1929–1931

In 1929, Faulkner married Estelle Oldham, with Andrew Kuhn serving as best man at the wedding. Estelle brought with her two children from her previous marriage to [Cornell Franklin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornell_Franklin "Cornell Franklin") and Faulkner hoped to support his new family as a writer. Faulkner and Estelle had one child together, daughter Jill (1933–2008). He began writing *[As I Lay Dying](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/As_I_Lay_Dying "As I Lay Dying")* in 1929 while working night shifts at the [University of Mississippi Power House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Mississippi_Power_House "University of Mississippi Power House"). The novel was published in 1930.

Beginning in 1930, Faulkner sent some of his short stories to various national magazines. Several of these were published and brought him enough income to buy a house in Oxford for his family, which he named [Rowan Oak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rowan_Oak "Rowan Oak"). Fueled by a desire to make money, Faulkner wrote *[Sanctuary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanctuary_(Faulkner_novel) "Sanctuary (Faulkner novel)")*. With limited royalties from his work, he published short stories in magazines such as *[The Saturday Evening Post](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Saturday_Evening_Post "The Saturday Evening Post")* to supplement his income.

In 1931, Faulkner's first child, a girl they named Alabama, was born but died nine days later.

### *Light in August* and Hollywood years

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/32/Light_in_August_%281932_dust_jacket_cover%29.jpg/250px-Light_in_August_%281932_dust_jacket_cover%29.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Light_in_August_(1932_dust_jacket_cover).jpg)

*[Light in August](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light_in_August "Light in August")* (1932)

By 1932, Faulkner was in need of money. He asked Wasson to sell the serialization rights for his newly completed novel, *Light in August*, to a magazine for $5,000, but none accepted the offer. Then [MGM Studios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer "Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer") offered Faulkner work as a screenwriter in Hollywood. Faulkner was not an avid moviegoer and had reservations about working in the movie industry. As André Bleikasten comments, he "was in dire need of money and had no idea how to get it...So he went to Hollywood." It has been noted that authors like Faulkner were not always hired for their writing prowess but "to enhance the prestige of the ...writers who hired them." He arrived in [Culver City, California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culver_City,_California "Culver City, California"), in May 1932. The job began a sporadic relationship with moviemaking and with California, which was difficult but he endured in order to earn "a consistent salary that supported his family back home."

Initially, he declared a desire to work on [Mickey Mouse cartoons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mickey_Mouse_(film_series) "Mickey Mouse (film series)"), not realizing that they were produced by [Walt Disney Productions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walt_Disney_Animation_Studios "Walt Disney Animation Studios") and not MGM. His first screenplay was for *[Today We Live](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Today_We_Live "Today We Live")*, an adaptation of his short story "Turnabout", which received a mixed response. He then wrote a screen adaptation of *Sartoris* that was never produced. From 1932 to 1954, Faulkner worked on around 50 films. In early 1944, Faulkner wrote a screenplay adaptation of [Ernest Hemingway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_Hemingway "Ernest Hemingway")'s novel *[To Have and Have Not](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/To_Have_and_Have_Not "To Have and Have Not")*. The [film](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/To_Have_and_Have_Not_(film) "To Have and Have Not (film)") was the first starring [Lauren Bacall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lauren_Bacall "Lauren Bacall") and [Humphrey Bogart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humphrey_Bogart "Humphrey Bogart"). Bogart and Bacall would star in Hawks's *[The Big Sleep](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Big_Sleep_(1946_film) "The Big Sleep (1946 film)")*, another film Faulkner worked on.

Faulkner was highly critical of what he found in Hollywood, and he wrote letters that were "scathing in tone, painting a miserable portrait of a literary artist [imprisoned in a cultural Babylon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylonian_captivity "Babylonian captivity")." Many scholars have brought attention to the dilemma he experienced and the predicament that caused him serious unhappiness. In Hollywood he worked with director [Howard Hawks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Howard_Hawks "Howard Hawks"), with whom he quickly developed a friendship, as they both enjoyed drinking and hunting. Howard Hawks' brother, [William Hawks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Hawks "William Hawks"), became Faulkner's [Hollywood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cinema_of_the_United_States "Cinema of the United States") agent. Faulkner continued to find reliable work as a screenwriter from the 1930s to the 1950s. While staying in Hollywood, Faulkner adopted a "vagrant" lifestyle, living in brief stints in hotels like the [Garden of Allah Hotel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garden_of_Allah_Hotel "Garden of Allah Hotel") and frequenting the bar at the [Roosevelt Hotel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Hollywood_Roosevelt_Hotel "The Hollywood Roosevelt Hotel") and the [Musso & Frank Grill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musso_&_Frank_Grill "Musso & Frank Grill") where he was said to have regularly gone behind the bar to mix his own Mint Juleps. He had an extramarital affair with Hawks' secretary and [script girl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Script_supervisor "Script supervisor"), Meta Carpenter.

With the onset of World War II, in 1942, Faulkner tried to join the [United States Air Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Air_Force "United States Air Force") but was rejected. He instead worked on local [civil defense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_civil_defense "United States civil defense"). The war drained Faulkner of his enthusiasm. He described the war as "bad for writing". Amid this [creative slowdown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Writer's_block "Writer's block"), in 1943, Faulkner began work on a new novel that merged World War I's [Unknown Soldier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomb_of_the_Unknown_Soldier#History "Tomb of the Unknown Soldier") with the [Passion of Christ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passion_of_Jesus "Passion of Jesus"). Published over a decade later as *[A Fable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Fable "A Fable")*, it won the 1954 Pulitzer Prize. The award for *A Fable* was a controversial political choice. The jury had selected [Milton Lott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milton_Lott "Milton Lott")'s *[The Last Hunt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Last_Hunt_(novel) "The Last Hunt (novel)")* for the prize, but Pulitzer Prize Administrator Professor John Hohenberg convinced the Pulitzer board that Faulkner was long overdue for the award, despite *A Fable* being a lesser work of his, and the board overrode the jury's selection, much to the disgust of its members.

By the time of *The Portable Faulkner*'s publication in 1946, most of his novels had been out of print.

### Nobel Prize and later years

[![Faulkner is pictured in a chair before a brick well. He looks to the left.](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/ac/William_Faulkner_1954_%282%29_%28photo_by_Carl_van_Vechten%29.jpg/250px-William_Faulkner_1954_%282%29_%28photo_by_Carl_van_Vechten%29.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:William_Faulkner_1954_(2)_(photo_by_Carl_van_Vechten).jpg)

Faulkner in 1954

Faulkner was awarded the [1949 Nobel Prize in Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1949_Nobel_Prize_in_Literature "1949 Nobel Prize in Literature") for "his powerful and artistically unique contribution to the modern American novel". It was awarded at the following year's banquet along with the 1950 Prize to [Bertrand Russell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bertrand_Russell "Bertrand Russell").

When Faulkner visited Stockholm in December 1950 to receive the Nobel Prize, he met Else Jonsson (1912–1996), who was the widow of journalist [Thorsten Jonsson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thorsten_Jonsson "Thorsten Jonsson") (1910–1950). Jonsson, a reporter for *[Dagens Nyheter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dagens_Nyheter "Dagens Nyheter")* from 1943 to 1946, had interviewed Faulkner in 1946 and introduced his works to Swedish readers. Faulkner and Else had an affair that lasted until the end of 1953. At the banquet where they met in 1950, publisher Tor Bonnier introduced Else as the widow of the man responsible for Faulkner winning the Nobel Prize.

Faulkner's Nobel Prize Acceptance Speech on the immortality of the artists, although brief, contained a number of allusions and references to other literary works. However, Faulkner detested the fame and glory that resulted from his recognition. His aversion was so great that his 17-year-old daughter learned of the Nobel Prize only when she was called to the principal's office during the school day. He began by saying: "I feel that this award was not made to me as a man, but to my work – a life's work in the agony and sweat of the human spirit, not for glory and least of all for profit, but to create out of the materials of the human spirit something which did not exist before. So this award is only mine in trust. It will not be difficult to find a dedication for the money part of it commensurate with the purpose and significance of its origin." He donated part of his Nobel money "to establish a fund to support and encourage new fiction writers", eventually resulting in the [William Faulkner Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Faulkner_Foundation "William Faulkner Foundation") (1960–1970).

Controversially, he is noted to have once told [J. Robert Oppenheimer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._Robert_Oppenheimer "J. Robert Oppenheimer") at a buffet supper: "Television is for [niggers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nigger "Nigger")." Faulkner's biographer, [Joseph Blotner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Blotner?action=edit&redlink=1 "Joseph Blotner"), attributes this remark to Faulkner's being "in a situation which had produced boredom and resentment, and he ... struck out verbally in response. He did not want to be there with these people, talking about television or anything else, and he simply choked off the conversation as quickly as he could." Critic Thomas Powers claims that this statement shows how "for many white southerners [like Faulkner] nothing changed with the end of slavery except slavery."

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c4/Faulkner_headstone.jpg/250px-Faulkner_headstone.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Faulkner_headstone.jpg)

Faulkner's headstone located in St. Peter's Cemetery in Oxford, Mississippi, USA

In 1951, Faulkner received the [Chevalier de la Légion d'honneur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legion_of_Honour "Legion of Honour") medal from the government of [France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/France "France").

Faulkner served as the first Writer-in-Residence at the [University of Virginia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Virginia "University of Virginia") at [Charlottesville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlottesville,_Virginia "Charlottesville, Virginia") from February to June 1957 and again in 1958.

In 1961, Faulkner began writing his nineteenth and final novel, *[The Reivers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Reivers "The Reivers")*. The novel is a nostalgic reminiscence, in which an elderly grandfather relates a humorous episode in which he and two boys stole a car to drive to a [Memphis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memphis,_Tennessee "Memphis, Tennessee") bordello. In summer 1961, he finished the first draft. During this time, he injured himself in a series of falls.

On June 17, 1962, Faulkner suffered a serious injury in a fall from his horse, which led to [thrombosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thrombosis "Thrombosis"). He suffered a fatal heart attack on July 6, 1962, at the age of 64, at Wright's Sanatorium in [Byhalia, Mississippi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byhalia,_Mississippi "Byhalia, Mississippi"). Faulkner is buried with his family in St. Peter's Cemetery in Oxford. Faulkner's funeral and graveside service were documented by photographer [Martin J. Dain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Dain "Martin Dain").

## Writing

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/6c/TheFaulknerPortable.jpg/250px-TheFaulknerPortable.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:TheFaulknerPortable.jpg)

One of Faulkner's typewriters

From the early 1920s to the outbreak of World War II, Faulkner published 13 novels and many short stories. This body of work formed the basis of his reputation and earned him the Nobel Prize at age 52. Faulkner's prodigious output include celebrated novels such as *[The Sound and the Fury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Sound_and_the_Fury "The Sound and the Fury")* (1929), *[As I Lay Dying](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/As_I_Lay_Dying "As I Lay Dying")* (1930), *[Light in August](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light_in_August "Light in August")* (1932), and *[Absalom, Absalom!](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absalom,_Absalom! "Absalom, Absalom!")* (1936). He was also a prolific writer of [short stories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Short_story "Short story").

Faulkner's first short story collection, *[These 13](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/These_13 "These 13")* (1931), includes many of his most acclaimed (and most frequently [anthologized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthology "Anthology")) stories, including "[A Rose for Emily](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Rose_for_Emily "A Rose for Emily")", "[Red Leaves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Leaves "Red Leaves")", "[That Evening Sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/That_Evening_Sun "That Evening Sun")", and "[Dry September](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dry_September "Dry September")". He set many of his short stories and novels in [Yoknapatawpha County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoknapatawpha_County "Yoknapatawpha County")—which was based on and nearly geographically identical to Lafayette County (of which his hometown of [Oxford, Mississippi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford,_Mississippi "Oxford, Mississippi"), is the county seat). Yoknapatawpha was Faulkner's "postage stamp", and the bulk of work that it represents is widely considered by critics to amount to one of the most monumental fictional creations in the history of literature. Three of his novels, *[The Hamlet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Hamlet "The Hamlet")*, *[The Town](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Town_(Faulkner_novel) "The Town (Faulkner novel)")* and *[The Mansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Mansion_(novel) "The Mansion (novel)")*, known collectively as the [Snopes trilogy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snopes_trilogy "Snopes trilogy"), document the town of Jefferson and its environs, as an extended family headed by Flem Snopes insinuates itself into the lives and psyches of the general populace. Yoknapatawpha County has been described as a mental landscape.

His short story "[A Rose for Emily](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Rose_for_Emily "A Rose for Emily")" was his first story published in a major magazine, the *Forum*, but received little attention from the public. After revisions and reissues, it gained popularity and is now considered one of his best.

Faulkner wrote two volumes of poetry which were published in small printings, *The Marble Faun* (1924), and *A Green Bough* (1933), and a collection of mystery stories, *[Knight's Gambit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knight's_Gambit "Knight's Gambit")* (1949).

### Style and technique

> The peacefullest words. Peacefullest words. *Non fui. Sum. Fui. Non sum.* Somewhere I heard bells once. Mississippi or Massachusetts. I was. I am not. Massachusetts or Mississippi. Shreve has a bottle in his trunk. *Aren't you even going to open it* Mr and Mrs Jason Richmond Compson announce the *Three times. Days. Aren't you even going to open it* marriage of their daughter Candace *that liquor teaches you to confuse the means with the end* I am. Drink. I was not. Let us sell Benjy's pasture so that Quentin may go to Harvard and I may knock my bones together and together. I will be dead in. Was it one year Caddy said.

— An example of Faulkner's prose in *[The Sound and the Fury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Sound_and_the_Fury "The Sound and the Fury")* (1929)

Carl Rollyson has argued that, "as an artist," Faulkner believed "he should be above worldly concerns and even morality." Faulkner was known for his experimental style with meticulous attention to [diction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diction "Diction") and [cadence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cadence_(poetry) "Cadence (poetry)"). In contrast to the [minimalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimalism "Minimalism") understatement of his contemporary [Ernest Hemingway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_Hemingway "Ernest Hemingway"), Faulkner made frequent use of [stream of consciousness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stream_of_consciousness_(narrative_mode) "Stream of consciousness (narrative mode)") in his writing, and wrote often highly emotional, subtle, cerebral, complex, and sometimes [Gothic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Gothic "Southern Gothic") or [grotesque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grotesque#In_literature "Grotesque") stories of a wide variety of characters including former slaves or descendants of slaves, poor white, agrarian, or working-class Southerners, and Southern aristocrats.

Faulkner's contemporary critical reception was mixed, with *[The New York Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_York_Times "The New York Times")* noting that many critics regarded his work as "raw slabs of pseudorealism that had relatively little merit as serious writing". His style has been described as "impenetrably convoluted".

In an interview with *[The Paris Review](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Paris_Review "The Paris Review")* in 1956, Faulkner remarked:

> Let the writer take up surgery or bricklaying if he is interested in technique. There is no mechanical way to get the writing done, no shortcut. The young writer would be a fool to follow a theory. Teach yourself by your own mistakes; people learn only by error. The good artist believes that nobody is good enough to give him advice. He has supreme vanity. No matter how much he admires the old writer, he wants to beat him.

In that same interview, [Jean Stein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Stein "Jean Stein") says "Some people say they can't understand your writing, even after they read it two or three times. What approach would you suggest for them?" Faulkner replies: "Read it four times."

When asked about his influences, Faulkner says "the books I read are the ones I knew and loved when I was a young man and to which I return as you do to old friends: the [Old Testament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Testament "Old Testament"), [Dickens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Dickens "Charles Dickens"), [Conrad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Conrad "Joseph Conrad"), [Cervantes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miguel_de_Cervantes "Miguel de Cervantes"), *[Don Quixote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Don_Quixote "Don Quixote")—*I read that every year, as some do the Bible. [Flaubert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gustave_Flaubert "Gustave Flaubert"), [Balzac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honoré_de_Balzac "Honoré de Balzac")—he created an intact world of his own, a bloodstream running through twenty books—[Dostoyevsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fyodor_Dostoevsky "Fyodor Dostoevsky"), [Tolstoy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leo_Tolstoy "Leo Tolstoy"), [Shakespeare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Shakespeare "William Shakespeare"). I read [Melville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herman_Melville "Herman Melville") occasionally and, of the poets, [Marlowe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Marlowe "Christopher Marlowe"), [Campion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Campion "Thomas Campion"), [Jonson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ben_Jonson "Ben Jonson"), [Herrick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Herrick_(poet) "Robert Herrick (poet)"), [Donne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Donne "John Donne"), [Keats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Keats "John Keats"), and [Shelley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Percy_Bysshe_Shelley "Percy Bysshe Shelley")."

Like his contemporaries [James Joyce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Joyce "James Joyce") and [T. S. Eliot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T._S._Eliot "T. S. Eliot"), Faulkner uses stories and themes from classic literature in a modern context. Joyce, in *[Ulysses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulysses_(novel) "Ulysses (novel)")*, modeled the journey of his hero [Leopold Bloom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leopold_Bloom "Leopold Bloom") on the adventures of [Odysseus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odysseus "Odysseus"). Eliot, in his essay "Ulysses, Order and Myth", wrote that "In using the myth, in manipulating a continuous parallel between contemporaneity and antiquity, Mr. Joyce is pursuing a method which others must pursue after him. They will not be imitators, any more than the scientist who uses the discoveries of an [Einstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Einstein "Einstein") in pursuing his own, independent, further investigations. It is simply a way of controlling, of ordering, of giving a shape and a significance to the immense panorama of futility and anarchy which is contemporary history." Faulkner's allusions to earlier authors are evidenced by his titles; the title of *[The Sound and the Fury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Sound_and_the_Fury "The Sound and the Fury")* comes from [Macbeth's soliloquy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomorrow_and_tomorrow_and_tomorrow "Tomorrow and tomorrow and tomorrow"): "it is a tale/ Told by an idiot, full of sound and fury,/ Signifying nothing." The opening of the novel is told from the perspective of the intellectually disabled Benjy Compson. The title of *[As I Lay Dying](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/As_I_Lay_Dying "As I Lay Dying")* comes from [Homer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homer "Homer")'s *[Odyssey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odyssey "Odyssey")*, where it is spoken by [Agamemnon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agamemnon "Agamemnon") in the past tense: "As I lay dying, the woman with the dog's eyes would not close my eyes as I descended into Hades." Faulkner's novel, in contrast, is narrated in the present tense. The title of *[Go Down, Moses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Go_Down,_Moses_(book) "Go Down, Moses (book)")* is from an [African American spiritual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Go_Down_Moses "Go Down Moses"), and the book is dedicated "To Mammy / Caroline Barr / Mississippi / [1840–1940] Who was born in slavery and who gave to my family a fidelity without stint or calculation of recompense and to my childhood an immeasurable devotion and love."

### Themes and analysis

Faulkner advocated a gradual abolition of [racial segregation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racial_segregation_in_the_United_States "Racial segregation in the United States") and made racial prejudices the subject of a number of his works including *Intruder in the Dust*, but was critical of forced or fast-moving [desegregation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desegregation "Desegregation"). In 1956, he argued against school segregation in *[Harper's Magazine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harper's_Magazine "Harper's Magazine")*. He argued that [civil rights activists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_rights_activist "Civil rights activist") should "go slow" and be more moderate in their positions. The essayist and novelist [James Baldwin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Baldwin "James Baldwin") was highly critical of Faulkner's views around integration, seeing him as part of a group of educated white Southeners who falsely believed that there could be a middle ground between segregationists and integrationists. [Ralph Ellison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ralph_Ellison "Ralph Ellison") said that "No one in American fiction has done so much to explore the types of Negro personality as has Faulkner."

The [New Critics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Criticism "New Criticism") became interested in Faulkner's work, with [Cleanth Brooks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cleanth_Brooks "Cleanth Brooks") writing *The Yoknapatawpha Country* and Michael Millgate writing *The Achievement of William Faulkner*. Since then, critics have looked at Faulkner's work using other approaches, such as feminist and psychoanalytic methods. Faulkner's works have been placed within the literary traditions of [modernism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literary_modernism "Literary modernism") and the [Southern Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Renaissance "Southern Renaissance").

French philosopher [Albert Camus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Camus "Albert Camus") wrote that Faulkner successfully imported classical [tragedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tragedy "Tragedy") into the 20th century through his "interminably unwinding spiral of words and sentences that conducts the speaker to the abyss of sufferings buried in the past".

### Influence

Faulkner is widely considered a towering figure in [Southern literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_United_States_literature "Southern United States literature"); [Flannery O'Connor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flannery_O'Connor "Flannery O'Connor") wrote that "the presence alone of Faulkner in our midst makes a great difference in what the writer can and cannot permit himself to do. Nobody wants his mule and wagon stalled on the same track the *Dixie Limited* is roaring down". In 1943, while working at Warner Brothers, Faulkner wrote a letter of encouragement to a young Mississippi writer, [Eudora Welty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eudora_Welty "Eudora Welty"). According to critic and translator [Valerie Miles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valerie_Miles "Valerie Miles"), Faulkner's influence on [Latin American fiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_American_literature "Latin American literature") is considerable, with fictional worlds created by [Gabriel García Márquez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gabriel_García_Márquez "Gabriel García Márquez") ([Macondo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macondo "Macondo")) and [Juan Carlos Onetti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juan_Carlos_Onetti "Juan Carlos Onetti") (Santa Maria) being "very much in the vein of" Yoknapatawpha, and that "[Carlos Fuentes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carlos_Fuentes "Carlos Fuentes")'s *[The Death of Artemio Cruz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Death_of_Artemio_Cruz "The Death of Artemio Cruz")* wouldn't exist if not for *[As I Lay Dying](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/As_I_Lay_Dying "As I Lay Dying")*". Fuentes himself cited Faulkner as one of the writers most important to him. Faulkner had great influence on [Mario Vargas Llosa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mario_Vargas_Llosa "Mario Vargas Llosa"), particularly on his early novels *[The Time of the Hero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Time_of_the_Hero "The Time of the Hero")*, *[The Green House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Green_House "The Green House")* and *[Conversation in The Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conversation_in_The_Cathedral "Conversation in The Cathedral")*. Vargas Llosa has claimed that during his student years he learned more from Yoknapatawpha than from classes. Jorge Luis Borges is credited with the translation of Faulkner's *[The Wild Palms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Wild_Palms "The Wild Palms")* into Spanish, although [Douglas Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Douglas_Day "Douglas Day") believes it's not impossible that Borges' mother may have done the translation. Day also believes that Borges' deep immersion in and study of *The Wild Palms* may have influenced him to abandon the novel as his own writing form of choice.

The works of William Faulkner are a clear influence on the French novelist [Claude Simon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claude_Simon "Claude Simon"), and the Portuguese novelist [António Lobo Antunes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/António_Lobo_Antunes "António Lobo Antunes"). [Cormac McCarthy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cormac_McCarthy "Cormac McCarthy") has been described as a "disciple of Faulkner".

In *[The Elements of Style](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Elements_of_Style "The Elements of Style")*, [E. B. White](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E._B._White "E. B. White") cites Faulkner: "If the experiences of [Walter Mitty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Mitty "Walter Mitty"), of [Dick Diver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tender_Is_the_Night "Tender Is the Night"), of [Rabbit Angstrom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rabbit_Angstrom "Rabbit Angstrom") have seemed for the moment real to countless readers, if in reading Faulkner we have almost the sense of inhabiting Yoknapatawpha County during the decline of the South, it is because the details used are definite, the terms concrete." Later, Faulkner's style is contrasted with that of [Hemingway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_Hemingway "Ernest Hemingway").

After his death, Estelle and their daughter, Jill, lived at Rowan Oak until Estelle's death in 1972. The property was sold to the [University of Mississippi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Mississippi "University of Mississippi") that same year. The house and furnishings are maintained much as they were in Faulkner's day. Faulkner's scribblings are preserved on the wall, including the day-by-day outline covering a week he wrote on the walls of his small study to help him keep track of the plot twists in his novel *[A Fable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Fable "A Fable")*. Some of Faulkner's Nobel Prize winnings went to establish the [William Faulkner Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Faulkner_Foundation "William Faulkner Foundation"). It gave an Award for Notable First Novel; winners included [John Knowles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Knowles "John Knowles")'s *[A Separate Peace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Separate_Peace "A Separate Peace")*, [Thomas Pynchon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Pynchon "Thomas Pynchon")'s *[V.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V. "V.")*, [Cormac McCarthy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cormac_McCarthy "Cormac McCarthy")'s *[The Orchard Keeper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Orchard_Keeper "The Orchard Keeper")*, [Robert Coover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Coover "Robert Coover")'s *[The Origin of the Brunists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Origin_of_the_Brunists "The Origin of the Brunists")* and [Frederick Exley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_Exley "Frederick Exley")'s *[A Fan's Notes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Fan's_Notes "A Fan's Notes")*. Starting in 1981, this became the [PEN/Faulkner Award for Fiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PEN/Faulkner_Award_for_Fiction "PEN/Faulkner Award for Fiction"), founded by, among others, [Mary Lee Settle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Lee_Settle "Mary Lee Settle") as an alternative to the National Book Award.

Some of Faulkner's works have been adapted into films. They have received a polarized response, with many critics contending that Faulkner's works are "unfilmable". Faulkner's final work, *The Reivers*, was adapted into a [1969 film](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Reivers_(film) "The Reivers (film)") starring [Steve McQueen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steve_McQueen "Steve McQueen"). [Tommy Lee Jones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tommy_Lee_Jones "Tommy Lee Jones")'s neo-Western film *[The Three Burials of Melquiades Estrada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Three_Burials_of_Melquiades_Estrada "The Three Burials of Melquiades Estrada")* was partly based on Faulkner's *[As I Lay Dying](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/As_I_Lay_Dying "As I Lay Dying")*.

During the [Nazi Occupation of France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_military_administration_in_occupied_France_during_World_War_II "German military administration in occupied France during World War II") in World War II, the German occupiers banned American literature. A black-market of American books emerged, and reading works by Hemingway and Faulkner became an act of defiance. Faulkner remains especially popular in France, where a 2009 poll found him the second most popular writer (after only [Marcel Proust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcel_Proust "Marcel Proust")). Contemporary [Jean-Paul Sartre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Paul_Sartre "Jean-Paul Sartre") stated that "for young people in France, Faulkner is a god", and [Albert Camus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Camus "Albert Camus") made a stage adaptation of Faulkner's *[Requiem for a Nun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Requiem_for_a_Nun "Requiem for a Nun")*. In [Jean-Luc Godard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Luc_Godard "Jean-Luc Godard")'s *[Breathless](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breathless_(1960_film) "Breathless (1960 film)")*, Patricia ([Jean Seberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Seberg "Jean Seberg")) quotes *The Wild Palms*: "Between grief and nothing, I will take grief."

He also won the U.S. [National Book Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Book_Award "National Book Award") twice, for *Collected Stories* in 1951 and *[A Fable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Fable "A Fable")* in 1955.

The [United States Postal Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Postal_Service "United States Postal Service") issued a 22-cent postage stamp in his honor on August 3, 1987. Faulkner had once served as Postmaster at the University of Mississippi, and in his letter of resignation in 1923 wrote:

> As long as I live under the capitalistic system, I expect to have my life influenced by the demands of moneyed people. But I will be damned if I propose to be at the beck and call of every itinerant scoundrel who has two cents to invest in a postage stamp.
> This, sir, is my resignation.

On October 10, 2019, a [Mississippi Writers Trail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mississippi_Writers_Trail "Mississippi Writers Trail") historical marker was installed at [Rowan Oak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rowan_Oak "Rowan Oak") in Oxford, Mississippi, honoring the contributions of William Faulkner to the American literary landscape.

Faulkner has a tribute on Mississippi's float for the 137th [Rose Parade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rose_Parade "Rose Parade").

### Collections

The manuscripts of most of Faulkner's works, correspondence, personal papers, and over 300 books from his working library reside at the [Albert and Shirley Small Special Collections Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_and_Shirley_Small_Special_Collections_Library "Albert and Shirley Small Special Collections Library") at the [University of Virginia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Virginia "University of Virginia"), where he spent much of his time in his final years. The library also houses some of the writer's personal effects and the papers of major Faulkner associates and scholars, such as his biographer [Joseph Blotner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Blotner?action=edit&redlink=1 "Joseph Blotner"), bibliographer Linton Massey, and Random House editor Albert Erskine.

[Southeast Missouri State University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southeast_Missouri_State_University "Southeast Missouri State University"), where the [Center for Faulkner Studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_for_Faulkner_Studies "Center for Faulkner Studies") is located, also owns a generous collection of Faulkner materials, including first editions, manuscripts, letters, photographs, artwork, and many materials pertaining to Faulkner's time in Hollywood. The university possesses many personal files and letters kept by [Joseph Blotner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Blotner?action=edit&redlink=1 "Joseph Blotner"), along with books and letters that once belonged to Malcolm Cowley. The university achieved the collection due to a generous donation by Louis Daniel Brodsky, a collector of Faulkner materials, in 1989.

Further significant Faulkner materials reside at the [University of Mississippi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Mississippi "University of Mississippi"), the [Harry Ransom Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harry_Ransom_Center "Harry Ransom Center"), and the [New York Public Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_Public_Library "New York Public Library").

The Random House records at Columbia University also include letters by and to Faulkner.

In 1966, the [United States Military Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Military_Academy "United States Military Academy") dedicated a William Faulkner Room in its library.
