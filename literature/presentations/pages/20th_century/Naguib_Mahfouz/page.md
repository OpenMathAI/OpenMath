**Naguib Mahfouz Abdelaziz Ibrahim Ahmed Al-Basha** (11 December 1911 – 30 August 2006) was an Egyptian [writer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Writer "Writer") who won the [1988 Nobel Prize in Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1988_Nobel_Prize_in_Literature "1988 Nobel Prize in Literature"). In awarding the prize, the [Swedish Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swedish_Academy "Swedish Academy") described him as a writer "who, through works rich in nuance – now clear-sightedly realistic, now evocatively ambiguous – has formed an Egyptian narrative art that applies to all mankind".

Mahfouz is regarded as one of the first contemporary writers in [Egyptian literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian_literature "Egyptian literature"), along with [Taha Hussein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taha_Hussein "Taha Hussein"), to explore themes of [existentialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Existentialism "Existentialism"). He is the only Egyptian to win the [Nobel Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize "Nobel Prize") in Literature. He published 35 novels, over 350 short stories, 26 screenplays, hundreds of op-ed columns for Egyptian newspapers, and seven plays over a 70-year career, from the 1930s until 2004. All of his novels are set in Egypt, and always mention the concept of "the lane" as a microcosm of the world. His most famous works include *[The Cairo Trilogy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cairo_Trilogy "Cairo Trilogy")* and *[Children of Gebelawi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Children_of_Gebelawi "Children of Gebelawi")*. Many of Mahfouz's works have been adapted into [Egyptian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian_cinema "Egyptian cinema") and international films, making him one of the most widely adapted Egyptian authors. While Mahfouz's literature is classified as [realist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literary_realism "Literary realism") literature, [existential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Existentialism "Existentialism") themes appear in it.

## Early life and education

Mahfouz was born in a lower middle-class Muslim Egyptian family in [Old Cairo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Cairo "Old Cairo") in 1911. The first part of his compound given name was chosen in appreciation of the well-known obstetrician, [Naguib Pasha Mahfouz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naguib_Pasha_Mahfouz "Naguib Pasha Mahfouz"), who oversaw his difficult birth. Mahfouz was the seventh and the youngest child, with four brothers and two sisters, all of them much older than he. (Experientially, he grew up an "only child".) The family lived in two popular districts of Cairo: first, in the Bayt al-Qadi neighborhood in the Gamaleya quarter in the old city, from where they moved in 1924 to [Abbaseya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbassia "Abbassia"), then a new Cairo suburb north of the old city, locations that would provide the backdrop for many of Mahfouz's later writings. His father, Abdel-Aziz Ibrahim, whom Mahfouz described as having been "old-fashioned", was a [civil servant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_servant "Civil servant"), and Mahfouz eventually followed in his footsteps in 1934. Mahfouz's mother, Fatimah, was the daughter of Mustafa Qasheesha, an [Al-Azhar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Azhar "Al-Azhar") sheikh, and although illiterate herself, took the boy Mahfouz on numerous excursions to cultural locations such as the [Egyptian Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian_Museum "Egyptian Museum") and the [Pyramids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian_Pyramids "Egyptian Pyramids").

The Mahfouz family were devout Muslims and Mahfouz had a strict Islamic upbringing. In an interview, he elaborated on the stern religious climate at home during his childhood. He stated, "You would never have thought that an artist would emerge from that family."

The [Egyptian Revolution of 1919](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian_Revolution_of_1919 "Egyptian Revolution of 1919") had a strong effect on Mahfouz, although he was at the time only seven years old. From the window he saw British soldiers firing at the demonstrators in an effort to disperse them. According to Mahfouz, "You could say ... that the one thing which most shook the security of my childhood was the 1919 revolution", he later said.

In his early years, Mahfouz read extensively and was influenced by Hafiz Najib, [Taha Hussein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taha_Hussein "Taha Hussein") and [Salama Moussa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salama_Moussa "Salama Moussa"), the [Fabian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fabian_Society "Fabian Society") intellectual.

After completing his secondary education, Mahfouz was admitted in 1930 to the [Egyptian University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian_University "Egyptian University") (now [Cairo University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cairo_University "Cairo University")), where he studied [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy "Philosophy"), graduating in 1934. By 1936, having spent a year working on an M.A. in philosophy, he decided to discontinue his studies and become a professional writer. He published his first work in *[Al Majalla Al Jadida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al_Majalla_Al_Jadida "Al Majalla Al Jadida")*, a magazine started by [Salama Musa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salama_Moussa "Salama Moussa") in 1929. Mahfouz then worked as a journalist for *[Arrissalah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arrissalah "Arrissalah")*, and contributed short stories to *[Al-Hilal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Hilal_(magazine) "Al-Hilal (magazine)")* and *[Al-Ahram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Ahram "Al-Ahram")*.

## Civil service

After receiving his bachelor's degree in philosophy from [Cairo University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cairo_University "Cairo University") in 1934, Mahfouz joined the Egyptian civil service, where he continued to work in various positions and ministries until retirement in 1971. He served first as a clerk at Cairo University, then, in 1938, in the [Ministry of Islamic Endowments (Awqaf)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Awqaf_(Egypt) "Ministry of Awqaf (Egypt)") as parliamentary secretary to the Minister of Islamic Endowments. In 1945, he requested a transfer to the [al-Ghuri Mausoleum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sultan_Al-Ghuri_Complex "Sultan Al-Ghuri Complex") library, where he interviewed residents of his childhood neighborhood as part of the "Good Loans Project". In the 1950s, he worked as Director of Censorship in the Bureau of Arts, as Director of the Foundation for the Support of the Cinema, and finally as a consultant to the [Ministry of Culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Culture_(Egypt) "Ministry of Culture (Egypt)").

## Writing career

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/47/Naguib_Mahfouz_in_1960s.jpg/250px-Naguib_Mahfouz_in_1960s.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Naguib_Mahfouz_in_1960s.jpg)

Mahfouz in 1960s

Mahfouz published 34 novels, over 350 short stories, dozens of screenplays, and five plays over a 70-year career. Possibly his most famous work, [*The Cairo Trilogy*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cairo_Trilogy "Cairo Trilogy"), depicts the lives of three generations of different families in Cairo from [World War I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Front_(World_War_I) "Eastern Front (World War I)") until after the [1952 military coup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian_revolution_of_1952 "Egyptian revolution of 1952") that overthrew [King Farouk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farouk_of_Egypt "Farouk of Egypt"). He was a board member of the publisher *Dar el-Ma'aref*. Many of his novels were serialized in *Al-Ahram*, and his writings also appeared in his weekly column, "Point of View". Before the Nobel Prize only a few of his novels had appeared in the West.

### Writing style and themes

Most of Mahfouz's early works were set in [Cairo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cairo "Cairo"). *Abath Al-Aqdar (Mockery of the Fates)* (1939), *Rhadopis* (1943), and *Kifah Tibah (The Struggle of Thebes)* (1944) were historical novels written as part of a larger unfulfilled 30-novel project. Inspired by [Sir Walter Scott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_Walter_Scott "Sir Walter Scott") (1771–1832), Mahfouz planned to cover the entire [history of Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Egypt "History of Egypt") in a series of books. However, following the third volume, his interest shifted to current settings and issues, as well as the psychological impact of [social change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_change "Social change") on ordinary people.

Mahfouz's [prose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prose "Prose") is characterised by the blunt expression of his ideas. His written works cover a broad range of topics, including the controversial and taboo such as [socialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialism "Socialism"), homosexuality, and God. Writing about some of these subjects was prohibited in Egypt.

Mahfouz's works often deal with [Egypt's development during the 20th century](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_modern_Egypt "History of modern Egypt"), and combined intellectual and cultural influences from both East and West. His own exposure to [foreign literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_literature "World literature") began in his youth with the enthusiastic consumption of [Western detective stories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Detective_fiction "Detective fiction"), [Russian classics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_literature "Russian literature"), and [modernist writers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modernism "Modernism") as [Marcel Proust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcel_Proust "Marcel Proust"), [Franz Kafka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_Kafka "Franz Kafka") and [James Joyce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Joyce "James Joyce"). Mahfouz's stories are almost always set in the heavily populated urban quarters of Cairo, where his characters, usually ordinary people, try to cope with the modernization of society and the temptations of [Western values](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Values_(Western_philosophy) "Values (Western philosophy)").

Mahfouz's central work in the 1950s was the *[Cairo Trilogy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cairo_Trilogy "Cairo Trilogy")*, which he completed before the [July Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian_Revolution_of_1952 "Egyptian Revolution of 1952"). The novels were titled with the street names *[Palace Walk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palace_Walk "Palace Walk")*, *[Palace of Desire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palace_of_Desire_(novel) "Palace of Desire (novel)")*, and *[Sugar Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sugar_Street_(Cairo) "Sugar Street (Cairo)")*. Mahfouz set the story in the parts of Cairo where he grew up. The novels depict the life of the patriarch el-Sayyed Ahmed Abdel Gawad and his family over three generations, from [World War I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I "World War I") until 1944. Mahfouz stopped writing for some years after finishing the trilogy.

Disappointed in the [Nasser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamal_Abdel_Nasser "Gamal Abdel Nasser") régime, which had overthrown the monarchy in 1952, he started publishing again in 1959, now prolifically pouring out novels, short stories, journalism, memoirs, essays, and screenplays. He stated in a 1998 interview that he "long felt that Nasser was one of the greatest political leaders in modern history. I only began to fully appreciate him after he nationalized the [Suez Canal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suez_Canal "Suez Canal")." His non-fiction, including his journalism and essays and his writing on literature and philosophy, were published in four volumes from 2016.

His 1966 novel *Tharthara Fawq Al-Nīl* ([Adrift on the Nile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adrift_on_the_Nile "Adrift on the Nile")) is one of his most popular works. It was later made into a film called *[Chitchat on the Nile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chitchat_on_the_Nile "Chitchat on the Nile")* during the régime of [Anwar al-Sadat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anwar_al-Sadat "Anwar al-Sadat"). The story criticizes the decadence of Egyptian society during the Nasser era. It was [banned](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_books_banned_by_governments "List of books banned by governments") by [Sadat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anwar_Sadat "Anwar Sadat") to avoid provoking Egyptians who still loved former president Nasser. Copies of the [banned book](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_censorship "Book censorship") were hard to find prior to the late 1990s.

The *[Children of Gebelawi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Children_of_Gebelawi "Children of Gebelawi")* (1959, also known as *Children of the Alley*), one of Mahfouz's best known works, portrayed the patriarch Gebelaawi and his children, average Egyptians living the lives of [Cain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cain "Cain") and [Abel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abel "Abel"), [Moses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moses "Moses"), [Jesus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesus "Jesus"), and [Mohammed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mohammed "Mohammed"). Gebelawi builds a mansion in an [oasis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oasis "Oasis") in the middle of a barren desert; his estate becomes the scene of a family feud that continues for generations. "Whenever someone is depressed, suffering or humiliated, he points to the mansion at the top of the alley at the end opening out to the desert, and says sadly, 'That is our ancestor's house, we are all his children, and we have a right to his property. Why are we starving? What have we done?'" The book was [banned throughout the Arab world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Censorship_in_Islamic_societies "Censorship in Islamic societies") except in [Lebanon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebanon "Lebanon") until 2006 when it was first published in Egypt. The work was prohibited because of its alleged [blasphemy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blasphemy_law_in_Egypt "Blasphemy law in Egypt") through the [allegorical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allegory "Allegory") portrayal of God and the [monotheistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotheistic "Monotheistic") [Abrahamic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abrahamic "Abrahamic") faiths of Judaism, Christianity, and [Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam "Islam").

In the 1960s, Mahfouz further developed the theme that humanity is moving further away from God in his [existentialist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Existentialism "Existentialism") novels. In *[The Thief and the Dogs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Thief_and_the_Dogs "The Thief and the Dogs")* (1961) he depicted the fate of a [Marxist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marxism "Marxism") thief who has been released from prison and plans revenge.

[Tharwat Abaza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tharwat_Abaza "Tharwat Abaza") and Naguib Mahfouz

In the 1960s and 1970s, Mahfouz began to construct his novels more freely and often used [interior monologues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internal_monologue "Internal monologue"). In [*Miramar*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miramar_(novel) "Miramar (novel)") (1967) he employed a form of multiple [First-person narratives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First-person_narrative "First-person narrative"). Four narrators, among them a Socialist and a [Nasserite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nasserism "Nasserism") opportunist, represent different political views. In the center of the story is an attractive servant girl. In *[Arabian Nights and Days](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabian_Nights_and_Days "Arabian Nights and Days")* (1979) and in [*The Journey of Ibn Fatouma*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Journey_of_Ibn_Fattouma "The Journey of Ibn Fattouma") (1983) he drew on traditional Arabic narratives as [subtexts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subtext "Subtext"). [*Akhenaten: Dweller in Truth*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akhenaten,_Dweller_in_Truth "Akhenaten, Dweller in Truth") (1985) deals with conflict between old and new religious truths.

Many of his novels were first published in serialized form, including *Children of Gebelawi* and *[Midaq Alley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midaq_Alley_(novel) "Midaq Alley (novel)")* which was also adapted into a [Mexican film](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cinema_of_Mexico "Cinema of Mexico") starring [Salma Hayek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salma_Hayek "Salma Hayek") called [*El callejón de los milagros*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midaq_Alley_(film) "Midaq Alley (film)").

### Political influence

Most of Mahfouz's writings deal mainly with politics, a fact he acknowledged: "In all my writings, you will find politics. You may find a story which ignores love or any other subject, but not politics; it is the very axis of our thinking".

He espoused [Egyptian nationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian_nationalism "Egyptian nationalism") in many of his works, and expressed sympathies for the post-World-War-era [Wafd Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wafd_Party "Wafd Party"). He was also attracted to socialist and [democratic ideals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democratic_ideals "Democratic ideals") early in his youth. The influence of socialist ideals is strongly reflected in his first two novels, *Al-Khalili* and *New Cairo*, as well as many of his later works. Parallel to his sympathy for socialism and democracy was his [antipathy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antipathy "Antipathy") towards [Islamic extremism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_extremism "Islamic extremism").

In his youth, Mahfouz had personally known [Sayyid Qutb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sayyid_Qutb "Sayyid Qutb") when Qutb was showing a greater interest in [literary criticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literary_criticism "Literary criticism") than in [Islamic fundamentalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_fundamentalism "Islamic fundamentalism"); Qutb later became a significant influence on the [Muslim Brotherhood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_Brotherhood "Muslim Brotherhood"). In the mid-1940s, Qutb was one of the first critics to recognize Mahfouz's talent, and by the 1960s, near the end of Qutb's life, Mahfouz even visited him in the hospital. But later, in the semi-autobiographical novel *[Mirrors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mirrors_(novel) "Mirrors (novel)")*, Mahfouz drew a negative portrait of Qutb. He was disillusioned with the [1952 revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian_revolution_of_1952 "Egyptian revolution of 1952") and by Egypt's defeat in the 1967 [Six-Day War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Six-Day_War "Six-Day War"). He had supported the principles of the revolution, but became disenchanted, saying that the practices failed to live up to the original ideals.

Mahfouz's writing influenced a new generation of Egyptian lawyers, including Nabil Mounir and Reda Aslan.

### Reception

Mahfouz's translated works received praise from American critics:

"The alleys, the houses, the palaces and mosques and the people who live among them are evoked as vividly in Mahfouz's work as the streets of London were conjured by Dickens."
—*[Newsweek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newsweek "Newsweek")*

"Throughout Naguib Mahfouz's fiction there is a pervasive sense of metaphor, of a literary artist who is using his fiction to speak directly and unequivocally to the condition of his country. His work is imbued with love for Egypt and its people, but it is also utterly honest and unsentimental."
—*[Washington Post](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington_Post "Washington Post")*

"Mahfouz's work is freshly nuanced and hauntingly lyrical. The Nobel Prize acknowledges the universal significance of [his] fiction."
—*[Los Angeles Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Los_Angeles_Times "Los Angeles Times")*

"Mr. Mahfouz embodied the essence of what makes the bruising, raucous, chaotic human anthill of Cairo possible."
—*[The Economist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Economist "The Economist")*

#### Nobel Prize for Literature

Mahfouz was awarded the 1988 Nobel Prize in Literature, the only Arab writer to have won the award. Shortly after winning the prize Mahfouz was quoted as saying:

> The Nobel Prize has given me, for the first time in my life, the feeling that my literature could be appreciated on an international level. The Arab world also won the Nobel with me. I believe that international doors have opened, and that from now on, literate people will consider Arab literature also. We deserve that recognition.

The Swedish letter to Mahfouz praised his "rich and complex work":

> [It] invites us to reconsider the fundamental things in life. Themes like the nature of time and love, society and norms, knowledge and faith recur in a variety of situations and are presented in thought-provoking, evocative, and clearly daring ways. And the poetic quality of your prose can be felt across the language barrier. In the prize citation you are credited with the forming of an Arabian narrative art that applies to all mankind.

Because Mahfouz found traveling to Sweden difficult at his age, he did not attend the award ceremony.

### Political involvement

Mahfouz did not shrink from controversy outside of his work. As a consequence of his support for Sadat's [Camp David peace treaty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camp_David_Accords "Camp David Accords") with Israel in 1978, his books were banned in many Arab countries until after he won the Nobel Prize. Like many Egyptian writers and intellectuals, Mahfouz was on an Islamic fundamentalist "death list".

He defended British-Indian writer [Salman Rushdie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salman_Rushdie "Salman Rushdie") after Ayatollah [Ruhollah Khomeini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruhollah_Khomeini "Ruhollah Khomeini") condemned Rushdie to death in a 1989 [fatwa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Satanic_Verses_controversy "The Satanic Verses controversy"), but also criticized Rushdie's novel *[The Satanic Verses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Satanic_Verses "The Satanic Verses")* as "insulting" to Islam. Mahfouz believed in [freedom of expression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_of_speech "Freedom of speech"), and, although he did not personally agree with Rushdie's work, he spoke out against the *fatwa* condemning him to death for it.

In 1989, after Ayatollah Khomeini's *fatwa* calling for Rushdie and his publishers to be killed, Mahfouz called Khomeini a terrorist. Shortly after, Mahfouz joined 80 other intellectuals in declaring that "no [blasphemy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blasphemy "Blasphemy") harms Islam and Muslims so much as the call for murdering a writer."

## Assassination attempt and aftermath

The publication of *[The Satanic Verses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Satanic_Verses "The Satanic Verses")* revived the controversy surrounding Mahfouz's novel *[Children of Gebelawi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Children_of_Gebelawi "Children of Gebelawi")*. Death threats against Mahfouz followed, including one from the "blind sheikh", Egyptian-born [Omar Abdel-Rahman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omar_Abdel-Rahman "Omar Abdel-Rahman"). Mahfouz was given police protection, but on October 14, 1994, an extremist succeeded in attacking the 82-year-old novelist by stabbing him in the neck outside his Cairo home.

He survived, permanently affected by damage to nerves of his right upper limb. Sixteen people were put on a military trial, and two of them received death penalty and eventually hanged. After the incident, Mahfouz was unable to write for more than a few minutes a day and consequently produced fewer and fewer works. Subsequently, he lived under constant bodyguard protection. Finally, in the beginning of 2006, the novel was published in Egypt with a preface written by Ahmad Kamal Aboul-Magd.

## Personal life

Mahfouz remained a bachelor until age 43 because he believed that, with its numerous restrictions and limitations, marriage would hamper his literary future. "I was afraid of marriage... especially when I saw how busy my brothers and sisters were with social events because of it. This one went to visit people, that one invited people. I had the impression that married life would take up all my time. I saw myself drowning in visits and parties. No freedom."

However, in 1954, he quietly married a [Coptic Orthodox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coptic_Orthodox_Church "Coptic Orthodox Church") woman from [Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandria "Alexandria"), Atiyyatallah Ibrahim, with whom he had two daughters, Fatima and Umm Kalthum. The couple initially lived on a houseboat in the [Agouza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agouza "Agouza") section of Cairo on the west bank of the [Nile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nile "Nile"), then moved to an apartment along the river in the same area. Mahfouz avoided public exposure, especially inquiries into his private life, which might have become, as he put it, "a silly topic in journals and radio programs."

Mahfouz distinctly did not like to travel. [Belgrade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belgrade "Belgrade") was one of the few cities to which he gladly went and he expressed great respect for [Serbia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serbia "Serbia").

Mahfouz died on 30 August 2006 after complications from two falls, which he had suffered at home and in a hospital.

## Legacy

Mahfouz's legacy is considered a cornerstone of Modern Egyptian culture. His books are frequently republished, and the Cairo International Book Fair celebrated Mahfouz more than once.

His books continue to be adapted into films and TV series, both in Egypt and internationally, such as Mexican adaptation of [Midaq Alley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midaq_Alley_(film) "Midaq Alley (film)") starring Salma Hayek in 1995, and Egyptian TV series Afrah AlQoba, Bayn El Samaa Wa El Ard and Hadith Alsabah wa Almassaa among others.

In 2019, Egyptian Ministry of Culture opened the [Naguib Mahfouz museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naguib_Mahfouz_museum?action=edit&redlink=1 "Naguib Mahfouz museum") located in Old Cairo near [Wikala of al-Ghuri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikala_of_al-Ghuri "Wikala of al-Ghuri"), Muzz Street and [Azhar mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Azhar_Mosque "Al-Azhar Mosque"), where most of Mahfouz novels take place. the museum has different artifacts from Mahfouz's life such as his hat, desk, photographs and his awards including his Nobel Medal.

In 2021, Egyptian actor [Ahmed Helmy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahmed_Helmy "Ahmed Helmy") announced plans to star in a biographical Television series about Mahfouz's life, written by Abdelreheem Kamal.
