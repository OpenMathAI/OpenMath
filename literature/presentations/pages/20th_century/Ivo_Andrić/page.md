**Ivo Andrić** ([Serbian Cyrillic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serbian_Cyrillic_alphabet "Serbian Cyrillic alphabet"): Иво Андрић, pronounced [[ǐːʋo ǎːndritɕ]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Serbo-Croatian "Help:IPA/Serbo-Croatian"); born **Ivan Andrić**; 9 October 1892 – 13 March 1975) was a Yugoslav novelist, poet and [short story writer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Short_story_writer "Short story writer") who won the [Nobel Prize in Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Literature "Nobel Prize in Literature") in [1961](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1961_Nobel_Prize_in_Literature "1961 Nobel Prize in Literature"). His writings dealt mainly with life in his native [Bosnia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bosnia_(region) "Bosnia (region)") under [Ottoman rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Bosnia_and_Herzegovina "Ottoman Bosnia and Herzegovina").

Born in [Travnik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Travnik "Travnik") in [Austria-Hungary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austria-Hungary "Austria-Hungary"), modern-day [Bosnia and Herzegovina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bosnia_and_Herzegovina "Bosnia and Herzegovina"), Andrić attended high school in [Sarajevo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarajevo "Sarajevo"), where he became an active member of several [South Slav](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Slavs "South Slavs") national youth organizations. Following the [assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assassination_of_Archduke_Franz_Ferdinand "Assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand") in June 1914, Andrić was arrested and imprisoned by the Austro-Hungarian police, who suspected his involvement in the plot. As the authorities were unable to build a strong case against him, he spent much of the war under [house arrest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_arrest "House arrest"), only being released following a general amnesty for such cases in July 1917. After the war, he studied South Slavic history and literature at universities in [Zagreb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zagreb "Zagreb") and [Graz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graz "Graz"), eventually attaining his PhD. in Graz in 1924. He worked in the diplomatic service of the [Kingdom of Yugoslavia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Yugoslavia "Kingdom of Yugoslavia") from 1920 to 1923 and again from 1924 to 1941. In 1939, he became Yugoslavia's ambassador to [Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi_Germany "Nazi Germany"), but his tenure ended in April 1941 with the [German-led invasion of his country](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invasion_of_Yugoslavia "Invasion of Yugoslavia"). Shortly after the invasion, Andrić returned to German-occupied [Belgrade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belgrade "Belgrade"). He lived quietly in a friend's apartment for the duration of World War II, in conditions likened by some biographers to house arrest, and wrote some of his most important works, including *[Na Drini ćuprija](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Bridge_on_the_Drina "The Bridge on the Drina")* (*The Bridge on the Drina*).

Following the war, Andrić was named to a number of ceremonial posts in Yugoslavia, which had since come under communist rule. In 1961, the [Nobel Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Committee "Nobel Committee") awarded him the Nobel Prize in Literature, selecting him over writers such as [J. R. R. Tolkien](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._R._R._Tolkien "J. R. R. Tolkien"), [Robert Frost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Frost "Robert Frost"), [John Steinbeck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Steinbeck "John Steinbeck") and [E. M. Forster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E._M._Forster "E. M. Forster"). The Committee cited "the epic force with which he ... traced themes and depicted human destinies drawn from his country's history". Afterwards, Andrić's works found an international audience and were translated into a number of languages. In subsequent years, he received a number of awards in his native country. Andrić's health declined substantially in late 1974 and he died in Belgrade the following March.

In the years following Andrić's death, the Belgrade apartment where he spent much of World War II was converted into a museum and a nearby street corner was named in his honour. A number of other cities in the former [Yugoslavia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yugoslavia "Yugoslavia") also have streets bearing his name. In 2012, filmmaker [Emir Kusturica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emir_Kusturica "Emir Kusturica") began construction of an ethno-town in eastern Bosnia that is [named after Andrić](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrićgrad "Andrićgrad"). As Yugoslavia's only Nobel Prize-winning writer, Andrić was well known and respected in his native country during his lifetime. In Bosnia and Herzegovina, beginning in the 1950s and continuing past the [breakup of Yugoslavia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breakup_of_Yugoslavia "Breakup of Yugoslavia"), his works have been disparaged by Bosniak literary critics for their supposed anti-Muslim bias. In Croatia, his works had occasionally been blacklisted following Yugoslavia's dissolution in the 1990s, but were rehabilitated by the literary community. He is highly regarded in Serbia for his contributions to [Serbian literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serbian_literature "Serbian literature").

## Early life

### Family

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/6d/Rodna_ku%C4%87a_Ive_Andri%C4%87a_u_Travniku_0.jpg/250px-Rodna_ku%C4%87a_Ive_Andri%C4%87a_u_Travniku_0.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Rodna_kuća_Ive_Andrića_u_Travniku_0.jpg)

The house in [Travnik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Travnik "Travnik") in which Andrić was born

Ivan Andrić was born in the village of [Dolac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dolac,_Travnik "Dolac, Travnik"), near [Travnik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Travnik "Travnik"), on 9 October 1892, while his mother, Katarina ([née](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_name#Maiden_and_married_names "Birth name") Pejić), was in the town visiting relatives. Andrić was of [Catholic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Church "Catholic Church") and [Bosnian Croat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Croats_of_Bosnia_and_Herzegovina "Croats of Bosnia and Herzegovina") origin. He was his parents' only child. His father, Antun, was a struggling [silversmith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silversmith "Silversmith") who resorted to working as a school janitor in [Sarajevo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarajevo "Sarajevo"), where he lived with his wife and infant son. At the age of 32, Antun died of [tuberculosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuberculosis "Tuberculosis"), like most of his siblings. Andrić was only two years old at the time. Widowed and penniless, Andrić's mother took him to [Višegrad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Višegrad "Višegrad") and placed him in the care of her sister-in-law Ana and brother-in-law Ivan Matković, a police officer. The couple were financially stable but childless, so they agreed to look after the infant and brought him up as their own. Meanwhile, Andrić's mother returned to Sarajevo seeking employment.

Andrić was raised in a country that had changed little since the [Ottoman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Empire "Ottoman Empire") period despite being [mandated to Austria-Hungary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austro-Hungarian_rule_in_Bosnia_and_Herzegovina "Austro-Hungarian rule in Bosnia and Herzegovina") at the [Congress of Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congress_of_Berlin "Congress of Berlin") in 1878. Eastern and Western culture intermingled in Bosnia to a far greater extent than anywhere else in the Balkan peninsula. Having lived there from an early age, Andrić came to cherish Višegrad, calling it "my real home". Though it was a small provincial town (or *kasaba*), Višegrad proved to be an enduring source of inspiration. It was a multi-ethnic and multi-confessional town, the predominant groups being [Serbs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serbs "Serbs") (Orthodox Christians) and [Bosniaks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bosniaks "Bosniaks") (Muslims). From an early age, Andrić closely observed the customs of the local people. These customs, and the particularities of life in eastern [Bosnia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bosnia_(region) "Bosnia (region)"), would later be detailed in his works. Andrić made his first friends in Višegrad, playing with them along the [Drina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drina "Drina") River and the town's famous [Mehmed Paša Sokolović Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mehmed_Paša_Sokolović_Bridge "Mehmed Paša Sokolović Bridge").

### Primary and secondary education

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/1c/Ivo_Andri%C4%87_Vi%C5%A1egrad1.jpg/250px-Ivo_Andri%C4%87_Vi%C5%A1egrad1.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Ivo_Andrić_Višegrad1.jpg)

The house in [Višegrad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Višegrad "Višegrad") where Andrić was raised

At age six, Andrić began [primary school](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primary_school "Primary school"). He later recounted that these were the happiest days of his life. At the age of ten, he received a three-year [scholarship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scholarship "Scholarship") from a Croat cultural group called *[Napredak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HKD_Napredak "HKD Napredak")* (Progress) to study in Sarajevo. In the autumn of 1902, he was registered at the Great Sarajevo Gymnasium ([Serbo-Croatian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serbo-Croatian_language "Serbo-Croatian language"): *Velika Sarajevska gimnazija*), the oldest [secondary school](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secondary_school "Secondary school") in Bosnia. While in Sarajevo, Andrić lived with his mother, who worked in a rug factory. At the time, the city was overflowing with civil servants from all parts of Austria-Hungary, and thus many languages could be heard in its restaurants, cafés and on its streets. Culturally, the city boasted a strong Germanic element, and the curriculum in educational institutions was designed to reflect this. From a total of 83 teachers that worked at Andrić's school over a twenty-year period, only three were natives of Bosnia and Herzegovina. "The teaching program," biographer Celia Hawkesworth notes, "was devoted to producing dedicated supporters of the [Habsburg] Monarchy". Andrić disapproved. "All that came ... at secondary school and university," he wrote, "was rough, crude, automatic, without concern, faith, humanity, warmth or love."

Andrić experienced difficulty in his studies, finding mathematics particularly challenging, and had to repeat the sixth grade. For a time, he lost his scholarship due to poor grades. Hawkesworth attributes Andrić's initial lack of academic success at least partly to his alienation from most of his teachers. Nonetheless, he excelled in languages, particularly Latin, Greek and German. Although he initially showed substantial interest in natural sciences, he later began focusing on literature, likely under the influence of his two Croat instructors, writer and politician [Đuro Šurmin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Đuro_Šurmin "Đuro Šurmin") and poet [Tugomir Alaupović](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tugomir_Alaupović "Tugomir Alaupović"). Of all his teachers in Sarajevo, Andrić liked Alaupović best, and the two became lifelong friends.

Andrić felt he was destined to become a writer. He began writing in secondary school, but received little encouragement from his mother. He recalled that when he showed her one of his first works, she replied: "Did you write this? What did you do that for?" Andrić published his first two poems in 1911 in a journal called *Bosanska vila* (Bosnian Fairy), which promoted Serbo-Croat unity. At the time, he was still a secondary school student. Prior to [World War I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I "World War I"), his poems, essays, reviews, and translations appeared in journals such as *Vihor* (Whirlwind), *Savremenik* (The Contemporary), *Hrvatski pokret* (The Croatian Movement), and *Književne novine* (Literary News). One of Andrić's favorite literary forms was [lyrical reflective prose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prose_poetry "Prose poetry"), and many of his essays and shorter pieces are prose poems. The historian [Wayne S. Vucinich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wayne_S._Vucinich "Wayne S. Vucinich") describes Andrić's poetry from this period as "subjective and mostly melancholic". Andrić's translations of [August Strindberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/August_Strindberg "August Strindberg"), [Walt Whitman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walt_Whitman "Walt Whitman"), and a number of Slovene authors also appeared around this time.

### Student activism

> The whole of our society is snoring ungracefully; only the poets and revolutionaries are awake.

—  Andrić's view of pre-war Sarajevo.

In 1908, Austria-Hungary officially [annexed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bosnian_crisis "Bosnian crisis") Bosnia and Herzegovina, to the chagrin of South Slav nationalists like Andrić. In late 1911, Andrić was elected the first president of the Serbo-Croat Progressive Movement (Serbo-Croatian Latin: *Srpsko-Hrvatska Napredna Organizacija*; SHNO), a Sarajevo-based secret society that promoted unity and friendship between Serb and Croat youth and opposed the Austro-Hungarian occupation. Its members were vehemently criticized by both Serb and Croat nationalists, who dismissed them as "traitors to their nations". Unfazed, Andrić continued agitating against the Austro-Hungarians. On 28 February 1912, he spoke before a crowd of 100 student protesters at Sarajevo's railway station, urging them to continue their demonstrations. The Austro-Hungarian police later began harassing and prosecuting SHNO members. Ten were expelled from their schools or penalized in some other way, though Andrić himself escaped punishment. Andrić also joined the South Slav student movement known as [Young Bosnia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Young_Bosnia "Young Bosnia"), becoming one of its most prominent members.

In 1912, Andrić registered at the [University of Zagreb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Zagreb "University of Zagreb"), having received a scholarship from an educational foundation in Sarajevo. He enrolled in the department of mathematics and natural sciences because these were the only fields for which scholarships were offered, but was able to take some courses in Croatian literature. Andrić was well received by South Slav nationalists there, and regularly participated in on-campus demonstrations. This led to his being reprimanded by the university. In 1913, after completing two semesters in Zagreb, Andrić transferred to the University of Vienna, where he resumed his studies. While in Vienna, he joined South Slav students in promoting the cause of Yugoslav unity and worked closely with two Yugoslav student societies, the Serbian cultural society *Zora* (Dawn) and the Croatian student club *Zvonimir*, which shared his views on "integral Yugoslavism" (the eventual assimilation of all South Slav cultures into one).

Despite finding like-minded students in Vienna, the city's climate took a toll on Andrić's health. He contracted tuberculosis and became seriously ill, then asked to leave Vienna on medical grounds and continue his studies elsewhere, though Hawkesworth believes he may actually have been taking part in a protest of South Slav students that were boycotting German-speaking universities and transferring to Slavic ones. For a time, Andrić had considered transferring to a school in Russia but ultimately decided to complete his fourth semester at [Jagiellonian University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jagiellonian_University "Jagiellonian University") in [Kraków](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kraków "Kraków"). He transferred in early 1914. Andrić started his literary career as a poet. In 1914, he was one of the contributors to *Hrvatska mlada lirika* (Croatian Youth Lyrics) and continued to publish translations, poems and reviews.

## World War I

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4c/Ovcarevo_monastery.jpg/250px-Ovcarevo_monastery.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Ovcarevo_monastery.jpg)

Ovčarevo monastery, [Travnik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Travnik "Travnik")

On 28 June 1914, Andrić learned of the [assassination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assassination_of_Archduke_Franz_Ferdinand_of_Austria "Assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand of Austria") of Archduke [Franz Ferdinand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archduke_Franz_Ferdinand_of_Austria "Archduke Franz Ferdinand of Austria") in Sarajevo. The assassin was [Gavrilo Princip](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gavrilo_Princip "Gavrilo Princip"), a Young Bosnian and close friend of Andrić who had been one of the first to join the SHNO in 1911. Upon hearing the news, Andrić decided to leave Kraków and return to Bosnia. He travelled by train to Zagreb, and in mid-July, departed for the coastal city of [Split](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Split,_Croatia "Split, Croatia") with his friend, the poet and fellow South Slav nationalist [Vladimir Čerina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladimir_Čerina?action=edit&redlink=1 "Vladimir Čerina"). Andrić and Čerina spent the rest of July at the latter's summer home. As the month progressed, the two became increasingly uneasy about the [escalating political crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/July_Crisis "July Crisis") that followed the Archduke's assassination and eventually led to the outbreak of World War I. They then went to [Rijeka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rijeka "Rijeka"), where Čerina left Andrić without explanation, only saying he urgently needed to go to Italy. Several days later, Andrić learned that Čerina was being sought by the police.

By the time war was declared, Andrić had returned to Split feeling exhausted and ill. Given that most of his friends had already been arrested for nationalist activities, he was certain the same fate would befall him. Despite not being involved in the assassination plot, in late July or early August, Andrić was arrested for "anti-state activities", and imprisoned in Split. He was subsequently transferred to a prison in [Šibenik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Šibenik "Šibenik"), then to Rijeka and finally to [Maribor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maribor "Maribor"), where he arrived on 19 August. Plagued by tuberculosis, Andrić passed the time reading, talking to his cellmates and learning languages.

By the following year, the case against Andrić was dropped due to lack of evidence, and he was released from prison on 20 March 1915. The authorities exiled him to the village of [Ovčarevo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ovčarevo "Ovčarevo"), near Travnik. He arrived there on 22 March and was placed under the supervision of local [Franciscan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franciscan "Franciscan") friars. Andrić soon befriended the [friar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friar "Friar") Alojzije Perčinlić and began researching the history of Bosnia's Catholic and Orthodox Christian communities under Ottoman rule. Andrić lived in the parish headquarters, and the Franciscans gave him access to the monastery chronicles. In return, he assisted the parish priest and taught religious songs to pupils at the monastery school. Andrić's mother soon came to visit him and offered to serve as the parish priest's housekeeper. "Mother is very happy," Andrić wrote. "It has been three whole years since she saw me. And she can't grasp all that has happened to me in that time, nor the whole of my crazy, cursed existence. She cries, kisses me and laughs in turn. Like a mother."

Andrić was later transferred to a prison in [Zenica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zenica "Zenica"), where Perčinlić regularly visited him. The [Austro-Hungarian Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austro-Hungarian_Army "Austro-Hungarian Army") declared Andrić a political threat in March 1917 and exempted him from armed service. He was thus registered with a non-combat unit until February of the following year. On 2 July 1917, Emperor [Charles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_I_of_Austria "Charles I of Austria") declared a general amnesty for all of Austria-Hungary's political prisoners. His freedom of movement restored, Andrić visited Višegrad and reunited with several of his school friends. He remained in Višegrad until late July, when he was mobilized. Because of his poor health, Andrić was admitted to a Sarajevo hospital and thus avoided service. He was then transferred to the *Reservospital* in Zenica, where he received treatment for several months before continuing to Zagreb. There, Andrić again fell seriously ill and sought treatment at the Sisters of Mercy hospital, which had become a gathering place for dissidents and former political prisoners.

In January 1918, Andrić joined several South Slav nationalists in editing a short-lived pan-Yugoslav periodical called *Književni jug* (Literary South). Here and in other periodicals, Andrić published book reviews, plays, verse, and translations. Over the course of several months in early 1918, Andrić's health began to deteriorate, and his friends believed he was nearing death. However, he recovered and spent the spring of 1918 in [Krapina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krapina "Krapina") writing *Ex ponto*, a book of prose poetry that was published in July. It was his first book.

## Interwar period

The end of World War I saw the disintegration of Austria-Hungary, which was replaced by a newly established South Slav state, the [Kingdom of Serbs, Croats and Slovenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Yugoslavia "Kingdom of Yugoslavia") (renamed Yugoslavia in 1929). In late 1918, Andrić re-enrolled at the University of Zagreb and resumed his studies. By January 1919, he fell ill again and was back in the hospital. Fellow writer [Ivo Vojnović](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivo_Vojnović "Ivo Vojnović") became worried for his friend's life and appealed to Andrić's old schoolteacher Tugomir Alaupović (who had just been appointed the new kingdom's Minister of Religious Affairs) to use his connections and help Andrić pay for treatment abroad. In February, Andrić wrote Alaupović and asked for help finding a government job in Belgrade. Eventually, Andrić chose to seek treatment in Split, where he stayed for the following six months. During his time on the [Mediterranean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mediterranean "Mediterranean") coast, Andrić completed a second volume of prose poetry, titled *Nemiri*, which was published the following year. By the time Andrić left, he had almost fully recovered, and quipped that he was cured by the "air, sun and figs." Troubled by news that his uncle was seriously ill, Andrić left Split in August and went to him in Višegrad. He returned to Zagreb two weeks later.

### Early diplomatic career

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/8c/Ivo_Andric_Graz_2011-08-29.jpg/250px-Ivo_Andric_Graz_2011-08-29.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Ivo_Andric_Graz_2011-08-29.jpg)

A [bust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bust_(sculpture) "Bust (sculpture)") of Andrić in [Graz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graz "Graz"), Austria

In the immediate aftermath of the war, Andrić's tendency to identify with Serbdom became increasingly apparent. In a correspondence dated December 1918, Vojnović described the young writer as "a Catholic ... a Serb from Bosnia." By 1919, Andrić had acquired his undergraduate degree in South Slavic history and literature at the University of Zagreb. He was perennially impoverished, and earned a meagre sum through his writing and editorial work. By mid-1919, he realized that he would be unable to financially support himself and his aging mother, aunt and uncle for much longer, and his appeals to Alaupović for help securing a government job became more frequent. In September 1919, Alaupović offered him a secretarial position at the Ministry of Religion, which Andrić accepted.

In late October, Andrić left for [Belgrade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belgrade "Belgrade"). He became involved in the city's literary circles and soon acquired the distinction of being one of Belgrade's most popular young writers. Though the Belgrade press wrote positively of him, Andrić disliked being a public figure, and went into seclusion and distanced himself from his fellow writers. At the same time, he grew dissatisfied with his government job and wrote to Alaupović asking for a transfer to the [Ministry of Foreign Affairs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Foreign_Affairs_(Yugoslavia) "Ministry of Foreign Affairs (Yugoslavia)"). On 20 February, Andrić's request was granted and he was assigned to the Foreign Ministry's mission at the [Vatican](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_See "Holy See").

Andrić left Belgrade soon after, and reported for duty in late February. At this time, he published his first short story, *Put Alije Đerzeleza* (The Journey of [Alija Đerzelez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Đerzelez_Alija "Đerzelez Alija")). He complained that the consulate was understaffed and that he did not have enough time to write. All evidence suggests he had a strong distaste for the ceremony and pomp that accompanied his work in the diplomatic service, but according to Hawkesworth, he endured it with "dignified good grace". Around this time, he began writing in the [Ekavian dialect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shtokavian_dialect#Yat_reflexes "Shtokavian dialect") used in Serbia, and ceased writing in the Ijekavian dialect used in his native Bosnia. Andrić soon requested another assignment, and in November, he was transferred to [Bucharest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bucharest "Bucharest"). Once again, his health deteriorated. Nevertheless, Andrić found his consular duties there did not require much effort, so he focused on writing, contributed articles to a Romanian journal and even had time to visit his family in Bosnia. In 1922, Andrić requested another reassignment. He was transferred to the consulate in [Trieste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trieste "Trieste"), where he arrived on 9 December. The city's damp climate only caused Andrić's health to deteriorate further, and on his doctor's advice, he transferred to [Graz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graz "Graz") in January 1923. He arrived in the city on 23 January, and was appointed vice-consul. Andrić soon enrolled at the [University of Graz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Graz "University of Graz"), resumed his schooling and began working on his doctoral dissertation in [Slavic studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavic_studies "Slavic studies").

### Advancement

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e7/Graz_University_main-front.jpg/250px-Graz_University_main-front.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Graz_University_main-front.jpg)

Andrić completed his doctoral dissertation at the [University of Graz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Graz "University of Graz").

In August 1923, Andrić experienced an unexpected career setback. A law had been passed stipulating that all civil servants had to have a doctoral degree. As Andrić had not completed his dissertation, he was informed that his employment would be terminated. Andrić's well-connected friends intervened on his behalf and appealed to Foreign Minister [Momčilo Ninčić](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Momčilo_Ninčić "Momčilo Ninčić"), citing Andrić's diplomatic and linguistic abilities. In February 1924, the Foreign Ministry decided to retain Andrić as a day worker with the salary of a vice-consul. This gave him the opportunity to complete his [Ph.D](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Philosophy "Doctor of Philosophy"). Three months later, on 24 May, Andrić submitted his dissertation to a committee of examiners at the University of Graz, who gave it their approval. This allowed Andrić to take the examinations necessary for his Ph.D. to be confirmed. He passed both his exams, and on 13 July, received his Ph.D. The committee of examiners recommended that Andrić's dissertation be published. Andrić chose the title *Die Entwicklung des geistigen Lebens in Bosnien unter der Einwirkung der türkischen Herrschaft* (The Development of Spiritual Life in Bosnia Under the Influence of Turkish Rule). In it, he characterized the Ottoman occupation as [a yoke](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/under%20the%20yoke "wikt:under the yoke") that still loomed over Bosnia. "The effect of Turkish rule was absolutely negative," he wrote. "The Turks could bring no cultural content or sense of higher mission, even to those [South Slavs who accepted Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Slavic_Muslims?action=edit&redlink=1 "South Slavic Muslims")."

Several days after receiving his Ph.D., Andrić wrote the Foreign Minister asking to be reinstated and submitted a copy of his dissertation, university documents and a medical certification that deemed him to be in good health. In September, the Foreign Ministry granted his request. Andrić stayed in Graz until 31 October, when he was assigned to the Foreign Ministry's Belgrade headquarters. During the two years he was in Belgrade, Andrić spent much of his time writing. His first collection of short stories was published in 1924, and he received a prize from the Serbian Royal Academy (of which he became a full-fledged member in February 1926). In October 1926, he was assigned to the consulate in [Marseille](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marseille "Marseille") and again appointed vice-consul. On 9 December 1926, he was transferred to the Yugoslav embassy in Paris. Andrić's time in France was marked by increasing loneliness and isolation. His uncle had died in 1924, his mother the following year, and upon arriving in France, he was informed that his aunt had died as well. "Apart from official contacts," he wrote Alaupović, "I have no company whatever." Andrić spent much of his time in the Paris archives poring over the reports of the French consulate in Travnik between 1809 and 1814, material he would use in *Travnička hronika*, one of his future novels.

In April 1928, Andrić was posted to [Madrid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madrid "Madrid") as vice-consul. While there, he wrote essays on [Simón Bolívar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simón_Bolívar "Simón Bolívar") and [Francisco Goya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francisco_Goya "Francisco Goya"), and began work on the novel *Prokleta avlija* (The Damned Yard). In June 1929, he was named secretary of the Yugoslav [legation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legation "Legation") to Belgium and [Luxembourg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luxembourg "Luxembourg") in [Brussels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brussels "Brussels"). On 1 January 1930, he was sent to Switzerland as part of Yugoslavia's permanent delegation to the [League of Nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/League_of_Nations "League of Nations") in [Geneva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geneva "Geneva"), and was named deputy delegate the following year. In 1933, Andrić returned to Belgrade; two years later, he was named head of the political department of the Ministry of Foreign Affairs. On 5 November 1937, Andrić became assistant to [Milan Stojadinović](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milan_Stojadinović "Milan Stojadinović"), Yugoslavia's [Prime Minister](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_Minister_of_Yugoslavia "Prime Minister of Yugoslavia") and Foreign Minister. That year, France decorated him with the Order of the Grand Officer of the [Legion of Honour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legion_of_Honour "Legion of Honour").

## World War II

> Composed ... of priceless elements from unknown worlds, a man is born ... to become a piece of nameless soot, and as such, to vanish. And we do not know for whose glory he is born, nor for whose amusement he is destroyed.

—  An excerpt from Andrić's only journal entry of 1940.

Andrić was appointed Yugoslavia's ambassador to [Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi_Germany "Nazi Germany") in late March or early April 1939. This appointment, Hawkesworth writes, shows that he was highly regarded by his country's leadership. Yugoslavia's King [Alexander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_I_of_Yugoslavia "Alexander I of Yugoslavia") had been assassinated in [Marseille](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marseille "Marseille") in 1934. He was succeeded by his ten-year-old son [Peter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_II_of_Yugoslavia "Peter II of Yugoslavia"), and a regency council led by Peter's uncle [Paul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prince_Paul_of_Yugoslavia "Prince Paul of Yugoslavia") was established to rule in his place until he turned 18. Paul's government established closer economic and political ties with Germany. In March 1941, Yugoslavia signed the [Tripartite Pact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tripartite_Pact "Tripartite Pact"), pledging support for Germany and Italy. Though the negotiations had occurred behind Andrić's back, in his capacity as ambassador he was obliged to attend the document's signing in Berlin. Andrić had previously been instructed to delay agreeing to the Axis powers' demands for as long as possible. He was highly critical of the move, and on 17 March, wrote to the Ministry of Foreign Affairs asking to be relieved of his duties. Ten days later, a group of pro-Western [Royal Yugoslav Air Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Yugoslav_Air_Force "Royal Yugoslav Air Force") officers [overthrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yugoslav_coup_d'état "Yugoslav coup d'état") the regency and proclaimed Peter of age. This led to a breakdown in relations with Germany and prompted [Adolf Hitler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adolf_Hitler "Adolf Hitler") to order Yugoslavia's [invasion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invasion_of_Yugoslavia "Invasion of Yugoslavia"). Given these circumstances, Andrić's position was an extremely difficult one. Nevertheless, he used the little influence he had and attempted unsuccessfully to assist Polish prisoners following the German [invasion of Poland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invasion_of_Poland "Invasion of Poland") in September 1939.

Prior to their invasion of his country, the Germans had offered Andrić the opportunity to evacuate to neutral Switzerland. He declined on the basis that his staff would not be allowed to go with him. On 6 April 1941, the Germans and their allies invaded Yugoslavia. The country capitulated on 17 April and was subsequently partitioned between the Axis powers. In early June, Andrić and his staff were taken back to German-occupied Belgrade, where some were jailed. Andrić was retired from the diplomatic service, but refused to receive his pension or cooperate in any way with the puppet government that the Germans had installed in Serbia. He was spared jail, but the Germans kept him under close surveillance throughout the occupation. Because of his Croat heritage, they had offered him the chance to settle in Zagreb, then the capital of the [fascist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fascist "Fascist") [puppet state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puppet_state "Puppet state") known as the [Independent State of Croatia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_State_of_Croatia "Independent State of Croatia"), but he declined. Andrić spent the following three years in a friend's Belgrade apartment in conditions that some biographers liken to [house arrest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_arrest "House arrest"). In August 1941, the puppet authorities in German-occupied Serbia issued the [Appeal to the Serbian Nation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appeal_to_the_Serbian_Nation?action=edit&redlink=1 "Appeal to the Serbian Nation"), calling upon the country's inhabitants to abstain from the [communist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communism "Communism")-led rebellion against the Germans; Andrić refused to sign. He directed most of his energies towards writing, and during this time completed two of his best known novels, *[Na Drini ćuprija](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Bridge_on_the_Drina "The Bridge on the Drina")* (*The Bridge on the Drina*) and *Travnička hronika*.

In mid-1942, Andrić sent a message of sympathy to [Draža Mihailović](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Draža_Mihailović "Draža Mihailović"), the leader of the royalist [Chetniks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chetniks "Chetniks"), one of two resistance movements vying for power in Axis-occupied Yugoslavia, the other being [Josip Broz Tito](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josip_Broz_Tito "Josip Broz Tito")'s communist [Partisans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yugoslav_Partisans "Yugoslav Partisans"). In 1944, Andrić was forced to leave his friend's apartment during the [Allied](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allies_of_World_War_II "Allies of World War II") [bombing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allied_bombing_of_Yugoslavia_in_World_War_II "Allied bombing of Yugoslavia in World War II") of Belgrade and evacuate the city. As he joined a column of refugees, he became ashamed that he was fleeing by himself, in contrast to the masses of people accompanied by their children, spouses and infirm parents. "I looked myself up and down," he wrote, "and saw I was saving only myself and my overcoat." In the ensuing months, Andrić refused to leave the apartment, even during the heaviest bombing. That October, the [Red Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Army "Red Army") and the Partisans drove the Germans out of Belgrade, and Tito proclaimed himself Yugoslavia's ruler.

## Later life

### Political career and marriage

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/88/Belgrade_Book_Fair_3.jpg/250px-Belgrade_Book_Fair_3.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Belgrade_Book_Fair_3.jpg)

Andrić signing books at the [Belgrade Book Fair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belgrade_Book_Fair "Belgrade Book Fair")

Andrić initially had a precarious relationship with the communists because he had previously been an official in the royalist government. He returned to public life only once the Germans had been forced out of Belgrade. *Na Drini ćuprija* was published in March 1945. It was followed by *[Travnička hronika](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bosnian_Chronicle "Bosnian Chronicle")* that September and *Gospođica* that November. *Na Drini ćuprija* came to be regarded as Andrić's *[magnum opus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masterpiece "Masterpiece")* and was proclaimed a classic of Yugoslav literature by the communists. It chronicles the history of the Mehmed Paša Sokolović Bridge and the town of Višegrad from the bridge's construction in the 16th century until the outbreak of World War I. The second novel, *Travnička hronika*, follows a French diplomat in Bosnia during the [Napoleonic Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleonic_Wars "Napoleonic Wars"). The third, *Gospođica*, revolves around the life of a Sarajevan woman. In the post-war period, Andrić also published several short story collections, some travel memoirs, and a number of essays on writers such as [Vuk Karadžić](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vuk_Karadžić "Vuk Karadžić"), [Petar II Petrović-Njegoš](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petar_II_Petrović-Njegoš "Petar II Petrović-Njegoš"), and [Petar Kočić](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petar_Kočić "Petar Kočić").

In November 1946, Andrić was elected vice-president of the Society for the Cultural Cooperation of Yugoslavia with the Soviet Union. The same month, he was named president of the Yugoslav Writers' Union. The following year, he became a member of the People's Assembly of Bosnia and Herzegovina. In 1948, Andrić published a collection of short stories he had written during the war. His work came to influence writers such as [Branko Ćopić](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Branko_Ćopić "Branko Ćopić"), [Vladan Desnica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladan_Desnica "Vladan Desnica"), [Mihailo Lalić](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mihailo_Lalić "Mihailo Lalić") and [Meša Selimović](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meša_Selimović "Meša Selimović"). In April 1950, Andrić became a deputy in the [National Assembly of Yugoslavia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliament_of_Yugoslavia "Parliament of Yugoslavia"). He was decorated by the Presidium of the National Assembly for his services to the Yugoslav people in 1952. In 1953, his career as a parliamentary deputy came to an end. The following year, Andrić published the [novella](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Novella "Novella") *Prokleta avlija* (The Damned Yard), which tells of life in an Ottoman prison in [Istanbul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Istanbul "Istanbul"). That December, he was admitted into the [League of Communists of Yugoslavia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/League_of_Communists_of_Yugoslavia "League of Communists of Yugoslavia"), the country's ruling party. According to Hawkesworth, it is unlikely he joined the party out of ideological conviction, but rather to "serve his country as fully as possible".

On 27 September 1958, the 66-year-old Andrić married [Milica Babić](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milica_Babić "Milica Babić"), a costume designer at the [National Theatre of Serbia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Theatre_of_Serbia "National Theatre of Serbia") who was almost twenty years his junior. Earlier, he had announced it was "probably better" that a writer never marry. "He was perpetually persecuted by a kind of fear," a close friend recalled. "It seemed as though he had been born afraid, and that is why he married so late. He simply did not dare enter that area of life."

### Nobel Prize, international recognition and death

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d1/S._Kragujevic%2C_Andric_na_vest_o_N._nagradi_1961.JPG/250px-S._Kragujevic%2C_Andric_na_vest_o_N._nagradi_1961.JPG?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:S._Kragujevic,_Andric_na_vest_o_N._nagradi_1961.JPG)

Andrić with his wife Milica upon learning he had won the [Nobel Prize in Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Literature "Nobel Prize in Literature")

By the late 1950s, Andrić's works had been translated into a number of languages. In 1958 the [Association of Writers of Yugoslavia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Association_of_Writers_of_Yugoslavia "Association of Writers of Yugoslavia") nominated Andrić as its first ever candidate for the [Nobel Prize in Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Literature "Nobel Prize in Literature"). On 26 October 1961, he was awarded the Nobel Prize in Literature by the [Swedish Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swedish_Academy "Swedish Academy"). Documents released 50 years later revealed that the [Nobel Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Committee "Nobel Committee") had selected Andrić over writers such as [J.R.R. Tolkien](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J.R.R._Tolkien "J.R.R. Tolkien"), [Robert Frost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Frost "Robert Frost"), [John Steinbeck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Steinbeck "John Steinbeck") and [E.M. Forster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E.M._Forster "E.M. Forster"). The Committee cited "the epic force with which he has traced themes and depicted human destinies drawn from his country's history". Once the news was announced, Andrić's Belgrade apartment was swarmed by reporters, and he publicly thanked the Nobel Committee for selecting him as the winner of that year's prize. Andrić donated the entirety of his prize money, which amounted to some 30 million [dinars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yugoslav_dinar "Yugoslav dinar"), and prescribed that it be used to purchase library books in Bosnia and Herzegovina.

The Nobel Prize ensured Andrić received global recognition. The following March, he fell ill while on a trip to [Cairo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cairo "Cairo") and had to return to Belgrade for an operation. He was obliged to cancel all promotional events in Europe and North America, but his works continued to be reprinted and translated into numerous languages. Judging by letters he wrote at the time, Andrić felt burdened by the attention but did his best not to show it publicly. Upon receiving the Nobel Prize, the number of awards and honours bestowed upon him multiplied. He received the Order of the Republic in 1962, as well as 27 July Award of Bosnia-Herzegovina, the [AVNOJ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-Fascist_Council_for_the_National_Liberation_of_Yugoslavia "Anti-Fascist Council for the National Liberation of Yugoslavia") Award in 1967, and the [Order of the Hero of Socialist Labour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_the_Hero_of_Socialist_Labour "Order of the Hero of Socialist Labour") in 1972. In addition to being a member of the [Yugoslav](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Croatian_Academy_of_Sciences_and_Arts "Croatian Academy of Sciences and Arts") and [Serbian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serbian_Academy_of_Sciences_and_Arts "Serbian Academy of Sciences and Arts") academies of sciences and arts, he also became a correspondent of their [Bosnian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academy_of_Sciences_and_Arts_of_Bosnia_and_Herzegovina "Academy of Sciences and Arts of Bosnia and Herzegovina") and [Slovenian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slovenian_Academy_of_Sciences_and_Arts "Slovenian Academy of Sciences and Arts") counterparts, and received honorary doctorates from the universities of [Belgrade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Belgrade "University of Belgrade"), [Sarajevo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Sarajevo "University of Sarajevo") and Kraków.

Andrić's wife died on 16 March 1968. His health deteriorated steadily and he travelled little in his final years. He continued to write until 1974, when his health took another turn for the worse. In December 1974, he was admitted to a Belgrade hospital. He soon fell into a coma, and died in the [Military Medical Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_Medical_Academy_(Serbia) "Military Medical Academy (Serbia)") at 1:15 a.m. on 13 March 1975, aged 82. His remains were cremated, and on 24 April, the [urn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urn "Urn") containing his ashes was buried at the Alley of Distinguished Citizens in Belgrade's [New Cemetery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Cemetery,_Belgrade "New Cemetery, Belgrade"). The ceremony was attended by about 10,000 residents of Belgrade.

## Influences, style and themes

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/05/Cuprija_na_Drini_u_Visegradu.jpg/250px-Cuprija_na_Drini_u_Visegradu.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Cuprija_na_Drini_u_Visegradu.jpg)

[Mehmed Paša Sokolović Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mehmed_Paša_Sokolović_Bridge "Mehmed Paša Sokolović Bridge"), the eponymous bridge on the Drina

Andrić was an avid reader in his youth. The young Andrić's literary interests varied greatly, ranging from the Greek and Latin [Classics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classics "Classics") to the works of past and contemporary literary figures, including German and Austrian writers such as [Johann Wolfgang von Goethe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Wolfgang_von_Goethe "Johann Wolfgang von Goethe"), [Heinrich Heine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heinrich_Heine "Heinrich Heine"), [Friedrich Nietzsche](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Nietzsche "Friedrich Nietzsche"), [Franz Kafka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_Kafka "Franz Kafka"), [Rainer Maria Rilke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rainer_Maria_Rilke "Rainer Maria Rilke") and [Thomas Mann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Mann "Thomas Mann"), the French writers [Michel de Montaigne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michel_de_Montaigne "Michel de Montaigne"), [Blaise Pascal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blaise_Pascal "Blaise Pascal"), [Gustave Flaubert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gustave_Flaubert "Gustave Flaubert"), [Victor Hugo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victor_Hugo "Victor Hugo") and [Guy de Maupassant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guy_de_Maupassant "Guy de Maupassant"), and the British writers [Thomas Carlyle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Carlyle "Thomas Carlyle"), [Walter Scott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Scott "Walter Scott") and [Joseph Conrad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Conrad "Joseph Conrad"). Andrić also read the works of the Spanish writer [Miguel de Cervantes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miguel_de_Cervantes "Miguel de Cervantes"), the Italian poet and philosopher [Giacomo Leopardi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giacomo_Leopardi "Giacomo Leopardi"), the Russian writer [Nikolay Chernyshevsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nikolay_Chernyshevsky "Nikolay Chernyshevsky"), the Norwegian writer [Henrik Ibsen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henrik_Ibsen "Henrik Ibsen"), the American writers [Walt Whitman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walt_Whitman "Walt Whitman") and [Henry James](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_James "Henry James"), and the Czechoslovak philosopher [Tomáš Garrigue Masaryk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomáš_Garrigue_Masaryk "Tomáš Garrigue Masaryk"). Andrić was especially fond of Polish literature, and later stated that it had greatly influenced him. He held several Serb writers in high esteem, particularly Karadžić, Njegoš, Kočić and [Aleksa Šantić](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aleksa_Šantić "Aleksa Šantić"). Andrić also admired the Slovene poets [Fran Levstik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fran_Levstik "Fran Levstik"), [Josip Murn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josip_Murn "Josip Murn") and [Oton Župančič](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oton_Župančič "Oton Župančič"), and translated some of their works. Kafka appears to have had a significant influence on Andrić's prose, and his philosophical outlook was informed strongly by the works of Danish philosopher [Søren Kierkegaard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Søren_Kierkegaard "Søren Kierkegaard"). At one point in his youth, Andrić even took an interest in Chinese and Japanese literature.

Much of Andrić's work was inspired by the traditions and peculiarities of life in Bosnia, and examines the complexity and cultural contrasts of the region's Muslim, Serb and Croat inhabitants. His two best known novels, *Na Drini ćuprija* and *Travnička hronika*, subtly contrast Ottoman Bosnia's "oriental" propensities to the "Western atmosphere" first introduced by the French and later the Austro-Hungarians. His works contain many words of Turkish, Arabic or Persian origin that found their way into the languages of the South Slavs during Ottoman rule. According to Vucinich, Andrić uses these words to "express oriental nuances and subtleties that cannot be rendered as well in his own Serbo-Croatian".

In the opinion of literary historian [Nicholas Moravcevich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nikola_Moravčević "Nikola Moravčević"), Andrić's work "frequently betrays his profound sadness over the misery and waste inherent in the passing of time". *Na Drini ćuprija* remains his most famous novel and has received the most scholarly analysis of all his works. Most scholars have interpreted the eponymous bridge as a [metonym](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metonym "Metonym") for Yugoslavia, which was itself a bridge between East and West during the [Cold War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cold_War "Cold War"). In his Nobel acceptance speech, Andrić described the country as one "which, at break-neck speed and at the cost of great sacrifices and prodigious efforts, is trying in all fields, including the field of culture, to make up for those things of which it has been deprived by a singularly turbulent and hostile past." In Andrić's view, the seemingly conflicting positions of Yugoslavia's disparate ethnic groups could be overcome by knowing one's history. This, he surmised, would help future generations avoid the mistakes of the past, and was in line with his cyclical view of time. Andrić expressed hope that these differences could be bridged and "histories demystified".

## Legacy

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/08/Ivo_Andric_Beograd_spomenik.jpg/250px-Ivo_Andric_Beograd_spomenik.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Ivo_Andric_Beograd_spomenik.jpg)

Statue of Andrić adjacent to Belgrade's [Novi dvor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Novi_dvor "Novi dvor")

Shortly before his death, Andrić stated that he wished for all his possessions to be preserved as part of an endowment to be used for "general cultural and humanitarian purposes". In March 1976, an administrative committee decided that the purpose of the endowment would be to promote the study of Andrić's work, as well as art and literature in general. The Ivo Andrić Foundation has since organized a number of international conferences, made grants to foreign scholars studying the writer's works and offered financial aid to cover the publication costs of books relating to Andrić. An annual yearbook, titled *Sveske Zadužbine Ive Andrića* (The Journals of the Ivo Andrić Foundation), is published by the organization. Andrić's [will and testament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Will_and_testament "Will and testament") stipulated that an award be given annually to the author of each subsequent year's best collection of short stories, which led to the [Andrić Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrić_Prize "Andrić Prize")'s founding in 1975. The street that runs beside Belgrade's [New Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Novi_dvor "Novi dvor"), now the seat of the [President of Serbia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_of_Serbia "President of Serbia"), was posthumously named *Andrićev venac* (Andrić's Crescent) in his honour. It includes a life-sized statue of the writer. The flat in which Andrić spent his final years has been turned into a museum. Opened over a year after Andrić's death, it houses books, manuscripts, documents, photographs and personal belongings.

Andrić remains the only writer from the former Yugoslavia to have been awarded the Nobel Prize. Given his use of the Ekavian dialect, and the fact that most of his novels and short stories were written in Belgrade, his works have become associated almost exclusively with [Serbian literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serbian_literature "Serbian literature"). The Slavonic studies professor [Bojan Aleksov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bojan_Aleksov "Bojan Aleksov") characterizes Andrić as one of Serbian literature's two central pillars, the other being [Petar II Petrović-Njegoš](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petar_II_Petrović-Njegoš "Petar II Petrović-Njegoš"). "The plasticity of his narrative," Moravcevich writes, "the depth of his psychological insight, and the universality of his symbolism remain unsurpassed in all of Serbian literature." Due to his self-identification as a Serb, many in the Bosniak and Croat literary establishments have come to "reject or limit Andrić's association with their literatures". In Croatia, Andrić was not generally seen as part of Croatian literature even in former Yugoslavia, though that started to change in the Croatian literary circles around 1990. Following [Yugoslavia's disintegration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breakup_of_Yugoslavia "Breakup of Yugoslavia") in the early 1990s, Andrić's works were among those that the [Croatian Democratic Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Croatian_Democratic_Union "Croatian Democratic Union") activists had purged from some of the city libraries and schools in Croatia. [Dubravka Ugrešić](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dubravka_Ugrešić "Dubravka Ugrešić") criticized the then-President [Franjo Tuđman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franjo_Tuđman "Franjo Tuđman") for promoting the likes of [Ivan Aralica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivan_Aralica "Ivan Aralica") over Ivo Andrić, [Slobodan Šnajder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slobodan_Šnajder "Slobodan Šnajder"), [Slavenka Drakulić](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavenka_Drakulić "Slavenka Drakulić") and herself. The Croatian historian and politician [Ivo Banac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivo_Banac "Ivo Banac") characterized Andrić as a writer who "missed the Chetnik train by a very small margin". Though Andrić remains a controversial figure in Croatia, the Croatian literary establishment largely rehabilitated his works.

Bosniak scholars have objected to the ostensibly negative portrayal of Muslim characters in Andrić's works. During the 1950s, his most vocal Bosniak detractors accused him of being a [plagiarist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plagiarism "Plagiarism"), [homosexual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homosexuality "Homosexuality") and Serbian nationalist. Some went so far as to call for his Nobel Prize to be taken away. Most Bosniak criticism of his works appeared in the period immediately prior to the breakup of Yugoslavia and in the aftermath of the [Bosnian War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bosnian_War "Bosnian War"). In early 1992, a Bosniak nationalist in Višegrad destroyed a statue of Andrić with a sledgehammer. In 2009, Nezim Halilović, the [imam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imam "Imam") of Sarajevo's [King Fahd Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Fahd_Mosque_(Sarajevo) "King Fahd Mosque (Sarajevo)"), derided Andrić as a "Chetnik ideologue" during a sermon. In 2012, the filmmaker [Emir Kusturica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emir_Kusturica "Emir Kusturica") and Bosnian Serb [President](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_of_Republika_Srpska "President of Republika Srpska") [Milorad Dodik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milorad_Dodik "Milorad Dodik") unveiled another statue of Andrić in Višegrad, this time as part of the construction of an ethno-town called [Andrićgrad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrićgrad "Andrićgrad"), sponsored by Kusturica and the [Government of Republika Srpska](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_of_Republika_Srpska "Government of Republika Srpska"). Andrićgrad was officially inaugurated in June 2014, on the occasion of the 100th anniversary of the assassination of Franz Ferdinand.

Starting in the early 1990s, Andrić's likeness began to appear on the banknotes of the Yugoslav dinar. His likeness is also featured on 1[KM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bosnia_and_Herzegovina_convertible_mark "Bosnia and Herzegovina convertible mark") banknotes that were issued in [Republika Srpska](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republika_Srpska "Republika Srpska") and 200KM banknotes that were issued at the national level in Bosnia and Herzegovina, as well as 20 [dinar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serbian_dinar "Serbian dinar") coins minted by [Serbia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serbia "Serbia") in 2011 to mark the fiftieth anniversary of his being awarded the Nobel Prize in Literature.

## Notes

1. [↑](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivo_Andrić#cite_ref-5) Though of Croat origin, Andrić came to identify as a Serb upon moving to Belgrade. Above all, he is renowned for his contributions to Serbian literature. As a youth, he wrote in his native [Ijekavian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shtokavian_dialect#Yat_reflexes "Shtokavian dialect") dialect, but switched to Serbia's [Ekavian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ekavian "Ekavian") dialect while living in the Yugoslav capital. The Nobel Committee lists him as a Yugoslav and identifies the language he used as Serbo-Croatian.
2. [↑](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivo_Andrić#cite_ref-7) Ivo is the [hypocoristic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypocorism "Hypocorism") form of Andrić's birth name, Ivan. The latter was used on his birth and marriage certificates, but all other documents read "Ivo".
3. [↑](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivo_Andrić#cite_ref-22) The full name of the group was The Croat-Serb or Serb-Croat or Yugoslav Progressive Youth Movement.
4. [↑](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivo_Andrić#cite_ref-29) On one occasion, Princip asked Andrić to examine a poem he had written. Later, when Andrić inquired about the poem, Princip told him that he had destroyed it.
5. [↑](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivo_Andrić#cite_ref-31) Disagreement exists as to the exact date. Hawkesworth writes that Andrić was arrested on 29 July, while Vucinich gives the date as 4 August.
6. [↑](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivo_Andrić#cite_ref-37) "Unrest" is Vucinich's translation of the title. Hawkesworth translates it as "Anxieties".
7. [↑](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivo_Andrić#cite_ref-51) Hawkesworth and Vucinich translate *Travnička hronika* as "Bosnian Story".
8. [↑](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivo_Andrić#cite_ref-54) Hawkesworth writes that Andrić was appointed on 1 April. Vucinich gives the date as 28 March.
9. [↑](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivo_Andrić#cite_ref-65) In early 1944, there were rumours that Andrić and several other prominent writers from Serbia were planning to join the Chetniks. This may have been Chetnik propaganda to counteract the news that a number of intellectuals were swearing allegiance to the Partisans.
10. [↑](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivo_Andrić#cite_ref-67) Andrić was perturbed by a billboard that the Partisans had put up in [Terazije](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terazije "Terazije") Square, a photograph of the signing of the Tripartite Pact with his face clearly visible. The billboard was part of a propaganda campaign against the royalists and Andrić perceived it as an indictment of his actions while ambassador to Germany. In a subsequent conversation with senior communist official [Milovan Đilas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milovan_Djilas "Milovan Djilas"), he requested that the billboard be removed, and Đilas obliged.

12. [↑](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivo_Andrić#cite_ref-97) An ethno-town or ethno-village is a tourist attraction that is designed to resemble a traditional settlement inhabited by a particular group of people.
