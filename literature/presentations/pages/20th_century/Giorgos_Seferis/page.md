**Giorgos** or **George Seferis** ([/səˈfɛrɪs/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English"); [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language "Greek language"): Γιώργος Σεφέρης [[ˈʝorɣos seˈferis]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Greek "Help:IPA/Greek")), the pen name of **Georgios Seferiadis** (Γεώργιος Σεφεριάδης; March 13  [[O.S.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Style_and_New_Style_dates "Old Style and New Style dates") February 28] 1900 – September 20, 1971), was a Greek [poet and diplomat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poet-diplomat "Poet-diplomat"). He was one of the most important [Greek poets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_Greek_literature "Modern Greek literature") of the 20th century, and a [Nobel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize "Nobel Prize") laureate.

He was a career diplomat in the Greek Foreign Service, culminating in his appointment as Ambassador to the UK, a post which he held from 1957 to 1962.

## Biography

Seferis was born in [Smyrna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smyrna "Smyrna") in [Asia Minor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asia_Minor "Asia Minor"), in the [Aidin Vilayet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aidin_Vilayet "Aidin Vilayet") of the [Ottoman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Empire "Ottoman Empire") (now [İzmir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/İzmir "İzmir"), Turkey). His father, Stelios Seferiadis, was a [lawyer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawyer "Lawyer"), and later a professor at the [University of Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Athens "University of Athens"), as well as a poet and translator in his own right. He was also a staunch [Venizelist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venizelism "Venizelism") and a supporter of the [demotic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_Greek "Modern Greek") [Greek language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language "Greek language") over the formal, official language (*[katharevousa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Katharevousa "Katharevousa")*). Both of these attitudes influenced his son. In 1914, the family moved to [Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athens "Athens"), where Seferis completed his secondary school education. He continued his studies in Paris from 1918 to 1925, studying law at the [Sorbonne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Paris "University of Paris"). While he was there, in September 1922, Smyrna/Izmir was taken by the Turkish Army after a [two-year Greek military campaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asia_Minor_Campaign "Asia Minor Campaign") on [Anatolian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatolia "Anatolia") soil. Many Greeks, including Seferis's family, [fled](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_refugees "Greek refugees") from Anatolia. Seferis would not visit Smyrna again until 1950; the sense of being an exile from his childhood home would inform much of Seferis's poetry, showing itself particularly in his interest in the story of [Odysseus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odysseus "Odysseus"). Seferis was also greatly influenced by [Constantine P. Cavafy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantine_P._Cavafy "Constantine P. Cavafy"), [T. S. Eliot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T._S._Eliot "T. S. Eliot") and [Ezra Pound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ezra_Pound "Ezra Pound").

He returned to Athens in 1925 and was admitted to the [Greek Ministry of Foreign Affairs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_Ministry_of_Foreign_Affairs "Greek Ministry of Foreign Affairs") in the following year. This was the beginning of a long and successful diplomatic career, during which he held posts in the United Kingdom (1931–1934) and [Albania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albania "Albania") (1936–1938). He married Maria Zannou ('Maro') on April 10, 1941, on the eve of the [German invasion of Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_invasion_of_Greece "German invasion of Greece"). During the Second World War, Seferis accompanied the [Greek government-in-exile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_government-in-exile "Greek government-in-exile") to [Crete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crete "Crete"), Egypt, South Africa, and [Italy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italy "Italy"), and returned to liberated Athens in 1944. He continued to serve in the Ministry of Foreign Affairs and held diplomatic posts in [Ankara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ankara "Ankara"), Turkey (1948–1950) and London (1951–1953). He was appointed minister to [Lebanon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebanon "Lebanon"), Syria, [Jordan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jordan "Jordan"), and [Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraq "Iraq") (1953–1956), and was Ambassador to the United Kingdom from 1957 to 1961, the last post before his retirement in Athens. Seferis received many honours and prizes, among them honorary doctoral degrees from the universities of Cambridge (1960), Oxford (1964), Thessaloniki (1964), and Princeton (1965).

### Cyprus

Seferis first visited [Cyprus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyprus "Cyprus") in November 1953. He immediately fell in love with the island, partly because of its resemblance, in its landscape, the mixture of populations, and in its traditions, to his childhood summer home in Skala (Urla). His book of poems *Imerologio Katastromatos III* was inspired by the island, and mostly written there, bringing to an end a period of six or seven years in which Seferis had not produced any poetry. Its original title *Cyprus, where it was ordained for me...* (a quotation from [Euripides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euripides "Euripides")' *[Helen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helen_(play) "Helen (play)")* in which [Teucer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teucer "Teucer") states that [Apollo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo "Apollo") has decreed that Cyprus shall be his home) made clear the optimistic sense of homecoming Seferis felt on discovering the island. Seferis changed the title in the 1959 edition of his poems.

Politically, Cyprus was entangled in the dispute between the UK, Greece and [Turkey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkey "Turkey") over its international status. Over the next few years, Seferis made use of his position in the diplomatic service to strive towards a resolution of the [Cyprus dispute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyprus_dispute "Cyprus dispute"), investing a great deal of personal effort and emotion. This was one of the few areas in his life in which he allowed the personal and the political to mix. Seferis described his political principles as "[liberal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberalism "Liberalism") and democratic [or republican]."

### Nobel Prize

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b1/Giorgos_Seferis_1963.jpg/250px-Giorgos_Seferis_1963.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Giorgos_Seferis_1963.jpg)

George Seferis in 1963

In 1963, Seferis was awarded the [Nobel Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize "Nobel Prize") for Literature "for his eminent lyrical writing, inspired by a deep feeling for the Hellenic world of culture." Seferis was nominated in total four times for the Nobel Prize. [Romilly Jenkins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romilly_Jenkins "Romilly Jenkins") nominated him in 1955, [T.S. Eliot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T._S._Eliot "T. S. Eliot") nominated him in 1961, [Eyvind Johnson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eyvind_Johnson "Eyvind Johnson") and [C. A. Trypanis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C._A._Trypanis "C. A. Trypanis") both nominated in 1962, and it was the 1963 nomination again by Eyvind Johnson that won him the prize. Seferis was the first Greek to receive the prize (followed by [Odysseas Elytis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odysseas_Elytis "Odysseas Elytis"), who became a Nobel laureate in 1979). But in his acceptance speech, Seferis chose rather to emphasise his own humanist philosophy, concluding: "When on his way to Thebes Oedipus encountered the Sphinx, his answer to its riddle was: 'Man'. That simple word destroyed the monster. We have many monsters to destroy. Let us think of the answer of Oedipus." While Seferis has sometimes been considered a nationalist poet, his 'Hellenism' had more to do with his identifying a unifying strand of [humanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humanism "Humanism") in the continuity of [Greek culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture_of_Greece "Culture of Greece") and [literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_literature "Greek literature"). The other five finalists for the prize that year were [W. H. Auden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W._H._Auden "W. H. Auden"), [Pablo Neruda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pablo_Neruda "Pablo Neruda") (1971 winner), [Samuel Beckett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Beckett "Samuel Beckett") (1969 winner), [Yukio Mishima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yukio_Mishima "Yukio Mishima") and [Aksel Sandemose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aksel_Sandemose "Aksel Sandemose").

### Later life

In 1967, the repressive nationalist, right-wing [Regime of the Colonels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_military_junta_of_1967–1974 "Greek military junta of 1967–1974") took power in Greece after a coup d'état. After two years marked by widespread censorship, political detentions and torture, Seferis took a stand against the regime. On March 28, 1969, he made a statement on the BBC World Service, with copies simultaneously distributed to every newspaper in Athens. In authoritative and absolute terms, he stated "This anomaly must end".

Seferis did not live to see the [end of the junta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metapolitefsi "Metapolitefsi") in 1974 as a direct result of the [Turkish invasion of Cyprus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkish_invasion_of_Cyprus "Turkish invasion of Cyprus"), which had itself been prompted by the junta's attempt to overthrow Cyprus's president, Archbishop [Makarios III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Makarios_III "Makarios III"). He died in Athens on September 20, 1971. The cause of death was reported to be pneumonia, aggravated by a stroke he had suffered after undergoing surgery for a bleeding ulcer about two months earlier.

At his funeral, huge crowds followed his coffin through the streets of Athens, singing [Mikis Theodorakis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikis_Theodorakis "Mikis Theodorakis")' setting of Seferis's poem ['Denial'](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denial_(poem) "Denial (poem)") (then banned); he had become a popular hero for his resistance to the regime. He is buried at the [First Cemetery of Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Cemetery_of_Athens "First Cemetery of Athens").

## Legacy

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/9a/Blue_plaque_George_Seferis%2C_Sloane_Avenue.jpg/250px-Blue_plaque_George_Seferis%2C_Sloane_Avenue.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Blue_plaque_George_Seferis,_Sloane_Avenue.jpg)

[Blue plaque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_plaque "Blue plaque") at 7 [Sloane Avenue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sloane_Avenue "Sloane Avenue"), London

His house at [Pangrati](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pangrati "Pangrati") district of central [Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athens "Athens"), just next to the [Panathinaiko Stadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panathinaiko_Stadium "Panathinaiko Stadium") of Athens, still stands today at Agras Street.

There are commemorative [blue plaques](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_plaque "Blue plaque") on two of his London homes – 51 Upper Brook Street (residence of the Greek Ambassador), and at 7 [Sloane Avenue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sloane_Avenue "Sloane Avenue").

In 1999, there was a dispute over the naming of a street in İzmir *Yorgos Seferis Sokagi* due to continuing ill-feeling over the [Greco-Turkish War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greco-Turkish_War_(1919–1922) "Greco-Turkish War (1919–1922)") in the early 1920s.

In 2004, the band Sigmatropic released "16 Haiku & Other Stories," an album dedicated to and lyrically derived from Seferis's work. Vocalists included recording artists [Laetitia Sadier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laetitia_Sadier "Laetitia Sadier"), [Alejandro Escovedo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alejandro_Escovedo "Alejandro Escovedo"), [Cat Power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cat_Power "Cat Power"), and [Robert Wyatt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Wyatt "Robert Wyatt"). Seferis's famous [stanza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanza "Stanza") from *Mythistorema* was featured in the Opening Ceremony of the [2004 Athens Olympic Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2004_Athens_Olympic_Games "2004 Athens Olympic Games"):

*I woke with this marble head in my hands;*
*It exhausts my elbows and I don't know where to put it down.*
*It was falling into the dream as I was coming out of the dream.*
*So our life became one and it will be very difficult for it to separate again.*

[Stephen King](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_King "Stephen King") quotes several of Seferis's poems as epigraphs in his 1975 novel *['Salem's Lot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/'Salem's_Lot "'Salem's Lot")*.

The composer [Richard Causton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Causton_(composer) "Richard Causton (composer)") wrote a piece for solo flute, *Sleep*, which is inspired by *Mythistorema*.
