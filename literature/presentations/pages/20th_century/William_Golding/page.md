**Sir William Gerald Golding** (19 September 1911 – 19 June 1993) was a British novelist, playwright, and poet. Best known for his [debut novel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Debut_novel "Debut novel") *[Lord of the Flies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_of_the_Flies "Lord of the Flies")* (1954), Golding published another 12 volumes of fiction in his lifetime. In 1980, Golding was awarded the [Booker Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Booker_Prize "Booker Prize") for *[Rites of Passage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rites_of_Passage_(novel) "Rites of Passage (novel)")*, the first novel in what became his sea trilogy, *[To the Ends of the Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/To_the_Ends_of_the_Earth "To the Ends of the Earth")*. Golding was awarded the [1983 Nobel Prize in Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1983_Nobel_Prize_in_Literature "1983 Nobel Prize in Literature").

As a result of his contributions to literature, Golding was [knighted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knight_Bachelor "Knight Bachelor") in 1988. He was also a fellow of the [Royal Society of Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society_of_Literature "Royal Society of Literature"). In 2008, *[The Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Times "The Times")* ranked Golding third on its list of "The 50 greatest [British writers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_literature "British literature") since 1945".

## Early life and education

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/85/William_Golding%27s_Plaque_at_Bishop_Wordsworth%27s_School.jpg/250px-William_Golding%27s_Plaque_at_Bishop_Wordsworth%27s_School.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:William_Golding's_Plaque_at_Bishop_Wordsworth's_School.jpg)

Plaque at [Bishop Wordsworth's School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bishop_Wordsworth's_School "Bishop Wordsworth's School"), [Salisbury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salisbury "Salisbury").

Son of Alec Golding, a science master at [Marlborough Grammar School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marlborough_Grammar_School "Marlborough Grammar School") (1905 to retirement), and Mildred ([née](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_name#Maiden_and_married_names "Birth name") Curnoe), William Golding was born on 19 September 1911 at his maternal grandmother's house, 47 Mount Wise, [Newquay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newquay "Newquay"), [Cornwall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornwall "Cornwall"). The house was known as *Karenza*, the [Cornish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornish_language "Cornish language") word for love, and he spent many childhood holidays there. The Golding family lived at 29, The Green, [Marlborough, Wiltshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marlborough,_Wiltshire "Marlborough, Wiltshire"), and Golding and his elder brother Joseph attended the school where their father taught. Golding's mother was a campaigner for female suffrage. She was [Cornish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornish_people "Cornish people"), and was considered by her son "a superstitious [Celt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celts "Celts")" who used to tell him old Cornish ghost stories from her own childhood.

In 1930, Golding went to [Brasenose College, Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brasenose_College,_Oxford "Brasenose College, Oxford"), where he read [natural sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_sciences "Natural sciences") for two years before transferring to English for his final two years. Golding's original [tutor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tutorial_system "Tutorial system") was the chemist [Thomas Taylor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Taylor_(chemist) "Thomas Taylor (chemist)"). In a private journal and in a memoir for his wife, Golding admitted that, as a teenager during a vacation, he tried to rape a teenage girl with whom he had previously taken piano lessons, perceiving her to have "wanted heavy sex".

Golding took his B.A. degree with second class honours during the summer of 1934, and later that year a book of his *[Poems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poems_(Golding_collection) "Poems (Golding collection)")* was published by [Macmillan & Co](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macmillan_Publishers "Macmillan Publishers"), with the help of his Oxford friend, the [anthroposophist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthroposophy "Anthroposophy") Adam Bittleston.

In 1935, Golding took a job teaching English at [Michael Hall School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Hall_School "Michael Hall School"), a [Steiner-Waldorf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steiner-Waldorf "Steiner-Waldorf") school then in Streatham, South London, staying there for two years. After a year in Oxford studying for a Diploma of Education, he became a schoolmaster, teaching English and music at [Maidstone Grammar School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maidstone_Grammar_School "Maidstone Grammar School") from 1938 to 1940, before moving to [Bishop Wordsworth's School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bishop_Wordsworth's_School "Bishop Wordsworth's School"), [Salisbury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salisbury "Salisbury"), in April 1940. There, Golding taught English, philosophy, Greek, and drama until joining the navy on 18 December 1940, reporting for duty at [HMS Raleigh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HMS_Raleigh_(shore_establishment) "HMS Raleigh (shore establishment)"). He returned in 1945 and taught the same subjects until 1961.

Golding kept a personal journal for over 22 years from 1971 until the night before his death; it contained approximately 2.4 million words in total. The journal was initially used by Golding to record his dreams, but over time it began to function as a record of his life. The journals contained insights including retrospective thoughts about Golding's novels and memories from his past. At one point, Golding described setting his students up into two groups to fight each other – an experience he drew on when writing *Lord of the Flies*. [John Carey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Carey_(critic) "John Carey (critic)"), an emeritus professor of English literature at Oxford University, was eventually given 'unprecedented access to Golding's unpublished papers and journals by the Golding estate'. Although Golding had not written the journals specifically so that a biography could be written about him, Carey published *William Golding: The Man Who Wrote Lord of the Flies* in 2009.

### Military service

During [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II"), Golding joined the [Royal Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Navy "Royal Navy") in 1940. He served on a destroyer which was briefly involved in the pursuit and sinking of the German battleship [*Bismarck*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_battleship_Bismarck "German battleship Bismarck"). Additionally, Golding participated in the [invasion of Normandy on D-Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normandy_landings "Normandy landings"), commanding a [landing craft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landing_Craft_Tank_(Rocket) "Landing Craft Tank (Rocket)") that fired salvoes of [rockets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RP-3#tanks-encyclopedia "RP-3") onto the beaches. He was also [in action at Walcheren](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Infatuate "Operation Infatuate") in October and November 1944, during which time 10 out of 27 assault craft that went into the attack were sunk. Golding rose to the rank of lieutenant.

### Crisis

Golding had a [troubled relationship with alcohol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alcohol_abuse "Alcohol abuse"); his daughter Judy Carver notes that her father was "always very open, if rueful, about problems with drink". Golding suggested that his self-described "crisis", of which alcoholism played a major part, had plagued him his entire life.

John Carey mentions several instances of Golding's [binge drinking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binge_drinking "Binge drinking") in his biography, including Golding's experiences in 1963. Whilst on holiday in [Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greece "Greece") when he was meant to have been finishing his novel *[The Spire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Spire "The Spire")*, Golding would, after working on his writing in the morning, go to his preferred "[Kapheneion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kafenio "Kafenio")" to drink at midday. By the evening, he would move on to [ouzo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ouzo "Ouzo") and [brandy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brandy "Brandy"), and developed a reputation locally for "provoking explosions".

Unfortunately, the eventual publication of *The Spire* the following year did not help Golding's developing struggle with alcohol; it had precisely the opposite effect, with the novel's scathingly negative reviews in a BBC radio broadcast affecting him severely.

Following the publication of *[The Pyramid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Pyramid_(Golding_novel) "The Pyramid (Golding novel)")* in 1967, Golding experienced a severe [writer's block](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Writer's_block "Writer's block"), the result of a myriad of crises: family anxieties, [insomnia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insomnia "Insomnia"), and a general sense of dejection. Golding eventually became unable to deal with what he perceived to be the intense reality of his life without first drinking copious amounts of alcohol. [Tim Kendall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tim_Kendall "Tim Kendall") suggests that these experiences manifest in Golding's writing as the character Wilf in *[The Paper Men](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Paper_Men "The Paper Men")*; "an ageing novelist whose alcohol-sodden journeys across Europe are bankrolled by the continuing success of his first book". By the late 1960s, Golding was hopelessly addicted to alcohol – which he referred to as "the old, old [anodyne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anodyne "Anodyne")".

Golding's first steps towards recovery came from his study of [Carl Jung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Jung "Carl Jung")'s writings, and in what he called "an admission of discipleship," Golding travelled to [Switzerland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Switzerland "Switzerland") in 1971 to see Jung's landscapes for himself. That same year, Golding started keeping a journal in which he recorded and interpreted his dreams; the last entry is from the day before he died, in 1993, and the work came to be thousands of pages long by this time.

The crisis did inevitably affect Golding's output, and his next novel, *[Darkness Visible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darkness_Visible_(novel) "Darkness Visible (novel)")*, would be published twelve years after *The Pyramid*; a far cry from the prolific author who had produced six novels in thirteen years since the start of his career. Despite this, the extent of Golding's recovery is evident from the fact that this was only the first of six further novels that Golding completed before his death.

## Career

### Writing success

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/be/Golding%2C_Lundkvist_och_Sartre_i_Leningrad_1963.jpg/250px-Golding%2C_Lundkvist_och_Sartre_i_Leningrad_1963.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Golding,_Lundkvist_och_Sartre_i_Leningrad_1963.jpg)

Golding (left), [Artur Lundkvist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artur_Lundkvist "Artur Lundkvist"), and [Jean-Paul Sartre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Paul_Sartre "Jean-Paul Sartre") at a writers' congress in [Leningrad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Petersburg "Saint Petersburg"), [USSR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USSR "USSR"), 1963

In *William Golding: A Critical Study* (2008), George states that, "Golding experienced two things that he counts the greatest influences on his writing—first, the war and his service in the navy and second, his learning ancient Greek." While still a teacher at [Bishop Wordsworth's School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bishop_Wordsworth's_School "Bishop Wordsworth's School"), in 1951 Golding began writing a manuscript of the novel initially titled *Strangers from Within*.

In September 1953, after rejections from seven other publishers, Golding sent a manuscript to [Faber and Faber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faber_and_Faber "Faber and Faber") and was initially rejected by their reader, Jan Perkins, who labelled it as "Rubbish & dull. Pointless". However, Golding's book was championed by Charles Monteith, a new editor at the firm. Monteith asked for some changes to the text, and the novel was published in September 1954 as *[Lord of the Flies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_of_the_Flies "Lord of the Flies")*.

After moving in 1958 from [Salisbury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salisbury "Salisbury") to nearby [Bowerchalke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bowerchalke "Bowerchalke"), Golding met his fellow villager and walking companion [James Lovelock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Lovelock "James Lovelock"). The two discussed Lovelock's [hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaia_Hypothesis "Gaia Hypothesis"), that the living matter of the planet Earth functions like a single organism, and Golding suggested naming this hypothesis after [Gaia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaia_(mythology) "Gaia (mythology)"), the personification of the Earth in Greek mythology, and mother of the Titans. Literary scholar Theo Mantion has argued that Golding's involvement with the Gaia hypothesis extended beyond suggesting its name. According to Mantion, Golding became one of the hypothesis's earliest literary advocates, further developing and defending its ecological implications in essays such as "Gaia Lives, OK?" (1976) and "The Earth's Revenge" (1990). His publishing success made it possible for Golding to resign his teaching post at Bishop Wordsworth's School in 1961, and Golding spent that academic year in the United States as writer-in-residence at [Hollins College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hollins_University "Hollins University") (now Hollins University), near [Roanoke, Virginia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roanoke,_Virginia "Roanoke, Virginia").

Golding won the [James Tait Black Memorial Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Tait_Black_Memorial_Prize "James Tait Black Memorial Prize") for *[Darkness Visible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darkness_Visible_(Golding) "Darkness Visible (Golding)")* in 1979, and the [Booker Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Booker_Prize "Booker Prize") for *[Rites of Passage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rites_of_Passage_(novel) "Rites of Passage (novel)")* in 1980. Having been appointed [Commander of the Order of the British Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commander_of_the_Order_of_the_British_Empire "Commander of the Order of the British Empire") (CBE) in the [1966 New Year Honours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1966_New_Year_Honours "1966 New Year Honours"), Golding was appointed a [Knight Bachelor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knight_Bachelor "Knight Bachelor") in the [1988 Birthday Honours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1988_Birthday_Honours "1988 Birthday Honours"). In September 1993, only a few months after his unexpected death, the First International William Golding Conference was held in France.

### Nobel Prize in Literature

In 1983, Golding was awarded the [Nobel Prize for Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_for_Literature "Nobel Prize for Literature") "for his novels which, with the perspicuity of realistic narrative art and the diversity and universality of myth, illuminate the human condition in the world of today". It was, according to the *[Oxford Dictionary of National Biography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford_Dictionary_of_National_Biography "Oxford Dictionary of National Biography")*, "an unexpected and even contentious choice".

### Fiction

Golding's first novel, *[Lord of the Flies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_of_the_Flies "Lord of the Flies")* (1954; film, 1963 and 1990; play, adapted by [Nigel Williams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nigel_Williams_(author) "Nigel Williams (author)"), 1995), describes a group of boys stranded on a tropical island descending into a lawless and increasingly wild existence before being rescued. *[The Inheritors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Inheritors_(William_Golding) "The Inheritors (William Golding)")* (1955) depicts a tribe of gentle Neanderthals encountering modern humans, who by comparison are deceitful and violent. His 1956 novel *[Pincher Martin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pincher_Martin "Pincher Martin")* records the thoughts of a drowning sailor. *[Free Fall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_Fall_(Golding_novel) "Free Fall (Golding novel)")* (1959) explores the question of freedom of choice. The novel's narrator, a World War Two soldier in a German POW Camp, endures interrogation and solitary confinement. After these events and while recollecting the experiences, he looks back over the choices he has made, trying to trace precisely where he lost the freedom to make his own decisions. *[The Spire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Spire "The Spire")* (1964) follows the construction (and near collapse) of an impossibly large spire on the top of a medieval cathedral (generally assumed to be [Salisbury Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salisbury_Cathedral "Salisbury Cathedral")).

Golding's 1967 novel, *[The Pyramid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Pyramid_(Golding) "The Pyramid (Golding)")*, consists of three linked stories with a shared setting in a small English town based partly on Marlborough where Golding grew up. *[The Scorpion God](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Scorpion_God "The Scorpion God")* (1971) contains three novellas, the first set in an ancient Egyptian court ("The Scorpion God"); the second describing a prehistoric African hunter-gatherer group ("Clonk, Clonk"); and the third in the court of a Roman emperor ("Envoy Extraordinary"). The last of these, originally published in 1956, was reworked by Golding into a play, *The Brass Butterfly*, in 1958. From 1971 to 1979, Golding published no novels. After this period he published *[Darkness Visible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darkness_Visible_(Golding) "Darkness Visible (Golding)")* (1979): a story involving terrorism, paedophilia, and a mysterious figure who survives a fire in [the Blitz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Blitz "The Blitz") and appears to have supernatural powers.

In 1980, Golding published *[Rites of Passage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/To_the_Ends_of_the_Earth#Rites_of_Passage "To the Ends of the Earth")*, the first of his novels about a voyage to Australia in the early nineteenth century. The novel won the [Booker Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Booker_Prize "Booker Prize") in 1980 and Golding followed this success with *[Close Quarters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/To_the_Ends_of_the_Earth#Close_Quarters "To the Ends of the Earth")* (1987) and *[Fire Down Below](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/To_the_Ends_of_the_Earth#Fire_Down_Below "To the Ends of the Earth")* (1989) to complete his 'sea trilogy', later published as one volume entitled *[To the Ends of the Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/To_the_Ends_of_the_Earth "To the Ends of the Earth")*. In 1984, he published *[The Paper Men](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Paper_Men "The Paper Men")*: an account of the struggles between a novelist and his would-be biographer.

## Personal life

Golding was engaged to Mollie Evans, a woman from Marlborough, who was well liked by both of his parents. However, he broke off the engagement and married Ann Brookfield, an analytical chemist, on 30 September 1939. They had two children: David (born September 1940) and Judith (born July 1945). The novelist, screenwriter and producer Simon William Golding was a distant cousin of William Golding.

## Death

In 1985, Golding and his wife moved to a house called [Tullimaar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tullimaar_House "Tullimaar House") in [Perranarworthal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perranarworthal "Perranarworthal"), near [Truro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Truro "Truro"), Cornwall. He died of heart failure eight years later on 19 June 1993, at age 81. Golding's body was buried in the parish churchyard of [Bowerchalke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bowerchalke "Bowerchalke") near his former home and the [Wiltshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wiltshire "Wiltshire") county border with [Hampshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hampshire "Hampshire") and [Dorset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dorset "Dorset").

Golding left the draft of a novel, *[The Double Tongue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Double_Tongue "The Double Tongue")*, set in [ancient Delphi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delphi "Delphi"), which was published posthumously in 1995.
