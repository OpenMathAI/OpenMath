**Sigrid Undset** (Norwegian pronunciation: [[ˈsɪ̂ɡːɾiː ˈʉ̂nːseːt]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Norwegian "Help:IPA/Norwegian"); 20 May 1882 – 10 June 1949) was a [Danish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danish_people "Danish people")-born [Norwegian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norwegian_people "Norwegian people") novelist. She was awarded the [1928 Nobel Prize in Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1928_Nobel_Prize_in_Literature "1928 Nobel Prize in Literature").

Born in Denmark and raised in Norway, Undset had her first books of historical fiction published in 1907. She fled Norway for the United States in 1940 because of her opposition to [Nazi Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi_Germany "Nazi Germany") and the [German invasion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Weserübung "Operation Weserübung") and [occupation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_occupation_of_Norway "German occupation of Norway") of Norway, but returned after the [end of World War II in Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/End_of_World_War_II_in_Europe "End of World War II in Europe") in 1945.

Her best-known work is *[Kristin Lavransdatter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kristin_Lavransdatter "Kristin Lavransdatter")*, a trilogy about life in Norway in the [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages "Middle Ages"), portrayed through the experiences of a woman from birth until death. Its three volumes were published between 1920 and 1922.

## Early life

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/55/Sigrid_Undset_young.jpg/250px-Sigrid_Undset_young.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Sigrid_Undset_young.jpg)

Undset as a young girl

Sigrid Undset was born on 20 May 1882 in the small town of [Kalundborg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalundborg "Kalundborg"), Denmark, at the childhood home of her mother, Charlotte Undset (1855–1939, née Anna Maria Charlotte Gyth). Her father was the Norwegian archaeologist Ingvald Martin Undset (1853–1893). Sigrid Undset was the eldest of three daughters. She and her family moved to Norway when she was two.

She grew up in the Norwegian capital, [Oslo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oslo "Oslo") (or Kristiania, as it was known until 1925), where the family moved in July 1884. When she was only 11 years old, her father died at the age of 40 after a long illness.

The family's economic situation meant that Undset had to give up hope of a university education and after a one-year secretarial course she obtained work at the age of 16 as a secretary with an engineering company in Kristiania, a post she went on to hold for 10 years. She later said that she detested the work.

She joined the [Norwegian Authors' Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norwegian_Authors'_Union "Norwegian Authors' Union") in 1907 and from 1933 through 1935 headed its Literary Council, eventually serving as the union's chairwoman from 1935 until 1940.

## Writer

While employed at office work, Undset wrote and studied. She was 16 years old when she made her first attempt at writing a novel set in the Nordic [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages "Middle Ages"). The manuscript, a historical novel set in [medieval Denmark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval_Denmark "Medieval Denmark"), was ready by the time she was 22. It was turned down by the publishing house.

Nonetheless, two years later, she completed another manuscript, much less voluminous than the first at only 80 pages. She had put aside the Middle Ages and had instead produced a realistic description of a woman with a middle-class background in contemporary Kristiania. This book was also refused by the publishers at first but it was subsequently accepted.

At the age of 25, Undset made her literary debut with a short [realist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Realism_(arts) "Realism (arts)") novel on [adultery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adultery "Adultery"), set against a contemporary background. It created a stir, and she found herself ranked as a promising young author in Norway. During the years up to 1919, Undset published a number of novels set in contemporary Kristiania, concerning the city, its inhabitants, and relationships.

This realistic period culminated in the novels *[Jenny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jenny_(novel) "Jenny (novel)")* (1911) and *[Vaaren](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaaren "Vaaren")* (Spring) (1914). The first is about a woman painter who, as a result of romantic crises, believes that she is wasting her life, and, in the end, commits suicide. The other tells of a woman who succeeds in saving both herself and her love from a serious matrimonial crisis, finally creating a secure family. These books placed Undset apart from the incipient [women's emancipation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women's_emancipation "Women's emancipation") movement in Europe.

Undset's books sold well from the start, and, after the publication of her third book, she left her office job and prepared to live on her income as a writer. Having been granted a writer's scholarship, she set out on a lengthy journey in Europe. After short stops in Denmark and Germany, she continued to Italy, arriving in Rome in December 1909, where she remained for nine months. Undset's parents had had a close relationship with Rome, and, during her stay there, she followed in their footsteps. The encounter with Southern Europe meant a great deal to her; she made friends within the circle of Scandinavian artists and writers in Rome.

## Marriage and children

In Rome, Undset met [Anders Castus Svarstad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anders_Castus_Svarstad "Anders Castus Svarstad"), a Norwegian painter, whom she married almost three years later. She was 30; Svarstad was thirteen years older, married, and had a wife and three children in Norway. It was nearly three years before Svarstad divorced from his first wife.

Undset and Svarstad were married in 1912 and went to stay in London for six months. From London, they returned to Rome, where their first child was born in January 1913. A boy, he was named after his father. In the years up to 1919, she had another child, and the household also took in Svarstad's three children from his first marriage. These were difficult years: her second child, a girl, was [mentally handicapped](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mentally_handicapped "Mentally handicapped"), as was one of Svarstad's sons by his first wife.

She continued writing, finishing her last realistic novels and collections of [short stories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Short_stories "Short stories"). She also entered the public debate on topical themes: [women's emancipation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women's_emancipation "Women's emancipation") and other ethical and moral issues. She had considerable [polemical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polemical "Polemical") gifts, and was critical of emancipation as it was developing, and of the moral and ethical decline she felt was threatening in the wake of the [First World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_World_War "First World War").

In 1919, she moved to [Lillehammer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lillehammer "Lillehammer"), a small town in the [Gudbrand Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gudbrand_Valley "Gudbrand Valley") in southeast Norway, taking her two children with her, while her husband stayed in Italy. She was then expecting her third child. The intention was that she should take a rest at Lillehammer and move back to Kristiania as soon as Svarstad had their new house in order. However, the marriage broke down and a divorce followed. In August 1919, she gave birth to her third child, at Lillehammer. She decided to make Lillehammer her home, and within two years, Bjerkebæk, a large house of traditional Norwegian timber architecture, was completed, along with a large fenced garden with views of the town and the villages around. Here she was able to retreat and concentrate on her writing.

## *Kristin Lavransdatter* trilogy and *The Master of Hestviken* tetralogy

After the birth of her third child, and with a secure roof over her head, Undset started a major project: *[Kristin Lavransdatter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kristin_Lavransdatter "Kristin Lavransdatter")*. She was at home in the subject matter, having written a short novel at an earlier stage about a period in Norwegian history closer to the Pre-Christian era. She had also published a Norwegian retelling of the [Arthurian legends](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthurian_legends "Arthurian legends"). She had studied [Old Norse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Norse "Old Norse") manuscripts and medieval [chronicles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chronicle "Chronicle") and visited and examined medieval churches and [monasteries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monasteries "Monasteries"), both at home and abroad. She was now an authority on the period she was portraying and a very different person from the 22-year-old who had written her first novel about the Middle Ages.

It was only after the end of her marriage that Undset wrote her masterpiece. In the years between 1920 and 1927, she first published the three-volume *[Kristin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kristin_Lavransdatter "Kristin Lavransdatter")*, and then the 4-volume *Olav* (Audunssøn), translated into English as *[The Master of Hestviken](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Master_of_Hestviken "The Master of Hestviken")*.

Undset experimented with modernist literary techniques such as [stream of consciousness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stream_of_consciousness_(narrative_mode) "Stream of consciousness (narrative mode)") in her novel, although the original English translation by Charles Archer excised many of these passages. In 1997, the first volume of [Tiina Nunnally](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiina_Nunnally "Tiina Nunnally")'s new translation of the work won the [PEN/Faulkner Award for Fiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PEN/Faulkner_Award_for_Fiction "PEN/Faulkner Award for Fiction") in the category of translation. The names of each volume were translated by Archer as *The Bridal Wreath*, *The Mistress of Husaby*, and *The Cross*, and by Nunnally as *The Wreath*, *The Wife*, and *The Cross*. Subsequent translation of the Hestviken tetralogy by Nunnally are retitled *Olav Audunssøn (1):Vows* (*The Axe*)*, …(2) Providence*, (*The Snake Pit*), *…(3) Crossroads* (*In The Wilderness*), and *…(4) Winter* (*The Son Avenger*).

## Catholicism

Both Undset's parents were [atheists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atheists "Atheists") and, although, in accord with the norm of the day, she and her two younger sisters were baptised and with their mother regularly attended the local [Lutheran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lutheran "Lutheran") church, the milieu in which they were raised was a thoroughly secular one. Undset spent much of her life as an agnostic, but marriage and the outbreak of the First World War were to change her attitudes. During those difficult years she experienced a crisis of faith, almost imperceptible at first, then increasingly strong. The crisis led her from clear agnostic [skepticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_skepticism "Religious skepticism"), by way of painful uneasiness about the ethical decline of the age, towards Christianity.

Beginning around 1917, Undset developed a passionate interest in the writings of Monsignor [Robert Hugh Benson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Hugh_Benson "Robert Hugh Benson"), many of whose writings she would later translate into Norwegian. However, she did not turn to the [established](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Established_church "Established church") Lutheran [Church of Norway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_Norway "Church of Norway"), where she had been nominally reared. This is because, according to Geir Hasnes, Undset had always considered the Lutheran Church "anemic" and "detested the fact that every minister seemed to preach his personal version of [Lutheranism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lutheranism "Lutheranism")."

She was received into the [Catholic Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Church_in_Norway "Catholic Church in Norway") in November 1924, after thorough instruction from the Catholic priest in her local parish. She was 42 years old. She subsequently became a [lay Dominican](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_Order_of_Saint_Dominic "Third Order of Saint Dominic").

In Norway, Undset's [conversion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_conversion "Religious conversion") to Catholicism was not only considered sensational; it was scandalous. It was also noted abroad, where her name was becoming known through the international success of *Kristin Lavransdatter*. At the time, there were very few practicing Catholics in Norway, which was an almost exclusively Lutheran country. [Anti-Catholicism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-Catholicism "Anti-Catholicism") was widespread not only among the Lutheran clergy, but through large sections of the population.

For many years, she participated in the public debate, introducing the [Catholic literary revival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_literary_revival "Catholic literary revival") into [Norwegian literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norwegian_literature "Norwegian literature"). In response, she was dubbed "The Mistress of Bjerkebæk" and "The Catholic Lady".

Undset's essays about [Elizabethan era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabethan_era "Elizabethan era") English Catholic martyrs [Margaret Clitherow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Margaret_Clitherow "Margaret Clitherow") and [Robert Southwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Southwell_(Jesuit) "Robert Southwell (Jesuit)") were collected and published in *Stages on the Road*. Furthermore, Undset's *Saga of Saints* told the whole of [Norwegian history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norwegian_history "Norwegian history") through the lives of Norwegian saints.

In May 1928, Undset travelled to England and visited [G. K. Chesterton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G._K._Chesterton "G. K. Chesterton") and [Hilaire Belloc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilaire_Belloc "Hilaire Belloc"), both of whose writings she would soon translate into Norwegian. Undset's translation of *[The Everlasting Man](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Everlasting_Man "The Everlasting Man")* was published in 1931.

In 2026, during his sermon at Seljumannamesse, bishop [Fredrik Hansen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fredrik_Hansen "Fredrik Hansen") of the [Catholic Diocese of Oslo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Diocese_of_Oslo "Roman Catholic Diocese of Oslo") announced the initiation of the [canonization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canonization "Canonization") process for Sigrid Undset.

## Later life

After 1929, Undset completed a series of novels set in contemporary [Oslo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oslo "Oslo"). She also published a number of weighty historical works which put the history of Norway into a sober perspective. In addition, she translated several [Icelandic sagas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Icelandic_sagas "Icelandic sagas") into Norwegian and published a number of literary essays, mainly on English literature, of which a long essay on the [Brontë](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brontë "Brontë") sisters, and one on [D. H. Lawrence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D._H._Lawrence "D. H. Lawrence").

In 1934, she published *Eleven Years Old*, an autobiographical work.

At the end of the 1930s, she commenced work on a new historical novel set in 18th century Scandinavia. Only the first volume, *Madame Dorthea*, was published, in 1939. The [Second World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_World_War "Second World War") broke out that same year and proceeded to break her, both as a writer and as a woman. She never completed her new novel. When [Joseph Stalin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Stalin "Joseph Stalin")'s invasion of Finland touched off the [Winter War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winter_War "Winter War"), Undset supported the Finnish war effort by donating her Nobel Prize on 25 January 1940.

## Exile

When Germany invaded Norway in April 1940, Undset was forced to flee. She had strongly criticised both [Nazi ideology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi_ideology "Nazi ideology") and [Adolf Hitler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adolf_Hitler "Adolf Hitler") since the early 1930s, and, from an early date, her books were banned as part of [censorship in Nazi Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Censorship_in_Nazi_Germany "Censorship in Nazi Germany"). She accordingly knew her name was on a list of those to be rounded up in the first wave of arrests and had no wish to become a target of the [Gestapo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gestapo "Gestapo"). She accordingly fled to neutral Sweden.

Her eldest son, [Norwegian Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norwegian_Army "Norwegian Army") [Second Lieutenant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Lieutenant "Second Lieutenant") Anders Svarstad, was [killed in action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Killed_in_action "Killed in action") at the age of 27, on 27 April 1940, while defending Segalstad Bridge in [Gausdal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gausdal "Gausdal") from German troops.

Undset's sick daughter had died shortly before the outbreak of the war. Bjerkebæk was requisitioned by the [Wehrmacht](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Army_(Wehrmacht) "German Army (Wehrmacht)"), and used as officers' quarters throughout the [Occupation of Norway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occupation_of_Norway_by_Nazi_Germany "Occupation of Norway by Nazi Germany"). Undset's library had already been secretly divided between her closest local friends. The books were hidden at great risk throughout the Nazi occupation and were returned to her after the Liberation of Norway.

In 1940, Undset and her younger son left [neutral Sweden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sweden_during_World_War_II "Sweden during World War II") then crossed the [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union "Soviet Union") via the [Trans-Siberian Railroad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trans-Siberian_Railroad "Trans-Siberian Railroad") before arriving as a [political refugee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_refugee "Political refugee") in the [United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States "United States"). There, she untiringly pleaded occupied Norway's cause and the plight of European Jews in writings, speeches and interviews. She lived in [Brooklyn Heights, New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brooklyn_Heights,_New_York "Brooklyn Heights, New York"). She was active in [St. Ansgar's Scandinavian Catholic League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Ansgar's_Scandinavian_Catholic_League?action=edit&redlink=1 "St. Ansgar's Scandinavian Catholic League") and wrote several articles for its bulletin. She also traveled to [Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida "Florida"), where she became a close friend of novelist [Marjorie Kinnan Rawlings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marjorie_Kinnan_Rawlings "Marjorie Kinnan Rawlings").

Following the [Gestapo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gestapo "Gestapo")'s arrest and [summary execution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Summary_execution "Summary execution") of Danish Lutheran pastor and playwright [Kaj Munk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaj_Munk "Kaj Munk") on 4 January 1944, the Danish resistance newspaper *[De frie Danske](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_frie_Danske "De frie Danske")* printed protests from many famous Scandinavian intellectuals, including Undset.

## Return to Norway and death

Undset returned to Norway after the liberation in 1945. She lived another four years but no longer published. Neither she nor her son Hans thrived after returning to Bjerkebæk. In May 1948 she had a psychological breakdown, while on visit to her birth town [Kalundborg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalundborg "Kalundborg"). In June 1949 she was admitted to Lillehammer hospital with kidney inflammation. Undset died shortly after at 67 in [Lillehammer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lillehammer "Lillehammer"), Norway, where she had lived from 1919 through 1940. She was buried in the village of [Mesnali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesnali "Mesnali"), 15 kilometers east of Lillehammer, where her daughter and son are also buried. The grave is recognizable by three black crosses.

## Honors

- Undset won the Nobel prize for literature in 1928, for which she was nominated by [Helga Eng](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helga_Eng "Helga Eng"), member of the [Norwegian Academy of Science and Letters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norwegian_Academy_of_Science_and_Letters "Norwegian Academy of Science and Letters").
- A mountain on the moon, east of crater Lambert at Mare Imbrium, was called Mons Undset, however, it was erroneously mentioned as *Mons Undest* on Lunar Topographic Orthophotomap 40B4. The International Astronomical Union (IAU) refused to include Mons Undset in the alphabetic gazetteer of officially named lunar formations. This mountain is nowadays known as Lambert γ (Lambert gamma).
- A crater on the planet [Venus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venus "Venus") was named after Undset.
- Minor planet [9919 Undset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_minor_planets:_9001–10000#9901–10000 "List of minor planets: 9001–10000") is named after her. [MPC](https://minorplanetcenter.net/db_search/show_object?object_id=9919) **·** [JPL](https://ssd.jpl.nasa.gov/tools/sbdb_lookup.html#/?sstr=9919)
- Undset was depicted on a Norwegian 500 kroner note and a two-kroner postage stamp from 1982. Neighboring Sweden put her on a stamp in 1998.
- Bjerkebæk, Undset's home in Lillehammer, is now part of the [Maihaugen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maihaugen "Maihaugen") museum. The farmhouse was [listed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Listed_building "Listed building") in 1983. Efforts to restore and furnish the houses as they were during the time of her occupancy were begun in 1997. The museum was restored and more buildings were opened to the public in May 2007.
- Undset is depicted on the tail fin of a [Norwegian Air Shuttle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norwegian_Air_Shuttle "Norwegian Air Shuttle") Boeing [737-800](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_737_Next_Generation "Boeing 737 Next Generation"), with the registration LN-NGY.
