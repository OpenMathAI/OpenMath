**Harry Sinclair Lewis** (February 7, 1885 – January 10, 1951) was an American novelist, short-story writer, and playwright. In [1930](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1930_Nobel_Prize_in_Literature "1930 Nobel Prize in Literature"), he became the first author from the United States (and the first from the [Americas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Americas "Americas")) to receive the [Nobel Prize in Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Literature "Nobel Prize in Literature"), which was awarded "for his vigorous and graphic art of description and his ability to create, with wit and humor, new types of characters." Lewis wrote six popular novels: *[Main Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Main_Street_(novel) "Main Street (novel)")* (1920), *[Babbitt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babbitt_(novel) "Babbitt (novel)")* (1922), *[Arrowsmith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arrowsmith_(novel) "Arrowsmith (novel)")* (1925), *[Elmer Gantry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elmer_Gantry "Elmer Gantry")* (1927), *[Dodsworth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dodsworth_(novel) "Dodsworth (novel)")* (1929), and *[It Can't Happen Here](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/It_Can't_Happen_Here "It Can't Happen Here")* (1935).

Several of his notable works were critical of American [capitalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capitalism "Capitalism") and [materialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_materialism "Economic materialism") during the [interwar period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interwar_period "Interwar period"). Lewis is respected for his strong characterizations of modern working women. [H. L. Mencken](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H._L._Mencken "H. L. Mencken") wrote of him, "[If] there was ever a novelist among us with an authentic call to the trade ... it is this red-haired tornado from the Minnesota wilds."

## Early life

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/68/Boyhood_home_of_Sinclair_Lewis.jpg/250px-Boyhood_home_of_Sinclair_Lewis.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Boyhood_home_of_Sinclair_Lewis.jpg)

The [Sinclair Lewis Boyhood Home](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinclair_Lewis_Boyhood_Home "Sinclair Lewis Boyhood Home") museum

Lewis was born February 7, 1885, in the village of [Sauk Centre, Minnesota](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sauk_Centre,_Minnesota "Sauk Centre, Minnesota"), to Edwin J. Lewis, a physician of [Welsh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh_Americans "Welsh Americans") descent, and Emma Kermott Lewis. He had two older siblings, Fred (born 1875) and Claude (born 1878). His father was a stern disciplinarian, who had difficulty relating to his sensitive, unathletic third son. Lewis's mother died in 1891. The next year Edwin married Isabel Warner, whom young Lewis apparently liked. Lewis began reading books while young, and kept a diary. Throughout his lonely boyhood, the ungainly child—tall, extremely thin, stricken with [acne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acne "Acne") and somewhat pop-eyed—had trouble making friends and pined after local girls. At the age of 13, he ran away from home and unsuccessfully tried to become a drummer boy in the [Spanish–American War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish–American_War "Spanish–American War"). In late 1902, Lewis left home for a year at Oberlin Academy (the then-preparatory department of [Oberlin College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oberlin_College "Oberlin College")) to qualify for acceptance at [Yale University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yale_University "Yale University"). While at Oberlin, he developed a religious enthusiasm that waxed and waned for much of his remaining teenage years. Lewis later became an atheist. He entered Yale in 1903, but did not receive his [bachelor's degree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor's_degree "Bachelor's degree") until 1908, taking time off to work at [Helicon Home Colony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helicon_Home_Colony "Helicon Home Colony"), [Upton Sinclair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upton_Sinclair "Upton Sinclair")'s [cooperative-living](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Housing_cooperative "Housing cooperative") colony in [Englewood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Englewood,_New_Jersey "Englewood, New Jersey"), [New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Jersey "New Jersey"), and to travel to [Panama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panama "Panama"). Lewis's undistinguished looks, country manners and seeming self-importance made it difficult for him to win and keep friends at Oberlin and Yale. He did make a few friends among the students and professors, some of whom recognized his promise as a writer.

## Career

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/6c/Lewis-Sinclair-LOC.jpg/250px-Lewis-Sinclair-LOC.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Lewis-Sinclair-LOC.jpg)

Lewis in 1914

Lewis's earliest published creative work—romantic poetry and short sketches—appeared in the *Yale Courant* and the *[Yale Literary Magazine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yale_Literary_Magazine "Yale Literary Magazine")*, of which he became an editor. After graduation Lewis moved from job to job and from place to place in an effort to make ends meet, writing fiction for publication and to chase away boredom. In the summer of 1908, Lewis worked as an editorial writer at a newspaper in [Waterloo, Iowa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waterloo,_Iowa "Waterloo, Iowa"). In September of that year, he moved to the [Carmel-by-the-Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carmel-by-the-Sea,_California "Carmel-by-the-Sea, California") writers' colony near [Monterey, California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monterey,_California "Monterey, California") to work for the [MacGowan sisters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alice_MacGowan "Alice MacGowan") and to meet poet [George Sterling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Sterling "George Sterling") in person. He left Carmel after six months, moving to San Francisco where Sterling helped him get a job at the *San Francisco Evening Bulletin.* Lewis returned to Carmel in spring 1910 and met [Jack London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jack_London "Jack London").

In fall 1910, Lewis moved to [New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_(state) "New York (state)") to work for [Frederick A. Stokes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_A._Stokes "Frederick A. Stokes"). The next year, he joined the [New York chapter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialist_Party_of_New_York "Socialist Party of New York") of the [Socialist Party of America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialist_Party_of_America "Socialist Party of America"). Among his colleagues in the intellectual wing of the party were [Walter Lippmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Lippmann "Walter Lippmann"), [William English Walling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_English_Walling "William English Walling"), [Ernest Poole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_Poole "Ernest Poole"), and [John Sloan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Sloan "John Sloan").

While working for newspapers and publishing houses he developed a facility for turning out shallow, popular stories that were purchased by a variety of magazines. He also earned money by selling plots to London, including one for the latter's unfinished novel *[The Assassination Bureau, Ltd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Assassination_Bureau,_Ltd "The Assassination Bureau, Ltd")*.

Lewis's first published book was *Hike and the Aeroplane*, a [Tom Swift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tom_Swift "Tom Swift")-style [potboiler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potboiler "Potboiler") that appeared in 1912 under the pseudonym Tom Graham.

Sinclair Lewis's first serious novel, *[Our Mr. Wrenn: The Romantic Adventures of a Gentle Man](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Our_Mr._Wrenn "Our Mr. Wrenn")*, appeared in 1914, followed by *[The Trail of the Hawk: A Comedy of the Seriousness of Life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Trail_of_the_Hawk "The Trail of the Hawk")* (1915) and *[The Job](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Job_(1917_novel) "The Job (1917 novel)")* (1917). That same year also saw the publication of another potboiler, *[The Innocents: A Story for Lovers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Innocents:_A_Story_for_Lovers "The Innocents: A Story for Lovers")*, an expanded version of a [serial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serial_(literature) "Serial (literature)") story that had originally appeared in *[Woman's Home Companion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woman's_Home_Companion "Woman's Home Companion")*. *[Free Air](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_Air "Free Air")*, another refurbished serial story, was published in 1919.

### Commercial success

Upon moving to [Washington, D.C.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington,_D.C. "Washington, D.C."), Lewis devoted himself to writing. As early as 1916, he began taking notes for a realistic novel about small-town life. Work on that novel continued through mid-1920, when he completed *[Main Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Main_Street_(novel) "Main Street (novel)")*, which was published on October 23, 1920. His biographer [Mark Schorer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Schorer "Mark Schorer") wrote in 1961 that the phenomenal success of *Main Street* "was the most sensational event in twentieth-century American publishing history". Lewis's agent had the most optimistic projection of sales at 25,000 copies. In its first six months, *Main Street* sold 180,000 copies, and within a few years, sales were estimated at two million. Richard Lingeman wrote in 2002, "*Main Street* made [Lewis] rich—earning him about 3 million current dollars" (almost $5 million, as of 2022).

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d6/Sinclair_Lewis_House.JPG/250px-Sinclair_Lewis_House.JPG?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Sinclair_Lewis_House.JPG)

Sinclair Lewis's former residence in [Washington, D.C.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington,_D.C. "Washington, D.C.")

Lewis followed up this first great success with *[Babbitt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babbitt_(novel) "Babbitt (novel)")* (1922), a novel that satirized the American commercial culture and [boosterism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boosterism "Boosterism"). The story was set in the fictional Midwestern town of [Zenith, Winnemac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winnemac_(fictional_U.S._state) "Winnemac (fictional U.S. state)"), a setting to which Lewis returned in future novels, including *[Arrowsmith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arrowsmith_(novel) "Arrowsmith (novel)"), [Elmer Gantry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elmer_Gantry "Elmer Gantry")*, *[Gideon Planish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gideon_Planish "Gideon Planish")* and *[Dodsworth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dodsworth_(novel) "Dodsworth (novel)")*.

Lewis continued his success in the 1920s with *[Arrowsmith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arrowsmith_(novel) "Arrowsmith (novel)")* (1925), a novel about the challenges faced by an idealistic doctor. It was awarded the [Pulitzer Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulitzer_Prize_for_Fiction "Pulitzer Prize for Fiction"), which Lewis declined, still upset that *Main Street* had not won the prize. It was adapted as a 1931 [Hollywood film](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arrowsmith_(film) "Arrowsmith (film)") directed by [John Ford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Ford "John Ford") and starring [Ronald Colman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Colman "Ronald Colman") which was nominated for four [Academy Awards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academy_Award "Academy Award").

Next Lewis published *[Elmer Gantry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elmer_Gantry "Elmer Gantry")* (1927), which depicted an [evangelical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evangelicalism "Evangelicalism") minister as deeply hypocritical. The novel was denounced by many religious leaders and banned in some U.S. cities. It was [adapted for the screen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elmer_Gantry_(film) "Elmer Gantry (film)") more than a generation later as the basis of the 1960 movie starring [Burt Lancaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burt_Lancaster "Burt Lancaster"), who earned a [Best Actor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academy_Award_for_Best_Actor "Academy Award for Best Actor") Oscar for his performance in the title role. The film won two more awards as well.

Lewis next published *[Dodsworth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dodsworth_(novel) "Dodsworth (novel)")* (1929), a novel about the most affluent and successful members of American society. He portrayed them as leading essentially pointless lives in spite of great wealth and advantages. The book was adapted for the [Broadway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broadway_theatre "Broadway theatre") stage in 1934 by [Sidney Howard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidney_Howard "Sidney Howard"), who also wrote the screenplay for the [1936 film version](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dodsworth_(film) "Dodsworth (film)") directed by [William Wyler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Wyler "William Wyler"), which was a great success at the time. The film is still highly regarded; in 1990, it was selected for preservation in the [National Film Registry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Film_Registry "National Film Registry"), and in 2005 *[Time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_(magazine) "Time (magazine)")* magazine named it one of the "100 Best Movies" of the past 80 years.

During the late 1920s and 1930s, Lewis wrote many short stories for a variety of magazines and publications. "[Bongo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bongo_(short_story) "Bongo (short story)")" (1930) is a tale about a bear cub who wants to escape the circus in search of a better life in the real world, first published in *[Cosmopolitan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmopolitan_(magazine) "Cosmopolitan (magazine)")* magazine. The story was acquired by [Walt Disney Pictures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walt_Disney_Pictures "Walt Disney Pictures") in 1940 for a possible feature film. World War II sidetracked those plans until 1947. Disney used the story as part of its feature *[Fun and Fancy Free](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fun_and_Fancy_Free "Fun and Fancy Free")*.

### Nobel Prize

In 1930 Lewis won the Nobel Prize in Literature, the first writer from the United States to receive the award, after he had been nominated by [Henrik Schück](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henrik_Schück "Henrik Schück"), member of the [Swedish Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swedish_Academy "Swedish Academy"). In the academy's presentation speech, special attention was paid to *Babbitt*. In his Nobel Lecture, Lewis praised [Theodore Dreiser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodore_Dreiser "Theodore Dreiser"), [Willa Cather](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Willa_Cather "Willa Cather"), [Ernest Hemingway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_Hemingway "Ernest Hemingway"), and other contemporaries, but also lamented that "in America most of us—not readers alone, but even writers—are still afraid of any literature which is not a glorification of everything American, a glorification of our faults as well as our virtues," and that America is "the most contradictory, the most depressing, the most stirring, of any land in the world today." He also offered a profound criticism of the American literary establishment: "Our American professors like their literature clear and cold and pure and very dead."

## Later years

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/02/Browne-Lewis-1943.jpg/250px-Browne-Lewis-1943.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Browne-Lewis-1943.jpg)

After winning the Nobel Prize, Lewis wrote eleven more novels, ten of which appeared in his lifetime. The best remembered is *[It Can't Happen Here](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/It_Can't_Happen_Here "It Can't Happen Here")* (1935), a novel about the election of a [fascist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fascist "Fascist") to the American presidency.

After praising Dreiser as "pioneering", that he "more than any other man, marching alone, usually unappreciated, often hated, has cleared the trail from Victorian and Howellsian timidity and gentility in American fiction to honesty and boldness and passion of life" in his Nobel Lecture in December 1930, in March 1931 Lewis publicly accused Dreiser of plagiarizing a book by Dorothy Thompson, Lewis's wife, which led to a well-publicized fight, wherein Dreiser repeatedly slapped Lewis. Thompson initially made the accusation in 1928 regarding her work "The New Russia" and Dreiser's "Dreiser Goes to Russia", though *The New York Times* also linked the dispute to competition between Dreiser and Lewis over the Nobel Prize. Dreiser fired back that Sinclair's 1925 novel *[Arrowsmith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arrowsmith_(novel) "Arrowsmith (novel)")* (adapted later that year as a [feature film](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arrowsmith_(film) "Arrowsmith (film)")) was unoriginal and that Dreiser himself was first approached to write it, which was disputed by the wife of *Arrowsmith*'s subject, microbiologist Dr. [Paul de Kruif](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_de_Kruif "Paul de Kruif"). The feud carried on for some months. In 1944, Lewis campaigned to have Dreiser recognized by the [American Academy of Arts and Letters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Academy_of_Arts_and_Letters "American Academy of Arts and Letters").

After an alcoholic binge in 1937, Lewis checked in for treatment to the [Austen Riggs Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austen_Riggs_Center "Austen Riggs Center"), a psychiatric hospital in [Stockbridge, Massachusetts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stockbridge,_Massachusetts "Stockbridge, Massachusetts"). His doctors gave him a blunt assessment that he needed to decide "whether he was going to live without alcohol or die by it, one or the other." Lewis checked out after ten days, lacking any "fundamental understanding of his problem", as one of his physicians wrote to a colleague.

In the autumn of 1940, Lewis visited his old acquaintance, [William Ellery Leonard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Ellery_Leonard "William Ellery Leonard"), in Madison, Wisconsin. Leonard arranged a meeting with the chancellor of the University of Wisconsin–Madison and a tour of the campus. Lewis immediately became enthralled with the university and the city and offered to remain and teach a course in creative writing in the upcoming semester. For a month he was quite enamored of his professorial role. Suddenly, on November 7, after giving only five classes to his select group of 24 students, he announced that he had taught them all that he knew. He left Madison the next day.

In the 1940s, Lewis and rabbi-turned-popular-author [Lewis Browne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lewis_Browne "Lewis Browne") frequently appeared on the lecture platform together, touring the United States and debating before audiences of as many as 3,000 people, addressing such questions as "Has the Modern Woman Made Good?", "The Country Versus the City", "Is the Machine Age Wrecking Civilization?", and "Can Fascism Happen Here?". The pair were described as "the [Gallagher and Shean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gallagher_and_Shean "Gallagher and Shean") of the lecture circuit" by Lewis biographer Richard Lingeman.

In the early 1940s, Lewis lived in [Duluth, Minnesota](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duluth,_Minnesota "Duluth, Minnesota"). During this time, he wrote the novel *Kingsblood Royal* (1947), set in the fictional city of Grand Republic, Minnesota, an enlarged and updated version of Zenith. It is based on the [Sweet Trials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ossian_Sweet "Ossian Sweet") in [Detroit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Detroit "Detroit") in which an [African-American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African-American "African-American") doctor was denied the chance to purchase a house in a "white" section of the city. Lewis' creation of the novel was preceded by his introduction to the black community via [Edward Francis Murphy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Francis_Murphy "Edward Francis Murphy"), a [Josephite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Society_of_St._Joseph_of_the_Sacred_Heart_(Josephites) "Society of St. Joseph of the Sacred Heart (Josephites)") priest with whom he had attended school as a child. *Kingsblood* was a powerful and very early contribution to the [civil rights movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_rights_movement "Civil rights movement").

In 1943, Lewis went to Hollywood to work on a script with [Dore Schary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dore_Schary "Dore Schary"), who had just resigned as executive head of [Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer "Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer")'s low-budget film department to concentrate on writing and producing his own films. The resulting screenplay was *Storm In the West*, "a traditional American western" — except for the fact that it was also an allegory of World War II, with primary villain Hygatt ([Hitler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adolf_Hitler "Adolf Hitler")) and his henchmen Gribbles ([Goebbels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Goebbels "Joseph Goebbels")) and Gerrett ([Goering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann_Göring "Hermann Göring")) plotting to take over the Franson Ranch, the Poling Ranch, and so on. The screenplay was deemed too political by MGM studio executives and was shelved, and the film was never made. *Storm In the West* was finally published in 1963, with a foreword by Schary detailing the work's origins, the authors' creative process, and the screenplay's ultimate fate.

Sinclair Lewis had been a frequent visitor to Williamstown, Massachusetts. In 1946, he rented Thorvale Farm on Oblong Road. While working on his novel *Kingsblood Royal*, he purchased this summer estate and upgraded the [Georgian mansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georgian_architecture "Georgian architecture") along with a farmhouse and many outbuildings. By 1948, Lewis had created a gentleman's farm consisting of 720 acres (290 ha) of agricultural and forest land. His intended residence in Williamstown was short-lived because of his medical problems.

## Personal life

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c9/Sinclair_Lewis_with_Dorothy_Thompson_and_son_1935.jpg/330px-Sinclair_Lewis_with_Dorothy_Thompson_and_son_1935.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Sinclair_Lewis_with_Dorothy_Thompson_and_son_1935.jpg)

Lewis with Thompson and son in 1935

In 1914 Lewis married Grace Livingston Hegger (1887–1981), an editor at *[Vogue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vogue_(magazine) "Vogue (magazine)")* magazine. They had one son, Wells Lewis (1917–1944). It is commonly claimed, erroneously, that Wells Lewis was named after British author [H. G. Wells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H._G._Wells "H. G. Wells"). However, Hegger denies this in her autobigraphical book *With Love From Gracie*. ""We named the baby Wells. Not after H.G. Wells, the person, as is generally assumed. We did not know the distinguished English novelist then...."

Serving as a U.S. Army lieutenant during [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II"), Wells Lewis was killed in action on October 29, 1944, amid Allied efforts to rescue the ["Lost Battalion"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lost_Battalion_(Europe,_World_War_II) "Lost Battalion (Europe, World War II)") in France. [Dean Acheson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dean_Acheson "Dean Acheson"), the future [Secretary of State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Secretary_of_State "United States Secretary of State"), was a neighbor and family friend in Washington, and observed that Sinclair's literary "success was not good for that marriage, or for either of the parties to it, or for Lewis's work" and the family moved out of town.

Lewis divorced Grace on April 16, 1928. On May 14, he married the noted international correspondent and newspaper columnist [Dorothy Thompson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dorothy_Thompson "Dorothy Thompson"). In 1928, he and Dorothy purchased a second home in rural Vermont. They had a son, Michael Lewis (1930–1975), who became a stage actor. Their marriage had virtually ended by 1937, and they divorced in 1942.

Lewis died in Rome from advanced alcoholism, on January 10, 1951, aged 65. His body was cremated and his remains were buried at Greenwood Cemetery in [Sauk Centre, Minnesota](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sauk_Centre,_Minnesota "Sauk Centre, Minnesota"). His final novel *World So Wide* (1951) was published posthumously.

[William Shirer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Shirer "William Shirer"), a friend and admirer of Lewis, argued that Lewis did not die from alcoholism. He reported that Lewis had a heart attack and that his doctors advised him to stop drinking if he wanted to live. Lewis did not stop, and perhaps could not; he died when his heart stopped.

In summarizing Lewis's career, Shirer said:

> It has become rather commonplace for so-called literary critics to write off Sinclair Lewis as a novelist. Compared to ... [Fitzgerald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F._Scott_Fitzgerald "F. Scott Fitzgerald"), [Hemingway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_Hemingway "Ernest Hemingway"), [Dos Passos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Dos_Passos "John Dos Passos"), and [Faulkner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Faulkner "William Faulkner") ... Lewis lacked style. Yet his impact on modern American life ... was greater than all of the other four writers together.

## Legacy

Compared to his contemporaries, Lewis's reputation suffered a precipitous decline among literary scholars throughout the 20th century. Despite his enormous popularity during the 1920s, by the 21st century most of his works had been eclipsed in prominence by other writers with less commercial success during the same time period, such as [F. Scott Fitzgerald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F._Scott_Fitzgerald "F. Scott Fitzgerald") and [Ernest Hemingway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_Hemingway "Ernest Hemingway").

Since the 2010s there has been renewed interest in Lewis's work, in particular his 1935 dystopian satire *[It Can't Happen Here](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/It_Can't_Happen_Here "It Can't Happen Here")*. In the aftermath of the [2016 United States presidential election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016_United_States_presidential_election "2016 United States presidential election"), *It Can't Happen Here* surged to the top of [Amazon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amazon.com "Amazon.com")'s list of best-selling books. Scholars have found parallels in his novels to the [COVID-19 crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic "COVID-19 pandemic"), and to the rise of [Donald Trump](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Trump "Donald Trump").

He has been honored by the [U.S. Postal Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Postal_Service "United States Postal Service") with a [postage stamp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postage_stamp "Postage stamp") in the [Great Americans series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Americans_series "Great Americans series"). In 1960 Polish American sculptor [Joseph Kiselewski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Kiselewski "Joseph Kiselewski") was commissioned to create a bust of Lewis, now in the [Great River Regional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_River_Regional_Library "Great River Regional Library") public library in Sauk Centre.
