**Pär Fabian Lagerkvist** (23 May 1891 – 11 July 1974) was a Swedish author who received the [1951 Nobel Prize in Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1951_Nobel_Prize_in_Literature "1951 Nobel Prize in Literature").

Lagerkvist wrote poetry, plays, novels, short stories, and essays of considerable expressive power and influence from his early 20s to his late 70s. One of his central themes was the fundamental question of good and evil, which he examined through such figures as [Barabbas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barabbas "Barabbas"), the man who was freed instead of Jesus, and [Ahasuerus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahasuerus "Ahasuerus"), the [Wandering Jew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wandering_Jew "Wandering Jew"). As a moralist, he used religious motifs and figures from the [Christian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity "Christianity") tradition without following the doctrines of a [church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_Church "Christian Church").

## Biography and works

Lagerkvist was born in [Växjö](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Växjö "Växjö") ([Småland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Småland "Småland")). He received a traditional religious education – he would later say, with little exaggeration, that he "had had the good fortune to grow up in a home where the only books known were the Bible and the Book of Hymns". In his teens he broke away from Christian beliefs, but, unlike many other writers and thinkers in his generation, he did not become vehemently critical of religious beliefs as such. Though he was politically a socialist for most of his life, he never indulged in the idea that "religion is the opium of the people". Much of his writing is informed by a lifelong interest in man and his symbols and gods, and in the position of Man (both as individual and mankind) in a world where the Divine is no longer present, no longer speaking.

In his early years Lagerkvist supported modernist and aesthetically radical views, as shown by his manifesto *Ordkonst och bildkonst* (*Word Art and Picture Art*, 1913) and the play *Den Svåra Stunden* ("The Difficult Hour").

One of the author's earliest works is *Ångest* (*Anguish*, 1916), a violent and disillusioned collection of poems. His anguish was derived from his fear of death, the [World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I "World War I"), and personal crisis. He tried to explore how a person can find a meaningful life in a world where a war can kill millions for very little reason. "Anguish, anguish is my heritage / the wound of my throat / the cry of my heart in the world." ("Anguish", 1916.) "Love is nothing. Anguish is everything / the anguish of living." ("Love is nothing", 1916.) This pessimism, however, slowly faded, as testified by his subsequent works, *Det eviga leendet* (*The Eternal Smile*, 1920), the autobiographical novel *Gäst hos verkligheten* (*Guest of Reality*, 1925) and the prose monologue *Det besegrade livet* (*The defeated Life*, 1927), in which the faith in man is predominant. From *The Eternal Smile* on, his style largely abandoned the expressionist pathos and brusque effects of his early works and there was a strong striving for simplicity, classical precision and clean telling, sometimes appearing close to naivism. The content, however, was never truly naive. A Swedish critic remarked that "Lagerkvist and [John the Evangelist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_the_Evangelist "John the Evangelist") are two masters at expressing profound things with a highly restricted choice of words".

Ten years after *Ångest*, Lagerkvist married for the second time, a union which was to provide a pillar of safety in his life until the death of his wife forty years later. *Hjärtats sånger* (*Songs of the Heart*) (1926) appeared at this time, bearing witness to his pride and love for his consort. This collection is much less desperate in its tone than *Ångest*, and established him as one of the foremost Swedish poets of his generation.

His prose [novella](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Novella "Novella") *Bödeln* ("The Hangman", 1933), later adapted for the stage (*The Hangman*, 1933; play, 1934), shows his growing concern with the [totalitarianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Totalitarianism "Totalitarianism") and brutality that began to sweep across Europe in the years prior to World War II. [Nazism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazism "Nazism") was one of the main targets of the work and *[Der Stürmer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Der_Stürmer "Der Stürmer")* responded with a very dismissive review. Criticism against [Fascism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fascism "Fascism") is also present in the play *Mannen utan själ* (*The Man Without a Soul*, 1936). During the [Second World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II"), he was a member of the anti-Nazi organization Tisdagsklubbe and was on the [Gestapo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gestapo "Gestapo")'s death lists in the face of a possible German invasion of Sweden.

In September 1940 Lagerkvist was elected a member of the [Swedish Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swedish_Academy "Swedish Academy"), succeeding [Verner von Heidenstam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Verner_von_Heidenstam "Verner von Heidenstam") on chair 8 in December the same year.

Lagerkvist's 1944 novel *Dvärgen* (*[The Dwarf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Dwarf_(Lagerkvist_novel) "The Dwarf (Lagerkvist novel)")*), a searching, ironic tale about evil, was the first to bring him positive international attention outside of the Nordic countries. The work was followed in 1949 by the unusual, lyrical play *Låt människan leva* (*Let Man Live*).

*[Barabbas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barabbas_(novel) "Barabbas (novel)")* (1950), which was immediately hailed as a literary masterpiece (by fellow Nobel laureate [André Gide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/André_Gide "André Gide"), among others) is probably Lagerkvist's most famous work. The novel is based on a [Biblical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biblical "Biblical") story. Jesus of [Nazareth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazareth "Nazareth") was sentenced to die by the Roman authorities immediately before the Jewish [Passover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passover "Passover"), when it was customary for the Romans to release someone convicted of a capital offense. When the Roman procurator [Pontius Pilate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pontius_Pilate "Pontius Pilate") offers to free either Jesus or Barabbas (a convicted thief and murderer), a Jerusalem mob demands the release of Barabbas, who later watches Jesus as he bears the cross to Golgotha, witnesses the crucifixion, and then spends the rest of his life trying to understand why he was chosen to live rather than Jesus. A [movie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barabbas_(1961_film) "Barabbas (1961 film)") based upon the novel was filmed in 1961, with [Anthony Quinn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthony_Quinn "Anthony Quinn") playing the title role.

In 1951 Pär Lagerkvist was awarded the [Nobel Prize in Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Literature "Nobel Prize in Literature") "for the artistic vigour and true independence of mind with which he endeavours in his poetry to find answers to the eternal questions confronting mankind.". He had first been proposed for the prize in 1947. Following the publication of *Barabbas*, Lagerkvist had been one of the favourites to be awarded the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1950. In 1951 the Nobel committee for literature received nine nominations for Lagerkvist, including nominations from the French authors and previous laureates [André Gide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/André_Gide "André Gide") and [Roger Martin du Gard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roger_Martin_du_Gard "Roger Martin du Gard"), and the [Swedish Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swedish_Academy "Swedish Academy") decided to award him the prize.

Lagerkvist died in [Stockholm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stockholm "Stockholm") in 1974 at the age of 83, his wife having died in 1967.
