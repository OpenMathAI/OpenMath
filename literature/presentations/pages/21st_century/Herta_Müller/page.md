**Herta Müller** (German: [[ˈhɛʁta ˈmʏlɐ]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Standard_German "Help:IPA/Standard German") [ⓘ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:De-Herta_M%C3%BCller.ogg "File:De-Herta Müller.ogg"); born 17 August 1953) is a Romanian-German novelist, poet, essayist and recipient of the [2009 Nobel Prize in Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2009_Nobel_Prize_in_Literature "2009 Nobel Prize in Literature"). She was born in [Nițchidorf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nițchidorf "Nițchidorf"), [Timiș County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timiș_County "Timiș County") in Romania; her native languages are German and Romanian. Since the early 1990s, she has been internationally established, and her works have been translated into more than twenty languages.

Müller is noted for her works depicting the effects of violence, cruelty and terror, usually in the setting of the [Socialist Republic of Romania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialist_Republic_of_Romania "Socialist Republic of Romania") under the repressive [Nicolae Ceaușescu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolae_Ceaușescu "Nicolae Ceaușescu") regime which she has experienced herself. Many of her works are told from the viewpoint of the [German minority in Romania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germans_of_Romania "Germans of Romania") and are also a depiction of the modern history of the Germans in the [Banat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banat "Banat") and [Transylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transylvania "Transylvania"). Her much acclaimed 2009 novel *[The Hunger Angel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Hunger_Angel "The Hunger Angel")* (*Atemschaukel*) portrays the [deportation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deportation "Deportation") of Romania's German minority to [Soviet Gulags](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulag "Gulag") during the [Soviet occupation of Romania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_occupation_of_Romania "Soviet occupation of Romania") for use as [German forced labour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forced_labor_of_Germans_in_the_Soviet_Union "Forced labor of Germans in the Soviet Union").

Müller has received more than twenty awards to date, including the [Kleist Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kleist_Prize "Kleist Prize") (1994), the [Aristeion Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristeion_Prize "Aristeion Prize") (1995), the [International Dublin Literary Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Dublin_Literary_Award "International Dublin Literary Award") (1998) and the [Franz Werfel Human Rights Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_Werfel_Human_Rights_Award "Franz Werfel Human Rights Award") (2009). On 8 October 2009, the [Swedish Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swedish_Academy "Swedish Academy") announced that she had been awarded the Nobel Prize in Literature, describing her as a woman "who, with the concentration of poetry and the frankness of prose, depicts the landscape of the dispossessed".

## Early life

Müller was born to [Banat Swabian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banat_Swabians "Banat Swabians") [Catholic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic "Catholic") farmers in [Nițchidorf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nițchidorf "Nițchidorf") (German: Nitzkydorf; Hungarian: Niczkyfalva), up to the 1980s a German-speaking village in the [Romanian Banat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanian_Banat "Romanian Banat") in southwestern Romania. Her grandfather had been a wealthy farmer and merchant, but his property was confiscated by the Communist regime. Her father was a member of the [Waffen-SS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waffen-SS "Waffen-SS") during [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II"), and earned a living as a truck driver in Communist Romania. In 1945, her mother, born 1928 as Katarina Gion, then aged 17, was among 100,000 of the German minority [deported](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deportation "Deportation") to [forced labour camps in the Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forced_labor_of_Germans_in_the_Soviet_Union "Forced labor of Germans in the Soviet Union"), from which she was released in 1950. Müller's native language is German; she learned [Romanian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanian_language "Romanian language") only in [grammar school](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grammar_school "Grammar school"). She graduated from [Nikolaus Lenau High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nikolaus_Lenau_High_School "Nikolaus Lenau High School") before becoming a student of [German studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_studies "German studies") and [Romanian literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanian_literature "Romanian literature") at [West University of Timișoara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_University_of_Timișoara "West University of Timișoara").

In 1976, Müller began working as a translator for an engineering factory, but was dismissed in 1979 for her refusal to cooperate with the [Securitate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Securitate "Securitate"), the Communist regime's secret police. After her dismissal, she initially earned a living by teaching in [kindergarten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kindergarten "Kindergarten") and giving private German lessons.

## Career

Müller's first book, *Niederungen* (*[Nadirs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nadirs_(autobiography) "Nadirs (autobiography)")*), was published in Romania in German in 1982, receiving a prize from the Central Committee of the [Union of Communist Youth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_of_Communist_Youth "Union of Communist Youth"). The book was about a child's view of the German-cultural Banat. Some members of the Banat Swabian community criticized Müller for "fouling her own nest" by her unsympathetic portrayal of village life. Müller was a member of [Aktionsgruppe Banat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aktionsgruppe_Banat "Aktionsgruppe Banat"), a group of German-speaking writers in Romania who supported freedom of speech over the censorship they faced under [Nicolae Ceaușescu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolae_Ceaușescu "Nicolae Ceaușescu")'s government, and her works, including *[The Land of Green Plums](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Land_of_Green_Plums "The Land of Green Plums")*, deal with these issues. Radu Tinu, the Securitate officer in charge of her case, denies that she ever suffered any persecutions, a claim that is opposed by Müller's own version of her (ongoing) persecution in an article in the German weekly *[Die Zeit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Die_Zeit "Die Zeit")* in July 2009.

Müller in Hanover, 1992

After being refused permission to emigrate to West Germany in 1985, Müller was finally allowed to leave along with her then-husband, novelist [Richard Wagner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Wagner_(novelist) "Richard Wagner (novelist)"), in 1987, and they settled in [West Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Berlin "West Berlin"), where both still live. In the following years, she accepted lectureships at universities in Germany and abroad. Müller was elected to membership in the [Deutsche Akademie für Sprache und Dichtung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deutsche_Akademie_für_Sprache_und_Dichtung "Deutsche Akademie für Sprache und Dichtung") in 1995, and other honorary positions followed. In 1997, she withdrew from the [PEN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_PEN "International PEN") centre of Germany in protest of its merger with the former [German Democratic Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Democratic_Republic "German Democratic Republic") branch. In July 2008, Müller sent a critical open letter to [Horia-Roman Patapievici](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horia-Roman_Patapievici "Horia-Roman Patapievici"), president of the [Romanian Cultural Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanian_Cultural_Institute "Romanian Cultural Institute") in reaction to the moral and financial support given by the institute to two former informants of the Securitate participating at the Romanian-German Summer School.

The critic [Denis Scheck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denis_Scheck "Denis Scheck") described visiting Müller at her home in Berlin and seeing that her desk contained a drawer full of single letters cut from a newspaper she had entirely destroyed in the process. Realising that she used the letters to write texts, he felt he had "entered the workshop of a true poet".

Reading *The Hunger Angel*, Potsdam, July 2010

*[The Passport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Passport_(novel) "The Passport (novel)")*, first published in Germany as *Der Mensch ist ein großer Fasan auf der Welt* in 1986, is, according to *[The Times Literary Supplement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Times_Literary_Supplement "The Times Literary Supplement")*, "couched in the strange code engendered by repression: indecipherable because there is nothing specific to decipher, it is candid, but somehow beside the point, redolent of things unsaid. From odd observations the villagers sometimes make ("Man is nothing but a pheasant in the world"), to chapters titled after unimportant props ("The Pot Hole", "The Needle"), everything points to a strategy of displaced meaning ... Every such incidence of misdirection is the whole book in miniature," for although Ceausescu is only mentioned in one paragraph (in the chapter "A Big House"), he is central to the story, and cannot be forgotten. "The resulting sense that anything, indeed everything – whether spoken by the characters or described by the author – is potentially dense with tacit significance means this short novel expands in the mind to occupy an emotional space far beyond its size or the seeming simplicity of its story."

## 2009 success

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/81/Herta_Muller%27s_nail_trimmers_at_the_Nobel_Museum_%2851965%29.jpg/250px-Herta_Muller%27s_nail_trimmers_at_the_Nobel_Museum_%2851965%29.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Herta_Muller's_nail_trimmers_at_the_Nobel_Museum_(51965).jpg)

Müller's nail scissors, which she used to cut words from printed materials, hanging in the [Nobel Prize Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_Museum "Nobel Prize Museum").

In 2009, Müller enjoyed the greatest international success of her career. Her novel *Atemschaukel* (published in English as *[The Hunger Angel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Hunger_Angel "The Hunger Angel")*) was nominated for the [German Book Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Book_Prize "German Book Prize") and won the [Franz Werfel Human Rights Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_Werfel_Human_Rights_Award "Franz Werfel Human Rights Award"). In this book, Müller describes the journey of a young man to a gulag in the [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union "Soviet Union"), the fate of many Germans in [Transylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transylvania "Transylvania") after World War II. It was inspired by the experience of the poet [Oskar Pastior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oskar_Pastior "Oskar Pastior"), whose memories she had made notes of, and also by what happened to her own mother.

In October 2009, the [Swedish Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swedish_Academy "Swedish Academy") announced its decision to award that year's Nobel Prize in Literature to Müller "who, with the concentration of poetry and the frankness of prose, depicts the landscape of the dispossessed." The academy compared Müller's style and her use of German as a minority language with [Franz Kafka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_Kafka "Franz Kafka") and pointed out the influence of Kafka on Müller. The award coincided with the 20th anniversary of the fall of communism. Michael Krüger, head of Müller's publishing house, said: "By giving the award to Herta Müller, who grew up in a German-speaking minority in Romania, the committee has recognized an author who refuses to let the inhumane side of life under communism be forgotten".

In 2012, Müller commented on the Nobel Prize for [Mo Yan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mo_Yan "Mo Yan") by saying that the Swedish Academy had apparently chosen an author who 'celebrates censorship'.

On 6 July 2020 a no longer existing [Twitter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twitter "Twitter") account published the fake news of Herta Müller's death, which was immediately disclaimed by her publisher.

## Influences

Although Müller has revealed little about the specific people or books that have influenced her, she has acknowledged the importance of her university studies in German and Romanian literature, and particularly of the contrast between the two languages. "The two languages", the writer says, "look differently even at plants. In Romanian, 'snowdrops' are 'little tears', in German they are 'Schneeglöckchen', which is 'little snow bells', which means we're not only speaking about different words, but about different worlds." (However here she confuses snowdrops with [lily-of-the-valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lily-of-the-valley "Lily-of-the-valley"), the latter being called 'little tears' in Romanian.) She continues, "Romanians see a falling star and say that someone has died, with the Germans you make a wish when you see the falling star." Romanian folk music is another influence: "When I first heard [Maria Tănase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maria_Tănase "Maria Tănase") she sounded incredible to me, it was for the first time that I really felt what folklore meant. Romanian folk music is connected to existence in a very meaningful way."

Müller's work was also shaped by the many experiences she shared with her ex-husband, the novelist and essayist [Richard Wagner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Wagner_(novelist) "Richard Wagner (novelist)"). Both grew up in Romania as members of the Banat Swabian ethnic group and enrolled in German and Romanian literary studies at [Timișoara University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_University_of_Timișoara "West University of Timișoara"). Upon graduating, both worked as German-language teachers, and were members of Aktionsgruppe Banat, a literary society that fought for freedom of speech.

Müller's involvement with Aktionsgruppe Banat gave her the courage to write boldly, despite the threats and trouble generated by the Romanian secret police. Although her books are fictional, they are based on real people and experiences. Her 1996 novel, *[The Land of Green Plums](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Land_of_Green_Plums "The Land of Green Plums")*, was written after the deaths of two friends, in which Müller suspected the involvement of the secret police, and one of its characters was based on a close friend from Aktionsgruppe Banat.

## Letter from Liu Xia

Herta Müller wrote the foreword for the first publication of the poetry of [Liu Xia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liu_Xia_(poet) "Liu Xia (poet)"), wife of the imprisoned [Nobel Peace Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Peace_Prize "Nobel Peace Prize") recipient [Liu Xiaobo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liu_Xiaobo "Liu Xiaobo"), in 2015. Müller also translated and read a few of Liu Xia’s poems in 2014. On 4 December 2017, a photo of a letter from Liu Xia to Herta Müller in the form of a poem, in which Liu Xia wrote that she was going mad in her solitary life, was posted on Facebook by Chinese dissident [Liao Yiwu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liao_Yiwu "Liao Yiwu").

## On 7 October 2023 massacres

At the 7 October Forum held in Stockholm on 25 and 26 May 2024, Müller commented on the "unimaginable massacre" committed by [Hamas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamas "Hamas") in its "limitless contempt for humanity" in the [7 October attacks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/7_October_attacks "7 October attacks") and described it comparable to German [extermination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extermination_(crime) "Extermination (crime)") [pogroms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pogrom "Pogrom"). Müller also criticized two progressive groups: [Berlin club-goers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Techno#German_techno "Techno") and Ivy League students. "Hatred of Jews has infiltrated Berlin's nightlife," she said. Furthermore, she stated: "After October 7, the Berlin club scene literally ducked away. Although 364 young people, ravers like them, were massacred at a techno festival, the club association did not comment on it until days later. And even that was just a dull exercise in compulsory action, because antisemitism and Hamas were not even mentioned."

In the [Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frankfurter_Allgemeine_Zeitung "Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung"), she cast doubt on the veracity of images coming out of Gaza. "Hamas controls the selection of images and orchestrates our emotions," she wrote. "Our feelings are their strongest weapon against Israel’.
