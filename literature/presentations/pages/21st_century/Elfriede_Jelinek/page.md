**Elfriede Jelinek** (German: [[ɛlˈfʁiːdə ˈjɛlinɛk]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Standard_German "Help:IPA/Standard German"); born 20 October 1946) is an Austrian playwright and novelist. She is one of the most decorated authors to write in German and was awarded the [2004 Nobel Prize in Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2004_Nobel_Prize_in_Literature "2004 Nobel Prize in Literature") for her "musical flow of voices and counter-voices in novels and plays that, with extraordinary linguistic zeal, reveal the absurdity of society's clichés and their subjugating power". She is considered to be among the most important living playwrights of the German language.

## Biography

Elfriede Jelinek was born on 20 October 1946 in [Mürzzuschlag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mürzzuschlag "Mürzzuschlag"), [Styria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steiermark "Steiermark"), Austria, the daughter of Olga Ilona (*née* Buchner), a personnel director, and Friedrich Jelinek. She was raised in [Vienna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vienna "Vienna") by her [Romanian-German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germans_of_Romania "Germans of Romania") Catholic mother and a [non-observant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_secularism "Jewish secularism") [Czech Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Jews_in_the_Czech_Republic "History of the Jews in the Czech Republic") father (whose surname *Jelínek* means "little deer" in Czech). Her mother's family was of a bourgeois background, while her father was a working-class socialist.

Her father was a [chemist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemist "Chemist"), who managed to avoid persecution during the [Second World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_World_War "Second World War") by working in strategically important industrial production. However, many of his relatives were murdered during the [Holocaust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holocaust "Holocaust"). Her mother, with whom she had a strained relationship, was from a formerly prosperous Vienna family. At the age of six, Jelinek was a patient of [Hans Asperger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Asperger "Hans Asperger"), which she later described as "a crime [...] Instead of
sending me out to play in the company of kids my age, my mother sent me into the company of severe neurotics and psychopaths." In 1995, she stated that she was "[n]ot an [Asperger autistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asperger_syndrome "Asperger syndrome"), though indeed not far off." As a child, she attended a Roman Catholic convent school in Vienna. Her mother planned a career for her as a musical "[Wunderkind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wunderkind "Wunderkind")". She was instructed in piano, organ, guitar, violin, [viola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viola "Viola"), and recorder from an early age. Later, she went on to study at the [Vienna Conservatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Music_and_Performing_Arts,_Vienna "University of Music and Performing Arts, Vienna"), where she graduated with an organist diploma; during this time, she tried to meet her mother's high expectations, while coping with her psychologically ill father. She studied [art history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_history "Art history") and theater at the [University of Vienna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Vienna "University of Vienna"). However, she had to discontinue her studies due to an [anxiety disorder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety_disorder "Anxiety disorder"), which resulted in self-isolation at her parents' house for a year. During this time, she began serious literary work as a form of therapy. After a year, she began to feel comfortable leaving the house, often with her mother. She began writing poetry at a young age. She made her literary debut with *Lisas Schatten* (*Lisa's Shadow*) in 1967, and received her first literary prize in 1969. During the 1960s, she became active politically, read a great deal, and "spent an enormous amount of time watching television".

She married Gottfried Hüngsberg on 12 June 1974.

> I was 27; he was 29. I knew enough men. Sexuality was, strangely, the only area where I emancipated myself early on. Our marriage takes place in two cities. It's a kind of *[Tale of Two Cities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tale_of_Two_Cities "Tale of Two Cities")* in the [Dickensian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Dickens "Charles Dickens") sense. I've always commuted between Vienna and [Munich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Munich "Munich"). Vienna is where I've always lived because my friends are here and because I've never wanted to leave Vienna. In the end I've been caught up here. Munich is my husband's city and so I've always traveled to and from, and that's been good for our marriage.

## Work and political engagement

Despite the author's own differentiation from Austria (due to her criticism of Austria's Nazi past), Jelinek's writing is deeply rooted in the tradition of [Austrian literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austrian_literature "Austrian literature"), showing the influence of Austrian writers such as [Ingeborg Bachmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ingeborg_Bachmann "Ingeborg Bachmann"), [Marlen Haushofer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marlen_Haushofer "Marlen Haushofer"), and [Robert Musil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Musil "Robert Musil").

Editor Friederike Eigler states that Jelinek has three major and inter-related "targets" in her writing: what she views as capitalist consumer society and its [commodification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commodification "Commodification") of all human beings and relationships, what she views as the remnants of Austria's fascist past in public and private life, and what she views as the systematic [exploitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exploitation_of_labour "Exploitation of labour") and oppression of women in a capitalist-[patriarchal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patriarchy "Patriarchy") society. Jelinek has claimed in multiple interviews that the Austrian-Jewish [satirical tradition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_humor "Jewish humor") has been a formative influence on her writing, citing [Karl Kraus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Kraus_(writer) "Karl Kraus (writer)"), [Elias Canetti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elias_Canetti "Elias Canetti"), and Jewish cabaret in particular. In an interview with [Sigrid Löffler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigrid_Löffler "Sigrid Löffler"), Jelinek claimed that her work is considered an oddity in contemporary Austria, where she claims satire is unappreciated and misunderstood, "because the Jews are dead." She has stressed her Jewish identity as the daughter of a Holocaust survivor, claiming a continuity with a Jewish-Viennese tradition that she believes has been destroyed by fascism and is dying out. In 2024, Jelinek signed an open letter denouncing the [Palestinian Campaign for the Academic and Cultural Boycott of Israel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palestinian_Campaign_for_the_Academic_and_Cultural_Boycott_of_Israel "Palestinian Campaign for the Academic and Cultural Boycott of Israel").

### Work

Jelinek's output has included radio plays, poetry, theatre texts, polemical essays, anthologies, novels, translations, screenplays, musical compositions, libretti and ballets, film and video art.
Jelinek's work is multi-faceted, and highly controversial. It has been praised and condemned by leading [literary critics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literary_criticism "Literary criticism"). In the wake of the [Fritzl case](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fritzl_incest_case "Fritzl incest case"), for example, she was accused of "executing 'hysterical' portraits of Austrian perversity". Likewise, her political activism has encountered divergent and often heated reactions. Despite the controversy surrounding her work, Jelinek has won many distinguished awards; among them are the [Georg Büchner Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georg_Büchner_Prize "Georg Büchner Prize") in 1998; the [Mülheim Dramatists Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mülheim_Dramatists_Prize "Mülheim Dramatists Prize") in 2002 and 2004; the [Franz Kafka Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_Kafka_Prize "Franz Kafka Prize") in 2004; and the [Nobel Prize in Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Literature "Nobel Prize in Literature"), also in 2004.

[Female sexuality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Female_sexuality "Female sexuality"), sexual abuse, and the battle of the sexes in general are prominent topics in her work. Texts such as *Wir sind Lockvögel, Baby!* (*We are Decoys, Baby!*), *[Die Liebhaberinnen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women_as_Lovers_(Book) "Women as Lovers (Book)")* (*Women as Lovers*) and *Die Klavierspielerin* (*[The Piano Teacher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Piano_Teacher_(Jelinek_novel) "The Piano Teacher (Jelinek novel)")*) showcase the brutality and power play inherent in human relations in a style that is, at times, ironically formal and tightly controlled. According to Jelinek, power and aggression are often the principal driving forces of [relationships](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interpersonal_relationship "Interpersonal relationship"). Likewise *Ein Sportstück* (*Sports Play*) explores the darker side of competitive sports. Her provocative novel *[Lust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lust_(Jelinek_novel) "Lust (Jelinek novel)")* contains graphic description of sexuality, aggression and abuse. It received poor reviews by many critics, some of whom likened it to pornography. But others, who noted the power of the cold descriptions of moral failures, considered it to have been misunderstood and undervalued by them.

Incidentally, on 11 October 2005, just a few days before the announcement of the 2005 Nobel laureate for literature, [Knut Ahnlund](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knut_Ahnlund "Knut Ahnlund"), a member of the [Swedish Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swedish_Academy "Swedish Academy"), which chooses the laureates for the Nobel Prize in Literature, declared that he would leave the Academy in protest over the prize the previous year being given to Jelinek, whose work Ahnlund characterized as chaotic and pornographic.

In April 2006, Jelinek spoke out to support [Peter Handke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Handke "Peter Handke"), whose play *Die Kunst des Fragens* (*The Art of Asking*) was removed from the repertoire of the [Comédie-Française](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comédie-Française "Comédie-Française") for his alleged support of [Slobodan Milošević](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slobodan_Milošević "Slobodan Milošević"). Her work is less known in English-speaking countries. However, in July and August 2012, a major English language premiere of her play *Ein Sportstück* by Just a Must theatre company brought her dramatic work to the attention of English-speaking audiences. The following year, in February and March 2013, the Women's Project in New York staged the North American premiere of *Jackie*, one of her *Princess Dramas*.

### Political engagement

Jelinek was a member of Austria's [Communist Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communist_Party_of_Austria "Communist Party of Austria") from 1974 to 1991. She became a household name during the 1990s due to her vociferous clash with [Jörg Haider](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jörg_Haider "Jörg Haider")'s [Freedom Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_Party_of_Austria "Freedom Party of Austria"). Following the 1999 [National Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Council_of_Austria "National Council of Austria") elections, and the subsequent formation of a coalition cabinet consisting of the Freedom Party and the [Austrian People's Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austrian_People's_Party "Austrian People's Party"), Jelinek became one of the new cabinet's more vocal critics.

Many foreign governments moved swiftly to ostracize Austria's administration, citing the Freedom Party's alleged nationalism and authoritarianism. The cabinet construed the sanctions against it as directed against Austria as such, and attempted to prod the nation into a national rallying (*Nationaler Schulterschluss*) behind the coalition parties.

This provoked a temporary heating of the political climate severe enough for dissidents such as Jelinek to be accused of [treason](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treason "Treason") by coalition supporters.

On 28 August 2026, Jelinek wrote on her website condemning Israel's continuing "land grabbing" of the [West Bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Bank "West Bank"), [Gaza Strip](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaza_Strip "Gaza Strip") and [Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerusalem "Jerusalem") and called the [two-state solution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-state_solution "Two-state solution") as a "lie". On her website, Jelinek also stated that she feared "[ethnic cleansing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnic_cleansing "Ethnic cleansing")" in the region because "where the land is lacking, people themselves become meaningless".

## In popular culture

Her novel *[The Piano Teacher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Piano_Teacher_(Jelinek_novel) "The Piano Teacher (Jelinek novel)")* was the basis for the [2001 film of the same title](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Piano_Teacher_(film) "The Piano Teacher (film)") by Austrian director [Michael Haneke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Haneke "Michael Haneke"), starring [Isabelle Huppert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isabelle_Huppert "Isabelle Huppert") as the protagonist.

In 2022, a documentary about Jelinek was created by Claudia Müller, *Elfriede Jelinek – Language Unleashed* (German: *Elfriede Jelinek – Die Sprache von der Leine lassen*).
