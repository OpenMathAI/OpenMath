**Doris May Lessing** ([née](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_name#Maiden_and_married_names "Birth name") **Tayler**; 22 October 1919 – 17 November 2013) was a British novelist – sometimes identified as Rhodesian early in her career – and winner of the [Nobel Prize in Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Literature "Nobel Prize in Literature") in 2007. Lessing was born to British parents in [Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran "Iran"), where she lived until she was 6 in 1925. Her family then moved to [Southern Rhodesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Rhodesia "Southern Rhodesia") (now [Zimbabwe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zimbabwe "Zimbabwe")), where she remained until moving to London, England, in 1949. Her novels include *[The Grass Is Singing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Grass_Is_Singing "The Grass Is Singing")* (1950), the sequence of five novels collectively called *[Children of Violence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Children_of_Violence "Children of Violence")* (1952–1969), *[The Golden Notebook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Golden_Notebook "The Golden Notebook")* (1962), *[The Good Terrorist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Good_Terrorist "The Good Terrorist")* (1985), and five novels collectively known as *[Canopus in Argos: Archives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canopus_in_Argos "Canopus in Argos")* (1979–1983).

Lessing was awarded the [2007 Nobel Prize in Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2007_Nobel_Prize_in_Literature "2007 Nobel Prize in Literature"). In awarding the prize, the [Swedish Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swedish_Academy "Swedish Academy") described her as "that epicist of the female experience, who with scepticism, fire and visionary power has subjected a divided civilisation to scrutiny". Lessing was the oldest person ever to receive the Nobel Prize in Literature, at age 87.

In 2001, Lessing was awarded the [David Cohen Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Cohen_Prize "David Cohen Prize") for a lifetime's achievement in [British literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_literature "British literature"). In 2008, *[The Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Times "The Times")* ranked her fifth on a list of "The 50 greatest British writers since 1945".

## Life

### Early life

Lessing was born Doris May Tayler in [Kermanshah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kermanshah "Kermanshah"), [Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran "Iran"), on 22 October 1919, to Captain Alfred Tayler and Emily Maude Tayler (née McVeagh), both British subjects. Her father, who had lost a leg during his service in [World War I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I "World War I"), met his future wife, a nurse, at the [Royal Free Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Free_Hospital "Royal Free Hospital") in London where he was recovering from his [amputation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amputation "Amputation"). The couple moved to Iran, for Alfred to take a job as a clerk for the [Imperial Bank of Persia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_Bank_of_Persia "Imperial Bank of Persia").

In 1925, the family moved to the British colony of [Southern Rhodesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Rhodesia "Southern Rhodesia") (now Zimbabwe) to farm maize and other crops on about 1,000 acres (400 ha) of bush that Alfred bought. In the rough environment, his wife Emily aspired to lead an [Edwardian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edwardian "Edwardian") lifestyle. It might have been possible had the family been wealthy; in reality, they were short of money and the farm delivered very little income.

As a girl, Lessing was educated first at the [Dominican Convent High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominican_Convent_High_School,_Harare "Dominican Convent High School, Harare"), a Roman Catholic [convent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convent "Convent") [all-girls school](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single-sex_school "Single-sex school") in the Southern Rhodesian capital of Salisbury (now [Harare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harare "Harare")). Then followed a year at [Girls High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Girls_High_School,_Harare "Girls High School, Harare") in Salisbury. She left school at age 13 and was self-educated from then on. She left home at 15 and worked as a [nursemaid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nursemaid "Nursemaid"). She started reading material that her employer gave her on politics and sociology and began writing around this time.

In 1937, Lessing moved to [Salisbury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harare "Harare") to work as a [telephone operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Switchboard_operator "Switchboard operator"), and she soon married her first husband, civil servant Frank Wisdom, with whom she had two children (John, 1940–1992, and Jean, born in 1941), before the marriage ended in 1943. Lessing left the family home in 1943, leaving the two children with their father.

### Move to London; political views

After the divorce, Lessing's interest was drawn to the community around the [Left Book Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Left_Book_Club "Left Book Club"), an organisation she had joined the year before. It was here that she met her future second husband, [Gottfried Lessing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gottfried_Lessing "Gottfried Lessing"). They married shortly after she joined the group, and had a child together (Peter, 1946–2013), before they divorced in 1949. She did not marry again. Lessing also had a love affair with RAF serviceman John Whitehorn (brother of journalist [Katharine Whitehorn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Katharine_Whitehorn "Katharine Whitehorn")), who was stationed in Southern Rhodesia, and wrote him ninety letters between 1943 and 1949.

Lessing moved to London in 1949 with her younger son, Peter, to pursue her writing career and socialist beliefs, but left the two older children with their father, Frank Wisdom. She later said that at the time she saw no choice: "For a long time I felt I had done a very brave thing. There is nothing more boring for an intelligent woman than to spend endless amounts of time with small children. I felt I wasn't the best person to bring them up. I would have ended up an alcoholic or a frustrated intellectual like my mother."

As well as [campaigning against nuclear arms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_disarmament "Nuclear disarmament"), she was an active opponent of [apartheid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apartheid "Apartheid"), which led her to being banned from South Africa and Rhodesia in 1956 for many years. In the same year, following the [Soviet invasion of Hungary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungarian_Revolution_of_1956 "Hungarian Revolution of 1956"), she left the [Communist Party of Great Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communist_Party_of_Great_Britain "Communist Party of Great Britain"). In the 1980s, when Lessing was vocal in her opposition to Soviet actions in Afghanistan, she gave her views on feminism, communism and science fiction in an interview with *[The New York Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_York_Times "The New York Times")*.

On 21 August 2015, a five-volume secret file on Lessing, built up by both [MI5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MI5 "MI5") and [MI6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MI6 "MI6"), was made public and placed in [The National Archives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_National_Archives_(United_Kingdom) "The National Archives (United Kingdom)"). The file, which contains documents that are redacted in parts, shows Lessing was under surveillance by MI5 and MI6 for around twenty years, from the early 1940s onwards. Her associations with communist organisations and political activism were reported to be the reasons for the surveillance of Lessing.

Disaffected, and turning away from Marxist political philosophy, Lessing became increasingly absorbed with mystical and spiritual matters, devoting herself especially to the [Sufi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sufism "Sufism") tradition.

### Literary career

At the age of fifteen, Lessing began to sell her stories to magazines. Her first novel, *[The Grass Is Singing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Grass_Is_Singing "The Grass Is Singing")*, was published in 1950. The work that gained her international attention, *[The Golden Notebook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Golden_Notebook "The Golden Notebook")*, was published in 1962. By the time of her death, she had published more than 50 novels, some under a pseudonym.

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/8a/DorisLessing1984.jpg/250px-DorisLessing1984.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:DorisLessing1984.jpg)

Lessing in 1984

In 1982, Lessing wrote two novels under the literary pseudonym Jane Somers to show the difficulty new authors face in trying to get their work printed. The novels were rejected by Lessing's UK publisher but later accepted by another English publisher, [Michael Joseph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Joseph_(publisher) "Michael Joseph (publisher)"), and in the US by [Alfred A. Knopf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_A._Knopf "Alfred A. Knopf"). *[The Diary of a Good Neighbour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Diaries_of_Jane_Somers "The Diaries of Jane Somers")* was published in Britain and the US in 1983 and *[If the Old Could](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Diaries_of_Jane_Somers "The Diaries of Jane Somers")* in both countries in 1984, both as written by Jane Somers. In 1984 both novels were republished in both countries ([Viking Books](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viking_Books "Viking Books") publishing in the US), this time under one cover, with the title *[The Diaries of Jane Somers: The Diary of a Good Neighbour and If the Old Could](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Diaries_of_Jane_Somers "The Diaries of Jane Somers")*, listing Doris Lessing as author.

Lessing declined a [damehood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dame_(title) "Dame (title)") (DBE) in 1992 as an honour linked to a non-existent Empire; she had previously declined appointment as an OBE in 1977. Later she accepted appointment as a [Member of the Order of the Companions of Honour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Member_of_the_Order_of_the_Companions_of_Honour "Member of the Order of the Companions of Honour") at the end of 1999 for "conspicuous national service". She was also made a Companion of Literature by the [Royal Society of Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society_of_Literature "Royal Society of Literature").

In 2007, Lessing was awarded the [Nobel Prize in Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Literature "Nobel Prize in Literature"). She received the prize at the age of 88 years 52 days, making her the oldest winner of the literature prize at the time of the award and the third-oldest Nobel laureate in any category (after [Leonid Hurwicz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonid_Hurwicz "Leonid Hurwicz") and [Raymond Davis Jr.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raymond_Davis_Jr. "Raymond Davis Jr.")). Returning from the grocery store, Lessing was informed by a journalist of her win; she replied "Oh Christ" and continued to bring in her groceries, saying "One can't get more excited than one gets, you know?". While dismissing gathered reporters, she asked what they wanted to hear, saying "I'm sure you'd like some uplifting remarks of some kind." She was the eleventh woman to be awarded the Nobel Prize for Literature by the [Swedish Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swedish_Academy "Swedish Academy") in its 106-year history. In 2017, her Nobel medal was put up for auction. Previously, only one Nobel medal for literature had been sold at auction, for [André Gide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/André_Gide "André Gide") in 2016.

### Illness and death

During the late 1990s, Lessing had a stroke, which stopped her from travelling during her later years. She was still able to attend the theatre and opera. She began to focus her mind on death, for example asking herself if she would have time to finish a new book. She died on 17 November 2013, aged 94, at her home in [West Hampstead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Hampstead "West Hampstead"), London, of kidney failure, [sepsis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sepsis "Sepsis") and a chest infection, predeceased by her two sons, but was survived by her daughter, Jean, who lives in South Africa.

She was remembered with a [humanist funeral service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humanist_celebrant "Humanist celebrant").

## Fiction

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/f/f8/Idries_Shah.gif?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail_unscaled)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Idries_Shah.gif)

[Idries Shah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idries_Shah "Idries Shah"), who introduced Lessing to [Sufism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sufism "Sufism")

Lessing's fiction is commonly divided into three distinct phases.

During her Communist phase (1944–1956), she wrote radically about social issues, a theme to which she returned in *[The Good Terrorist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Good_Terrorist "The Good Terrorist")* (1985). Doris Lessing's first novel, *[The Grass Is Singing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Grass_Is_Singing "The Grass Is Singing")*, as well as the short stories later collected in *African Stories*, are set in [Southern Rhodesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Rhodesia "Southern Rhodesia") (today [Zimbabwe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zimbabwe "Zimbabwe")) where she was then living.

This was followed by a [psychological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychology "Psychology") phase from 1956 to 1969, including the *Golden Notebook* and the "Children of Violence" quintet.

Third came the [Sufi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sufism "Sufism") phase, explored in her 70s work, and in the *[Canopus in Argos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canopus_in_Argos "Canopus in Argos")* sequence of science fiction novels and novellas (or as she preferred to call it "space fiction", a description also preferred by [C.S. Lewis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C.S._Lewis "C.S. Lewis") for [his works of science fiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Space_Trilogy "The Space Trilogy")) .

Lessing's *Canopus* sequence received a mixed reception from mainstream [literary critics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literary_critic "Literary critic"). [John Leonard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Leonard_(American_critic) "John Leonard (American critic)") praised her 1980 novel *[The Marriages Between Zones Three, Four and Five](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Marriages_Between_Zones_Three,_Four_and_Five "The Marriages Between Zones Three, Four and Five")* in *The New York Times*, but in 1982 he wrote in reference to *[The Making of the Representative for Planet 8](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Making_of_the_Representative_for_Planet_8_(novel) "The Making of the Representative for Planet 8 (novel)")* that "[o]ne of the many sins for which the 20th century will be held accountable is that it has discouraged Mrs. Lessing... She now propagandises on behalf of our insignificance in the cosmic razzmatazz", to which Lessing replied: "What they didn't realise was that in science fiction is some of the best [social fiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_fiction "Social fiction") of our time. I also admire the classic sort of science fiction, like *[Blood Music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blood_Music_(novel) "Blood Music (novel)")*, by [Greg Bear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greg_Bear "Greg Bear"). He's a great writer." She attended [the 1987](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/45th_World_Science_Fiction_Convention "45th World Science Fiction Convention") [World Science Fiction Convention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Science_Fiction_Convention "World Science Fiction Convention") as its Writer Guest of Honor. Here she made a speech in which she described her [dystopian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dystopian "Dystopian") novel *[Memoirs of a Survivor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memoirs_of_a_Survivor "Memoirs of a Survivor")* as "an attempt at an autobiography".

The *Canopus in Argos* novels present an advanced interstellar society's efforts to accelerate the evolution of other worlds, including Earth. Using [Sufi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sufi "Sufi") concepts, to which Lessing had been introduced in the mid-1960s by her "good friend and teacher" [Idries Shah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idries_Shah "Idries Shah"), the series of novels also uses an approach similar to that employed by the early 20th-century mystic [G. I. Gurdjieff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G._I._Gurdjieff "G. I. Gurdjieff") in his work *[All and Everything](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All_and_Everything "All and Everything")*. Earlier works of "inner space" fiction like *[Briefing for a Descent into Hell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Briefing_for_a_Descent_into_Hell "Briefing for a Descent into Hell")* (1971) and *[Memoirs of a Survivor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memoirs_of_a_Survivor "Memoirs of a Survivor")* (1974) also connect to this theme. Lessing's interest had turned to Sufism after coming to the realisation that Marxism ignored spiritual matters, leaving her disillusioned.

Lessing's novel *[The Golden Notebook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Golden_Notebook "The Golden Notebook")* is considered a feminist classic by some scholars, but notably not by the author herself, who later wrote that its theme of mental breakdowns as a means of healing and freeing one's self from illusions had been overlooked by critics. She also regretted that critics failed to appreciate the exceptional structure of the novel. She explained in *Walking in the Shade* that she modelled Molly partly on her good friend [Joan Rodker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joan_Rodker "Joan Rodker"), the daughter of the modernist poet and publisher [John Rodker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Rodker "John Rodker").

Lessing did not like being pigeonholed as a feminist author. When asked why, she explained:

> What the feminists want of me is something they haven't examined because it comes from religion. They want me to bear witness. What they would really like me to say is, 'Ha, sisters, I stand with you side by side in your struggle toward the golden dawn where all those beastly men are no more.' Do they really want people to make oversimplified statements about men and women? In fact, they do. I've come with great regret to this conclusion.

— Doris Lessing, *[The New York Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_York_Times "The New York Times")*, 25 July 1982

## Doris Lessing Society

The Doris Lessing Society is dedicated to supporting the scholarly study of Lessing's work. The formal structure of the Society dates from January 1977, when the first issue of the *Doris Lessing Newsletter* was published. In 2002, the newsletter became the academic journal *Doris Lessing Studies*. The Society also organises panels at the [Modern Languages Association (MLA)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_Language_Association "Modern Language Association") annual Conventions and has held two international conferences in [New Orleans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Orleans "New Orleans") in 2004 and [Leeds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leeds "Leeds") in 2007.

## Archives

Lessing's literary archive is held by the [Harry Ransom Humanities Research Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harry_Ransom_Humanities_Research_Center "Harry Ransom Humanities Research Center"), at the [University of Texas at Austin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Texas_at_Austin "University of Texas at Austin"). The 76 archival boxes of Lessing's materials at the Ransom Center contain nearly all of her extant manuscripts and typescripts up to 2008. Original material for Lessing's early books is assumed not to exist because she kept none of her early manuscripts. The [McFarlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_M._McFarlin "Robert M. McFarlin") Library at the [University of Tulsa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Tulsa "University of Tulsa") holds a smaller collection.

The [University of East Anglia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_East_Anglia "University of East Anglia")'s British Archive for Contemporary Writing holds Lessing's personal archive: a vast collection of professional and personal correspondence, including the Whitehorn letters, a collection of love letters from the 1940s, written when Lessing was still living in Southern Rhodesia (now Zimbabwe). The collection also includes forty years of personal diaries. Some of the archive remained embargoed during the writing of Lessing's official [biography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biography "Biography").
