**Sir Kazuo Ishiguro** ([Japanese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_language "Japanese language"): 石黒 一雄, [Hepburn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hepburn_romanization "Hepburn romanization"): *Ishiguro Kazuo*; [/kæˈzuː.oʊ ˌɪʃɪˈɡuroʊ, ˈkæzu.oʊ/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English"); born 8 November 1954) is a Japanese-British novelist, screenwriter, musician, and short-story writer. He is one of the most critically acclaimed contemporary fiction authors writing in English, having been awarded several major literary prizes, including the [2017 Nobel Prize in Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2017_Nobel_Prize_in_Literature "2017 Nobel Prize in Literature"). In its citation, the [Swedish Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swedish_Academy "Swedish Academy") described Ishiguro as a writer "who, in novels of great emotional force, has uncovered the abyss beneath our illusory sense of connection with the world".

Ishiguro was born in [Nagasaki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nagasaki "Nagasaki"), Japan, and moved to Britain in 1960 with his parents when he was five. His first two novels, *[A Pale View of Hills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Pale_View_of_Hills "A Pale View of Hills")* and *[An Artist of the Floating World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/An_Artist_of_the_Floating_World "An Artist of the Floating World")*, were noted for their explorations of Japanese identity and their mournful tone. He has since explored themes more typically associated with [science fiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_fiction "Science fiction") and [historical fiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historical_fiction "Historical fiction"), while continuing a focus on the outsider's perspective, the search for an unattainable truth, and the sense of recent history as a spider's web in which humans vainly struggle.

He has been nominated for the [Booker Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Booker_Prize "Booker Prize") four times, winning in 1989 for *[The Remains of the Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Remains_of_the_Day "The Remains of the Day")*, which was adapted into a [film of the same name](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Remains_of_the_Day_(film) "The Remains of the Day (film)") in 1993. [Salman Rushdie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salman_Rushdie "Salman Rushdie") praised the novel as Ishiguro's masterpiece, in which he "turned away from the Japanese settings of his first two novels and revealed that his sensibility was not rooted in any one place, but capable of travel and metamorphosis".

*[Time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_(magazine) "Time (magazine)")* named Ishiguro's *[Never Let Me Go](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Never_Let_Me_Go_(novel) "Never Let Me Go (novel)")* as the best novel of 2005 and one of the 100 best English-language novels published between 1923 and 2005. He was nominated for the [Academy Award for Best Adapted Screenplay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academy_Award_for_Best_Adapted_Screenplay "Academy Award for Best Adapted Screenplay") for the 2022 film *[Living](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Living_(2022_film) "Living (2022 film)")*.

## Early life and education

Ishiguro was born in Nagasaki, Japan, on 8 November 1954, the son of [Shizuo Ishiguro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shizuo_Ishiguro "Shizuo Ishiguro"), a [physical oceanographer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_oceanography "Physical oceanography"), and his wife, Shizuko. In 1960, Ishiguro moved with his family to [Guildford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guildford "Guildford"), [Surrey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surrey "Surrey"), as his father was invited for research at the National Institute of Oceanography (now the [National Oceanography Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Oceanography_Centre,_Southampton "National Oceanography Centre, Southampton")). He did not return to visit Japan until 1989, nearly 30 years later, when he was a participant in the [Japan Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan_Foundation "Japan Foundation") Short-Term Visitors' Programme.

In an interview with [Kenzaburō Ōe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenzaburō_Ōe "Kenzaburō Ōe"), Ishiguro stated that the Japanese settings of his first two novels were imaginary: "I grew up with a very strong image in my head of this other country, a very important other country to which I had a strong emotional tie … In England I was all the time building up this picture in my head, an imaginary Japan."

Ishiguro, who has been described as a [British Asian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Asian "British Asian") author, explained in a [BBC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC "BBC") interview how growing up in a [Japanese family in the UK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_in_the_United_Kingdom "Japanese in the United Kingdom") was crucial to his writing, enabling him to see things from a different perspective from that of many of his English peers.

He attended Stoughton Primary School and then [Woking County Grammar School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woking_College#History "Woking College") in [Surrey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surrey "Surrey"). Ishiguro sang solos as a [choirboy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Choirboy "Choirboy") with his church and school choirs. He also enjoyed music as a teenager, listening to the likes of [Leonard Cohen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonard_Cohen "Leonard Cohen"), [Joni Mitchell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joni_Mitchell "Joni Mitchell"), and particularly [Bob Dylan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bob_Dylan "Bob Dylan"). Ishiguro began learning guitar and writing songs, initially aiming to become a professional songwriter. He says that "for me there's always been big overlap between fiction and song. My style as a novelist comes substantially from what I learnt writing songs. The intimate, first-person quality of a singer performing to an audience, for instance, carried over for me into novels. As did the need to approach meaning subtly, sometimes by nudging it into the spaces between the lines." After finishing school in 1973, he took a [gap year](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gap_year "Gap year") and traveled through the United States and Canada, writing a journal and sending [demo tapes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demo_tape "Demo tape") to record companies. He also worked as a grouse beater, a practice of [driven grouse shooting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Driven_grouse_shooting "Driven grouse shooting"), at [Balmoral Castle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balmoral_Castle "Balmoral Castle"). Ishiguro later reflected on his ephemeral songwriting career, saying, "I used to see myself as some sort of musician type but there came a point when I thought: actually, this isn't me at all. I'm much less glamorous. I'm one of these people with corduroy jackets with elbow patches. It was a real comedown."

In 1974, he began studies at the [University of Kent at Canterbury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Kent "University of Kent"), graduating in 1978 with a [Bachelor of Arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_Arts "Bachelor of Arts") (honours) in [English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_studies "English studies") and [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy "Philosophy"). After spending a year writing fiction, he resumed his studies at the [University of East Anglia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_East_Anglia "University of East Anglia") where he studied with [Malcolm Bradbury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malcolm_Bradbury "Malcolm Bradbury") and [Angela Carter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angela_Carter "Angela Carter") on the [UEA Creative Writing Course](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UEA_Creative_Writing_Course "UEA Creative Writing Course"), gaining the degree of [Master of Arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master_of_Arts "Master of Arts") in 1980. His thesis became his first novel, *[A Pale View of Hills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Pale_View_of_Hills "A Pale View of Hills")*, published in 1982.

He gained [British citizenship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_nationality_law "British nationality law") in 1983.

## Career

### 1982–1994: Literary beginnings and breakthrough

Ishiguro set his first two novels in Japan; however, in several interviews, he said that he has little familiarity with Japanese writing and that his works bear little resemblance to [Japanese fiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_literature "Japanese literature"). *[An Artist of the Floating World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/An_Artist_of_the_Floating_World "An Artist of the Floating World")* (1986) is set in an unnamed Japanese city during the [Allied Occupation of Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occupation_of_Japan "Occupation of Japan") following [the nation's surrender](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surrender_of_Japan "Surrender of Japan") at the end of [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II"). The narrator is forced to come to terms with his part in [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan_during_World_War_II "Japan during World War II"). He is criticized by the [younger generation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-occupation_Japan "Post-occupation Japan"), who hold him responsible for [Japan's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empire_of_Japan "Empire of Japan") misguided foreign policy. As a result, he is compelled to reevaluate his beliefs in light of the [modern ideals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_world "Modern world") represented by his grandson. Ishiguro said of his choice of time period, "I tend to be attracted to pre-war and postwar settings because I'm interested in this business of values and ideals being tested, and people having to face up to the notion that their ideals weren't quite what they thought they were before the test came."

In an interview in 1989, when discussing his Japanese heritage and its influence on his upbringing, he stated, "I'm not entirely like English people because I've been brought up by Japanese parents in a Japanese-speaking home. My parents (...) felt responsible for keeping me in touch with [Japanese values](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_values "Japanese values"). I do have a distinct background. I think differently, my perspectives are slightly different." In a 1990 interview, Ishiguro said, "If I wrote under a pseudonym and got somebody else to pose for my jacket photographs, I'm sure nobody would think of saying, 'This guy reminds me of that Japanese writer.'" Although some Japanese writers have had a distant influence on his writing—[Jun'ichirō Tanizaki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jun'ichirō_Tanizaki "Jun'ichirō Tanizaki") is the one he most frequently cites—Ishiguro has said that Japanese films, especially those of [Yasujirō Ozu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yasujirō_Ozu "Yasujirō Ozu") and [Mikio Naruse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikio_Naruse "Mikio Naruse"), have been a more significant influence.

In 1989 he released his book *[The Remains of the Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Remains_of_the_Day "The Remains of the Day")*, set in the large [country house](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_country_house "English country house") of an [English lord](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peerage_of_England "Peerage of England") in the period surrounding [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II"). The book received widespread acclaim as well as the [Booker Prize for Fiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Booker_Prize "Booker Prize"). The novel was adapted by [Merchant Ivory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merchant_Ivory "Merchant Ivory") and made into a [1993 film of the same name](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Remains_of_the_Day_(film) "The Remains of the Day (film)") starring [Anthony Hopkins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthony_Hopkins "Anthony Hopkins") and [Emma Thompson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emma_Thompson "Emma Thompson").

### 1995–2018: Established career and acclaim

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/41/Cast_of_Never_Let_Me_Go_%40_BFI_Film_Festival.jpg/250px-Cast_of_Never_Let_Me_Go_%40_BFI_Film_Festival.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Cast_of_Never_Let_Me_Go_@_BFI_Film_Festival.jpg)

Ishiguro (front) with the cast of the [*Never Let Me Go*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Never_Let_Me_Go_(2010_film) "Never Let Me Go (2010 film)") film in 2010

His fourth novel, *[The Unconsoled](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Unconsoled "The Unconsoled")* (1995), takes place in an unnamed [Central European](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Europe "Central Europe") city. It received the [Cheltenham Prize for Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cheltenham_Prize_for_Literature "Cheltenham Prize for Literature"). A 2006 poll of various literary critics voted the novel as the third "best British, Irish, or Commonwealth novel from 1980 to 2005", tied with [Anthony Burgess](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthony_Burgess "Anthony Burgess")'s *[Earthly Powers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earthly_Powers "Earthly Powers")*, [Salman Rushdie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salman_Rushdie "Salman Rushdie")'s *[Midnight's Children](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midnight's_Children "Midnight's Children")*, [Ian McEwan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ian_McEwan "Ian McEwan")'s *[Atonement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atonement_(novel) "Atonement (novel)")*, and [Penelope Fitzgerald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penelope_Fitzgerald "Penelope Fitzgerald")'s *[The Blue Flower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Blue_Flower "The Blue Flower")*.

Some of Ishiguro's novels are set in the past. *[Never Let Me Go](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Never_Let_Me_Go_(novel) "Never Let Me Go (novel)")* (2005) has [science fiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_fiction "Science fiction") qualities and a [futuristic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Futuristic "Futuristic") tone; however, it is set in the 1980s and 1990s, and takes place in a [parallel world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallel_universe_(fiction) "Parallel universe (fiction)") very similar to ours. *[Time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_(magazine) "Time (magazine)")* magazine named it the best novel of 2005 and included the novel in its "100 Best English-language novels published since 1923". The novel was adapted into the [2010 film of the same name](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Never_Let_Me_Go_(2010_film) "Never Let Me Go (2010 film)") starring [Keira Knightley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keira_Knightley "Keira Knightley"), [Andrew Garfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Garfield "Andrew Garfield"), and [Carey Mulligan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carey_Mulligan "Carey Mulligan"). With the exception of *[The Buried Giant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Buried_Giant "The Buried Giant")* (2015), Ishiguro's novels are written in the [first-person narrative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First-person_narrative "First-person narrative") style.

In 2017, Ishiguro was awarded the [Nobel Prize in Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Literature "Nobel Prize in Literature") (see below). Ishiguro was appointed [Knight Bachelor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knight_Bachelor "Knight Bachelor") for services to literature in the [2018 Birthday Honours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2018_Birthday_Honours "2018 Birthday Honours") and was later appointed to the [Order of the Companions of Honour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_the_Companions_of_Honour "Order of the Companions of Honour") in the [2024 New Year Honours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_New_Year_Honours "2024 New Year Honours").

### Nobel Prize in Literature

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/fe/Kazuo_Ishiguro_in_Stockholm_2017_07.jpg/250px-Kazuo_Ishiguro_in_Stockholm_2017_07.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Kazuo_Ishiguro_in_Stockholm_2017_07.jpg)

Kazuo Ishiguro at the press conference in Stockholm 6 December 2017

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/3a/Kazuo_Ishiguro_in_Stockholm_2017_06.jpg/250px-Kazuo_Ishiguro_in_Stockholm_2017_06.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Kazuo_Ishiguro_in_Stockholm_2017_06.jpg)

Kazuo Ishiguro with the permanent secretary of the Swedish Academy, [Sara Danius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sara_Danius "Sara Danius")

In 2017, Ishiguro was awarded the [Nobel Prize in Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Literature "Nobel Prize in Literature") with the motivation "in novels of great emotional force, [he] has uncovered the abyss beneath our illusory sense of connection with the world". Ishiguro was seen as a surprise choice by many observers, but the choice was generally well received. "The [Swedish Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swedish_Academy "Swedish Academy") has made some dubious – and [last year](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2016_Nobel_Prize_in_Literature "2016 Nobel Prize in Literature") attention-seeking – decisions in recent years, but this year its 18 voters have got it right", [John Mullan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Mullan_(academic) "John Mullan (academic)"), professor of English at [University College London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_College_London "University College London"), wrote in *[The Guardian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Guardian "The Guardian")*, "It is because he writes for all times, using such carefully controlled means, rigorous yet utterly original, that Ishiguro is such a worthy winner."

[Salman Rushdie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salman_Rushdie "Salman Rushdie") said “Many congratulations to my old friend Ish, whose work I’ve loved and admired ever since I first read *[A Pale View of Hills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Pale_View_of_Hills "A Pale View of Hills")*". In response to receiving the award, Ishiguro stated:

> It's a magnificent honour, mainly because it means that I'm in the footsteps of the greatest authors that have lived, so that's a terrific commendation. The world is in a very uncertain moment and I would hope all the Nobel Prizes would be a force for something positive in the world as it is at the moment. I'll be deeply moved if I could in some way be part of some sort of climate this year in contributing to some sort of positive atmosphere at a very uncertain time.

At the award ceremony in Stockholm on 10 December 2017, [Sara Danius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sara_Danius "Sara Danius"), permanent secretary of the [Swedish Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swedish_Academy "Swedish Academy"), compared his writing to 19th century writers such as [Jane Austen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jane_Austen "Jane Austen"), [Charles Dickens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Dickens "Charles Dickens"), [Charlotte Brontë](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlotte_Brontë "Charlotte Brontë") and [George Eliot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Eliot "George Eliot") mixed with influences from [Franz Kafka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_Kafka "Franz Kafka"), and said:

> Ishiguro too is an innovator, always taking risks. With every new book he investigates a new genre-mix, with elements of the detective story, science fiction, myth ... The window of the novel has always been wide. Ishiguro has widened it even more."

### 2021–present: *Klara and the Sun* and *Living*

Ishiguro's eighth novel, *[Klara and the Sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klara_and_the_Sun "Klara and the Sun")*, was published by [Faber and Faber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faber_and_Faber "Faber and Faber") on 2 March 2021. Rumaan Alam of *[The New Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_Republic "The New Republic")* wrote it is "more simple than it seems, less novel than parable". It was longlisted for the 2021 [Booker Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Booker_Prize "Booker Prize"). In this novel, narrated from the perspective of an "Artificial Friend" named Klara, he discusses subjects such as the dangers of [technological advancement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dangers_of_artificial_intelligence "Dangers of artificial intelligence"), the future of our world, and the meaning of being human that he also broached in his earlier books.

Ishiguro adapted the screenplay for the 2022 British film *[Living](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Living_(2022_film) "Living (2022 film)")*, directed by [Oliver Hermanus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oliver_Hermanus "Oliver Hermanus") and starring [Bill Nighy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bill_Nighy "Bill Nighy"), from the 1952 Japanese film *[Ikiru](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ikiru "Ikiru")* directed by [Akira Kurosawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akira_Kurosawa "Akira Kurosawa"). In 2023, *Living* was nominated for an [Academy Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academy_Award "Academy Award") in the Best Adapted Screenplay category.

## Musical work

Ishiguro has co-written several songs for the jazz singer [Stacey Kent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stacey_Kent "Stacey Kent") with Kent's husband, saxophonist [Jim Tomlinson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jim_Tomlinson "Jim Tomlinson"). Ishiguro contributed lyrics to Kent's 2007 Grammy-nominated album *[Breakfast on the Morning Tram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breakfast_on_the_Morning_Tram "Breakfast on the Morning Tram")*, including its title track, her 2011 album, *Dreamer in Concert*, her 2013 album *[The Changing Lights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Changing_Lights "The Changing Lights")*, and her 2017 album, *I Know I Dream*. Ishiguro also wrote the liner notes to Kent's 2002 album *In Love Again*. Ishiguro first met Kent after he chose her recording of "[They Can't Take That Away from Me](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/They_Can't_Take_That_Away_from_Me "They Can't Take That Away from Me")" as one of his *[Desert Island Discs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desert_Island_Discs "Desert Island Discs")* in 2002 and Kent subsequently asked him to write for her.

Ishiguro has said of his lyric writing that "with an intimate, confiding, first-person song, the meaning must not be self-sufficient on the page. It has to be oblique, sometimes you have to read between the lines" and that this realisation has had an "enormous influence" on his fiction writing.

In March 2024, [Faber & Faber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faber_&_Faber "Faber & Faber") published Ishiguro's book *[The Summer We Crossed Europe in the Rain: Lyrics for Stacey Kent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Summer_We_Crossed_Europe_in_the_Rain:_Lyrics_for_Stacey_Kent?action=edit&redlink=1 "The Summer We Crossed Europe in the Rain: Lyrics for Stacey Kent")*, a collection of 16 of his lyrics for Kent, with illustrations by Italian-French artist [Bianca Bagnarelli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bianca_Bagnarelli "Bianca Bagnarelli").

## Influences

Ishiguro counts [Dostoyevsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fyodor_Dostoyevsky "Fyodor Dostoyevsky") and [Proust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcel_Proust "Marcel Proust") among his influences. His works have also been compared to [Salman Rushdie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salman_Rushdie "Salman Rushdie"), [Jane Austen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jane_Austen "Jane Austen"), and [Henry James](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_James "Henry James"), though Ishiguro himself rejects these comparisons. When asked who his favourite novelist is, he says "[Charlotte Brontë](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlotte_Brontë "Charlotte Brontë")'s recently edged out Dostoevsky ... I owe my career, and a lot else besides, to *[Jane Eyre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jane_Eyre "Jane Eyre")* and *[Villette](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Villette_(novel) "Villette (novel)")*."

## Personal life

Ishiguro has been married to Lorna MacDougall, a social worker, since 1986. They met at the West London [Cyreneans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simon_Community "Simon Community") homelessness charity in [Notting Hill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Notting_Hill "Notting Hill"), where Ishiguro was working as a residential resettlement worker. The couple live in [Golders Green](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golders_Green "Golders Green"), North London. Their daughter [Naomi Ishiguro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naomi_Ishiguro "Naomi Ishiguro") is also an author, and has published two books, including the short story collection *[Escape Routes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Escape_Routes_(book) "Escape Routes (book)")*.

He describes himself as a "serious [cinephile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cinephile "Cinephile")" and "great admirer of [Bob Dylan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bob_Dylan "Bob Dylan")". On *[Desert Island Discs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desert_Island_Discs "Desert Island Discs")*, he chose Dylan's "[Tryin' to Get to Heaven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tryin'_to_Get_to_Heaven "Tryin' to Get to Heaven")" as his favourite song. His book choice was the *Collected Short Stories of [Anton Chekhov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anton_Chekhov "Anton Chekhov").*

Ishiguro has criticized [Donald Trump](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Trump "Donald Trump"), stating: "In the post-truth Trump era, there’s this relentless attack on accredited news media. It’s not just Trump: it’s a general atmosphere that whatever the evidence, if you don’t like it, you can just claim some alternative emotional truth for yourself. The whole status of what might be true has got very blurred lately." He is also critical of the rise of [artificial intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence "Artificial intelligence"), claiming that it "will become very good at manipulating emotions. I think we’re on the verge of that. At the moment we’re just thinking of AI crunching data or something. But very soon, AI will be able to figure out how you create certain kinds of emotions in people – anger, sadness, laughter.”

### Literary awards

- 1982: [Winifred Holtby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winifred_Holtby "Winifred Holtby") Memorial Prize for *[A Pale View of Hills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Pale_View_of_Hills "A Pale View of Hills")*
- 1986: [Whitbread Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whitbread_Prize "Whitbread Prize") for *[An Artist of the Floating World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/An_Artist_of_the_Floating_World "An Artist of the Floating World")*
- 1989: [Booker Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Booker_Prize "Booker Prize") for *The Remains of the Day*
- 1989: Elected [Fellow of the Royal Society of Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society_of_Literature "Royal Society of Literature")
- 2017: [Nobel Prize in Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2017_Nobel_Prize_in_Literature "2017 Nobel Prize in Literature")
- 2017: [American Academy of Achievement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Academy_of_Achievement "American Academy of Achievement")'s Golden Plate Award
- 2019: [Bodley Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bodley_Medal "Bodley Medal")

Except for *A Pale View of Hills* and *The Buried Giant*, all of Ishiguro's novels and his short story collection have been shortlisted for major awards. Most significantly, *[An Artist of the Floating World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/An_Artist_of_the_Floating_World "An Artist of the Floating World")*, *[When We Were Orphans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/When_We_Were_Orphans "When We Were Orphans")*, and *[Never Let Me Go](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Never_Let_Me_Go_(novel) "Never Let Me Go (novel)")* were all short-listed for the Booker Prize—as was *The Remains of the Day*, which won it. A leaked account of a judging committee's meeting revealed that the committee found itself deciding between *[Never Let Me Go](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Never_Let_Me_Go_(novel) "Never Let Me Go (novel)")* and John Banville's *[The Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Sea_(novel) "The Sea (novel)")* before awarding the prize to the latter.

### Other distinctions

- 1983: Published in the [Granta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Granta "Granta") Best Young British Novelists issue
- 1993: Published in the [Granta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Granta "Granta") Best Young British Novelists issue
- 2005: *[Never Let Me Go](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Never_Let_Me_Go_(novel) "Never Let Me Go (novel)")* named on *[Time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_(magazine) "Time (magazine)")* magazine's list of the 100 greatest English language novels since the magazine's formation in 1923.
- 2008: *[The Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Times "The Times")* ranked Ishiguro 32nd on their list of "The 50 Greatest British Writers Since 1945".
- 2018: Ishiguro was awarded a [Knighthood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knighthood "Knighthood") for Services to Literature
- 2023: *[Living](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Living_(2022_film) "Living (2022 film)")* was nominated for the 2023 [Academy Award for Best Adapted Screenplay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academy_Award_for_Best_Adapted_Screenplay "Academy Award for Best Adapted Screenplay"). With the nomination, Ishiguro became the 6th Nobel Prize recipient to earn an [Academy Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academy_Awards "Academy Awards") nomination. Only 2 individuals, [George Bernard Shaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Bernard_Shaw "George Bernard Shaw") and [Bob Dylan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bob_Dylan "Bob Dylan"), have won both. Ishiguro was also nominated for the 2022 [BAFTA Award for Best Adapted Screenplay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BAFTA_Award_for_Best_Adapted_Screenplay "BAFTA Award for Best Adapted Screenplay") and [BAFTA Award for Outstanding British Film](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BAFTA_Award_for_Outstanding_British_Film "BAFTA Award for Outstanding British Film").
