**Harold Pinter** ([/ˈpɪntər/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English"); 10 October 1930 – 24 December 2008) was an English playwright, screenwriter, director and actor. A recipient of the [2005 Nobel Prize in Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2005_Nobel_Prize_in_Literature "2005 Nobel Prize in Literature"), Pinter was one of the most influential modern dramatists of [British theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_theatre "British theatre") with a writing career that spanned more than 50 years. His best-known plays include *[The Birthday Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Birthday_Party_(play) "The Birthday Party (play)")* (1957), *[The Homecoming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Homecoming "The Homecoming")* (1964) and *[Betrayal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Betrayal_(play) "Betrayal (play)")* (1978), each of which he adapted for the screen. His screenplay adaptations of others' works include *[The Servant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Servant_(1963_film) "The Servant (1963 film)")* (1963), *[The Go-Between](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Go-Between_(1971_film) "The Go-Between (1971 film)")* (1971), *[The French Lieutenant's Woman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_French_Lieutenant's_Woman_(film) "The French Lieutenant's Woman (film)")* (1981), *[The Trial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Trial_(1993_film) "The Trial (1993 film)")* (1993) and *[Sleuth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sleuth_(2007_film) "Sleuth (2007 film)")* (2007). He also directed or acted in radio, stage, television and film productions of his own and others' works.

Pinter was born in [Hackney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_Borough_of_Hackney "Metropolitan Borough of Hackney"), east London, and educated at [Hackney Downs School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hackney_Downs_School "Hackney Downs School"). He was a sprinter and cricket player, acting in school plays and writing [poetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poetry "Poetry"). He attended the [Royal Academy of Dramatic Art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Academy_of_Dramatic_Art "Royal Academy of Dramatic Art") but did not complete the course. He was fined for refusing [national service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_service "National service") as a [conscientious objector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conscientious_objector "Conscientious objector"). Subsequently, he continued training at the [Central School of Speech and Drama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_School_of_Speech_and_Drama "Central School of Speech and Drama") and worked in repertory theatre in Ireland and England. In 1956 he married actress [Vivien Merchant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vivien_Merchant "Vivien Merchant") and had a son, Daniel, born in 1958. He left Merchant in 1975 and married author [Lady Antonia Fraser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonia_Fraser "Antonia Fraser") in 1980.

Pinter's career as a playwright began with a production of *[The Room](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Room_(play) "The Room (play)")* in 1957. His second play, *[The Birthday Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Birthday_Party_(play) "The Birthday Party (play)")*, closed after eight performances but was enthusiastically reviewed by critic [Harold Hobson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Hobson "Harold Hobson"). His early works were described by critics as "[comedy of menace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comedy_of_menace "Comedy of menace")". Later plays such as *[No Man's Land](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/No_Man's_Land_(play) "No Man's Land (play)")* (1975) and *[Betrayal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Betrayal_(play) "Betrayal (play)")* (1978) became known as "[memory plays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memory_play "Memory play")". He appeared as an actor in productions of his own work on radio and film, and directed nearly 50 productions for stage, theatre and screen. Pinter received over [50 awards, prizes and other honours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_awards_and_honours_received_by_Harold_Pinter "List of awards and honours received by Harold Pinter"), including the [Nobel Prize in Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Literature "Nobel Prize in Literature") in 2005 and the French [Légion d'honneur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Légion_d'honneur "Légion d'honneur") in 2007.

Despite frail health after being diagnosed with [oesophageal cancer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Esophageal_cancer "Esophageal cancer") in December 2001, Pinter continued to act on stage and screen, last performing the title role of [Samuel Beckett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Beckett "Samuel Beckett")'s one-act monologue *[Krapp's Last Tape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krapp's_Last_Tape#Harold_Pinter "Krapp's Last Tape")*, for the 50th anniversary season of the [Royal Court Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Court_Theatre "Royal Court Theatre"), in October 2006. He died from [liver cancer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liver_cancer "Liver cancer") on 24 December 2008.

## Biography

### Early life and education

Pinter was born on 10 October 1930, in [Hackney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_Borough_of_Hackney "Metropolitan Borough of Hackney"), east London, the only child of British Jewish parents of Eastern European descent. His father, Hyman "Jack" Pinter was a ladies' tailor, and his mother, Frances (née Moskowitz) was a housewife. Hackney Council lists H. Pinter an A.R.P. Warden as a Master Taylor in a protected occupation, and assigned to Clapton's Post 14. Pinter believed an aunt's erroneous view that the family was [Sephardic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sephardi_Jews "Sephardi Jews") and had fled the [Spanish Inquisition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_Inquisition "Spanish Inquisition"); thus, for his early poems, Pinter used the pseudonym *Pinta* and at other times used variations such as *da Pinto*. Later research by [Lady Antonia Fraser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonia_Fraser "Antonia Fraser"), Pinter's second wife, revealed the legend to be apocryphal; three of Pinter's grandparents came from Poland and the fourth from [Odesa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odesa "Odesa"), so the family was [Ashkenazic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashkenazi_Jews "Ashkenazi Jews").

Pinter's family home in London is described by his official biographer [Michael Billington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Billington_(critic) "Michael Billington (critic)") as "a solid, red-brick, three-storey villa just off the noisy, bustling, traffic-ridden thoroughfare of the [Lower Clapton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_Clapton "Lower Clapton") Road". In 1940 and 1941, after [the Blitz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Blitz "The Blitz"), Pinter was [evacuated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evacuations_of_civilians_in_Britain_during_World_War_II "Evacuations of civilians in Britain during World War II") from their house in London to [Cornwall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornwall "Cornwall") and [Reading](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reading,_Berkshire "Reading, Berkshire"). Billington states that the "life-and-death intensity of daily experience" before and during the Blitz left Pinter with profound memories "of loneliness, bewilderment, separation and loss: themes that are in all his works."

Pinter discovered his social potential as a student at [Hackney Downs School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hackney_Downs_School "Hackney Downs School"), a London grammar school, between 1944 and 1948. "Partly through the school and partly through the social life of Hackney Boys' Club ... he formed an almost [sacerdotal](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/sacerdotal "wiktionary:sacerdotal") belief in the power of male friendship. The friends he made in those days – most particularly [Henry Woolf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Woolf "Henry Woolf"), Michael (Mick) Goldstein and Morris (Moishe) Wernick – have always been a vital part of the emotional texture of his life." A major influence on Pinter was his inspirational English teacher Joseph Brearley, who directed him in school plays and with whom he took long walks, talking about literature. According to Billington, under Brearley's instruction, "Pinter shone at English, wrote for the school magazine and discovered a gift for acting." In 1947 and 1948, he played [Romeo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romeo_Montague "Romeo Montague") and [Macbeth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macbeth_(Macbeth) "Macbeth (Macbeth)") in productions directed by Brearley.

At the age of 12, Pinter began writing poetry, and in spring 1947, his poetry was first published in the *Hackney Downs School Magazine*. In 1950 his poetry was first published outside the school magazine, in *[Poetry London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poetry_London "Poetry London")*, some of it under the pseudonym "Harold Pinta".

Pinter was an atheist.

### Sport and friendship

Pinter enjoyed running and broke the Hackney Downs School sprinting record.
He was a [cricket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cricket "Cricket") enthusiast, taking his bat with him when evacuated during the Blitz. In 1971, he told [Mel Gussow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mel_Gussow "Mel Gussow"): "one of my main obsessions in life is the game of cricket—I play and watch and read about it all the time." He was chairman of the Gaieties Cricket Club, a supporter of [Yorkshire Cricket Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yorkshire_County_Cricket_Club "Yorkshire County Cricket Club"), and devoted a section of his official website to the sport. One wall of his study was dominated by a portrait of himself as a young man playing cricket, which was described by [Sarah Lyall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarah_Lyall "Sarah Lyall"), writing in *[The New York Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_York_Times "The New York Times")*: "The painted Mr. Pinter, poised to swing his bat, has a wicked glint in his eye; testosterone all but flies off the canvas." Pinter approved of the "urban and exacting idea of cricket as a bold theatre of aggression." After his death, several of his school contemporaries recalled his achievements in sports, especially cricket and running. The [BBC Radio 4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Radio_4 "BBC Radio 4") memorial tribute included an essay on Pinter and cricket.

Other interests that Pinter mentioned to interviewers are family, love and sex, drinking, writing, and reading. According to Billington, "If the notion of male loyalty, competitive rivalry and fear of betrayal forms a constant thread in Pinter's work from *The Dwarfs* onwards, its origins can be found in his teenage Hackney years. Pinter adores women, enjoys flirting with them, and worships their resilience and strength. But, in his early work especially, they are often seen as disruptive influences on some pure and [Platonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platonic_love "Platonic love") ideal of male friendship: one of the most crucial of all Pinter's lost [Edens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garden_of_Eden "Garden of Eden")."

### Early theatrical training and stage experience

Beginning in late 1948, Pinter attended the [Royal Academy of Dramatic Art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Academy_of_Dramatic_Art "Royal Academy of Dramatic Art") for two terms, but hating the school, missed most of his classes, feigned a nervous breakdown, and dropped out in 1949. In 1948 he was called up for [National Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_service "National service"). He was initially refused registration as a [conscientious objector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conscientious_objector "Conscientious objector"), leading to his twice being prosecuted, and fined, for refusing to accept a medical examination, before his CO registration was ultimately agreed. He had a small part in the Christmas [pantomime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pantomime "Pantomime") *[Dick Whittington and His Cat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dick_Whittington_and_His_Cat "Dick Whittington and His Cat")* at the Chesterfield Hippodrome in 1949 to 1950. From January to July 1951, he attended the [Central School of Speech and Drama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_School_of_Speech_and_Drama "Central School of Speech and Drama").

From 1951 to 1952, he toured Ireland with the [Anew McMaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anew_McMaster "Anew McMaster") repertory company, playing over a dozen roles. In 1952, he began acting in regional English repertory productions; from 1953 to 1954, he worked for the [Donald Wolfit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Wolfit "Donald Wolfit") Company, at the King's Theatre, [Hammersmith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hammersmith "Hammersmith"), performing eight roles. From 1954 until 1959, Pinter acted under the stage name David Baron. In all, Pinter played over 20 roles under that name. To supplement his income from acting, Pinter worked as a waiter, a postman, a bouncer, and a snow-clearer, meanwhile, according to Mark Batty, "harbouring ambitions as a poet and writer." In October 1989 Pinter recalled: "I was in English rep as an actor for about 12 years. My favourite roles were undoubtedly the sinister ones. They're something to get your teeth into." During that period, he also performed occasional roles in his own and others' works for radio, TV, and film, as he continued to do throughout his career.

### Marriages and family life

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/46/Harold_Pinter%27s_home%2C_Worthing.jpg/250px-Harold_Pinter%27s_home%2C_Worthing.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Harold_Pinter's_home,_Worthing.jpg)

Pinter's house in [Worthing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worthing "Worthing"), 1962–64

From 1956 until 1980, Pinter was married to [Vivien Merchant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vivien_Merchant "Vivien Merchant"), an actress whom he met on tour, perhaps best known for her performance in the 1966 film *[Alfie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfie_(1966_film) "Alfie (1966 film)")*. Their son Daniel was born in 1958. Through the early 1970s, Merchant appeared in many of Pinter's works, including *[The Homecoming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Homecoming "The Homecoming")* on stage (1965) and screen (1973), but the marriage was turbulent. For seven years, from 1962 to 1969, Pinter was engaged in a clandestine affair with BBC-TV presenter and journalist [Joan Bakewell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joan_Bakewell "Joan Bakewell"), which inspired his 1978 play *[Betrayal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Betrayal_(play) "Betrayal (play)")*, and also throughout that period and beyond he had an affair with an American socialite, whom he nicknamed "Cleopatra". This relationship was another secret he kept from both his wife and Bakewell. Initially, *Betrayal* was thought to be a response to his later affair with historian [Antonia Fraser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonia_Fraser "Antonia Fraser"), the wife of [Hugh Fraser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugh_Fraser_(British_politician) "Hugh Fraser (British politician)"), and Pinter's "marital crack-up".

Pinter and Merchant had both met Antonia Fraser in 1969, when all three worked together on a [National Gallery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Gallery_(London) "National Gallery (London)") programme about [Mary, Queen of Scots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary,_Queen_of_Scots "Mary, Queen of Scots"); several years later, on 8–9 January 1975, Pinter and Fraser became romantically involved. That meeting initiated their five-year extramarital love affair. After hiding the relationship from Merchant for two and a half months, on 21 March 1975, Pinter finally told her "I've met somebody". After that, "Life in Hanover Terrace gradually became impossible", and Pinter moved out of their house on 28 April 1975, five days after the première of *[No Man's Land](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/No_Man's_Land_(play) "No Man's Land (play)")*.

In mid-August 1977, after Pinter and Fraser had spent two years living in borrowed and rented quarters, they moved into her former family home in [Holland Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holland_Park "Holland Park"), where Pinter began writing *Betrayal*. He reworked it later, while on holiday at the [Grand Hotel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Hotel_Eastbourne "Grand Hotel Eastbourne") in [Eastbourne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastbourne "Eastbourne"), in early January 1978. After the Frasers' divorce had become final in 1977 and the Pinters' in 1980, Pinter married Fraser on 27 November 1980. Because of a two-week delay in Merchant's signing the divorce papers, however, the reception had to precede the actual ceremony, originally scheduled to occur on his 50th birthday. Vivien Merchant died of acute alcoholism in the first week of October 1982, at the age of 53. Billington writes that Pinter "did everything possible to support" her and regretted that he ultimately became estranged from their son, Daniel, after their separation, Pinter's remarriage, and Merchant's death.

A reclusive gifted musician and writer, Daniel changed his surname from Pinter to Brand, the maiden name of his maternal grandmother, before Pinter and Fraser became romantically involved; while according to Fraser, his father could not understand it, she says that she could: "Pinter is such a distinctive name that he must have got tired of being asked, 'Any relation?'" Michael Billington wrote that Pinter saw Daniel's name change as "a largely pragmatic move on Daniel's part designed to keep the press ... at bay." Fraser told Billington that Daniel "was very nice to me at a time when it would have been only too easy for him to have turned on me ... simply because he had been the sole focus of his father's love and now manifestly wasn't." Still unreconciled at the time of his father's death, Daniel Brand did not attend Pinter's funeral.

Billington observes that "The break-up with Vivien and the new life with Antonia was to have a profound effect on Pinter's personality and his work," though he adds that Fraser herself did not claim to have influence over Pinter or his writing. In her own contemporaneous diary entry dated 15 January 1993, Fraser described herself more as Pinter's literary midwife. Indeed, she told Billington that "other people [such as [Peggy Ashcroft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peggy_Ashcroft "Peggy Ashcroft"), among others] had a shaping influence on [Pinter's] politics" and attributed changes in his writing and political views to a change from "an unhappy, complicated personal life ... to a happy, uncomplicated personal life", so that "a side of Harold which had always been there was somehow released. I think you can see that in his work after *[No Man's Land](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/No_Man's_Land_(play) "No Man's Land (play)")* [1975], which was a very bleak play."

Pinter was content in his second marriage and enjoyed family life with his six adult stepchildren and 17 step-grandchildren. Even after battling cancer for several years, he considered himself "a very lucky man in every respect". [Sarah Lyall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarah_Lyall "Sarah Lyall") notes in her 2007 interview with Pinter in *[The New York Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_York_Times "The New York Times")* that his "latest work, a slim pamphlet called 'Six Poems for A.', comprises poems written over 32 years, with "A" of course being Lady Antonia. The first of the poems was written in Paris, where she and Mr. Pinter traveled soon after they met. More than three decades later the two are rarely apart, and Mr. Pinter turns soft, even cozy, when he talks about his wife." In that interview Pinter "acknowledged that his plays—full of infidelity, cruelty, inhumanity, the lot—seem at odds with his domestic contentment. 'How can you write a happy play?' he said. 'Drama is about conflict and degrees of perturbation, disarray. I've never been able to write a happy play, but I've been able to enjoy a happy life.'" After his death, Fraser told *[The Guardian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Guardian "The Guardian")*: "He was a great man, and it was a privilege to live with him for over 33 years. He will never be forgotten."

## Civic activities and political activism

In 1948–49, when he was 18, Pinter opposed the politics of the [Cold War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cold_War "Cold War"), leading to his decision to become a [conscientious objector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conscientious_objector "Conscientious objector") and to refuse to comply with [National Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conscription_in_the_United_Kingdom "Conscription in the United Kingdom") in the British military. However, he told interviewers that, if he had been old enough at the time, he would have fought against the [Nazis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazism "Nazism") in [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II"). He seemed to express ambivalence, both indifference and hostility, towards political structures and politicians in his Fall 1966 *[Paris Review](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Paris_Review "The Paris Review")* interview conducted by [Lawrence M. Bensky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Larry_Bensky "Larry Bensky"). Yet, he had been an early member of the [Campaign for Nuclear Disarmament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Campaign_for_Nuclear_Disarmament "Campaign for Nuclear Disarmament") and also had supported the British [Anti-Apartheid Movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-Apartheid_Movement "Anti-Apartheid Movement") (1959–1994), participating in British artists' refusal to permit professional productions of their work in South Africa in 1963 and in subsequent related campaigns. In "A Play and Its Politics", a 1985 interview with Nicholas Hern, Pinter described his earlier plays retrospectively from the perspective of the politics of power and the dynamics of oppression.

In his last 25 years, Pinter increasingly focused his essays, interviews and public appearances directly on political issues. He was an officer in [International PEN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_PEN "International PEN"), travelling with American playwright [Arthur Miller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Miller "Arthur Miller") to [Turkey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkey "Turkey") in 1985 on a mission co-sponsored with a [Helsinki Watch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helsinki_Watch "Helsinki Watch") committee to investigate and protest against the torture of imprisoned writers. There he met victims of political oppression and their families. Pinter's experiences in Turkey and his knowledge of the Turkish suppression of the [Kurdish language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurdish_language "Kurdish language") inspired his 1988 play *[Mountain Language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mountain_Language "Mountain Language").* He was also an active member of the [Cuba Solidarity Campaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuba_Solidarity_Campaign "Cuba Solidarity Campaign"), an organisation that "campaigns in the UK against the US blockade of [Cuba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuba "Cuba")". In 2001, Pinter joined the International Committee to Defend Slobodan Milošević (ICDSM), which appealed for a fair trial and for the freedom of [Slobodan Milošević](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slobodan_Milošević "Slobodan Milošević"), signing a related "Artists' Appeal for Milošević" in 2004.

Pinter strongly opposed the 1991 [Gulf War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_War "Gulf War"), the 1999 [NATO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NATO "NATO") bombing campaign in [FR Yugoslavia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Republic_of_Yugoslavia "Federal Republic of Yugoslavia") during the [Kosovo War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kosovo_War "Kosovo War"), the United States' 2001 [War in Afghanistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_in_Afghanistan_(2001–present) "War in Afghanistan (2001–present)"), and the [2003 Invasion of Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2003_invasion_of_Iraq "2003 invasion of Iraq"). Among his provocative political statements, Pinter called Prime Minister [Tony Blair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tony_Blair "Tony Blair") a "deluded idiot" and compared the administration of President [George W. Bush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_W._Bush "George W. Bush") to [Nazi Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi_Germany "Nazi Germany"). He stated that the United States "was charging towards world domination while the American public and Britain's '[mass-murdering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass-murder "Mass-murder")' prime minister sat back and watched." He was very active in the [antiwar movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peace_movement "Peace movement") in the United Kingdom, speaking at rallies held by the [Stop the War Coalition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stop_the_War_Coalition "Stop the War Coalition") and frequently criticising American aggression, as when he asked rhetorically, in his acceptance speech for the [Wilfred Owen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilfred_Owen "Wilfred Owen") Award for Poetry on 18 March 2007: "What would Wilfred Owen make of the [invasion of Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2003_invasion_of_Iraq "2003 invasion of Iraq")? A bandit act, an act of blatant [state terrorism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_terrorism "State terrorism"), demonstrating absolute contempt for the conception of [international law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_law "International law")."

Pinter earned a reputation for being pugnacious, enigmatic, taciturn, terse, prickly, explosive and forbidding. Pinter's blunt political statements, and the award of the Nobel Prize in Literature, elicited strong criticism and even, at times, provoked ridicule and personal attacks. The historian [Geoffrey Alderman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geoffrey_Alderman "Geoffrey Alderman"), author of the official history of Hackney Downs School, expressed his own "Jewish View" of Harold Pinter: "Whatever his merit as a writer, actor and director, on an ethical plane Harold Pinter seems to me to have been intensely flawed, and his moral compass deeply fractured." [David Edgar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Edgar_(playwright) "David Edgar (playwright)"), writing in *[The Guardian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Guardian "The Guardian")*, defended Pinter against what he termed Pinter's "being berated by the belligerati" like [Johann Hari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Hari "Johann Hari"), who felt that he did not "deserve" to win the Nobel Prize. Later Pinter continued to campaign against the Iraq War and on behalf of other political causes that he supported.

Pinter signed the mission statement of [Jews for Justice for Palestinians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jews_for_Justice_for_Palestinians "Jews for Justice for Palestinians") in 2005 and its full-page advertisement, "What Is Israel Doing? A Call by Jews in Britain", published in *[The Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Times "The Times")* on 6 July 2006, and he was a patron of the [Palestine Festival of Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palestine_Festival_of_Literature "Palestine Festival of Literature"). In April 2008, Pinter signed the statement "We're not celebrating Israel's anniversary". The statement noted: "We cannot celebrate the birthday of a state founded on terrorism, massacres and the dispossession of another people from their land.", "We will celebrate when Arab and Jew live as equals in a peaceful Middle East"

## Career

### As actor

Pinter's acting career spanned over 50 years and, although he often played [villains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Villain "Villain"), included a wide range of roles on stage and in radio, film, and television. In addition to roles in radio and television adaptations of his own plays and dramatic sketches, early in his screenwriting career he made several cameo appearances in films based on his own screenplays; for example, as a society man in *[The Servant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Servant_(1963_film) "The Servant (1963 film)")* (1963) and as Mr. Bell in *[Accident](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accident_(1967_film) "Accident (1967 film)")* (1967), both directed by [Joseph Losey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Losey "Joseph Losey"); and as a bookshop customer in his later film *[Turtle Diary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turtle_Diary "Turtle Diary")* (1985), starring [Michael Gambon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Gambon "Michael Gambon"), [Glenda Jackson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glenda_Jackson "Glenda Jackson"), and [Ben Kingsley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ben_Kingsley "Ben Kingsley").

Pinter's notable film and television roles included the lawyer Saul Abrahams opposite [Peter O'Toole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_O'Toole "Peter O'Toole") in *[Rogue Male](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rogue_Male_(1976_film) "Rogue Male (1976 film)")*, [BBC TV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Television "BBC Television")'s 1976 adaptation of [Geoffrey Household](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geoffrey_Household "Geoffrey Household")'s 1939 novel, and a drunk Irish journalist in *[Langrishe, Go Down](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Langrishe,_Go_Down_(film) "Langrishe, Go Down (film)")* (starring [Judi Dench](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judi_Dench "Judi Dench") and [Jeremy Irons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeremy_Irons "Jeremy Irons")) distributed on [BBC Two](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Two "BBC Two") in 1978 and released in movie theatres in 2002. Pinter's later film roles included the criminal Sam Ross in *Mojo* (1997), written and directed by [Jez Butterworth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jez_Butterworth "Jez Butterworth"), based on Butterworth's [play of the same name](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mojo_(play) "Mojo (play)"); Sir Thomas Bertram (his most substantial feature-film role) in *[Mansfield Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mansfield_Park_(1999_film) "Mansfield Park (1999 film)")* (1998), a character that Pinter described as "a very civilised man ... a man of great sensibility but in fact, he's upholding and sustaining a totally brutal system [the slave trade] from which he derives his money"; and Uncle Benny, opposite [Pierce Brosnan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierce_Brosnan "Pierce Brosnan") and [Geoffrey Rush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geoffrey_Rush "Geoffrey Rush"), in *[The Tailor of Panama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Tailor_of_Panama_(film) "The Tailor of Panama (film)")* (2001). In [television films](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Television_movie "Television movie"), he played Mr. Bearing, the father of [ovarian cancer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ovarian_cancer "Ovarian cancer") patient Vivian Bearing, played by [Emma Thompson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emma_Thompson "Emma Thompson") in [Mike Nichols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mike_Nichols "Mike Nichols")'s [HBO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HBO "HBO") film of the [Pulitzer Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulitzer_Prize "Pulitzer Prize")-winning play *[Wit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wit_(film) "Wit (film)")* (2001); and the Director opposite [John Gielgud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Gielgud "John Gielgud") (Gielgud's last role) and [Rebecca Pidgeon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rebecca_Pidgeon "Rebecca Pidgeon") in *[Catastrophe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catastrophe_(play) "Catastrophe (play)")*, by [Samuel Beckett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Beckett "Samuel Beckett"), directed by [David Mamet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Mamet "David Mamet") as part of *Beckett on Film* (2001).

### As director

Pinter began to direct more frequently during the 1970s, becoming an associate director of the [National Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_National_Theatre "Royal National Theatre") (NT) in 1973. He directed almost 50 productions of his own and others' plays for stage, film, and television, including 10 productions of works by [Simon Gray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simon_Gray "Simon Gray"): the stage and/or film premières of *[Butley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butley_(film) "Butley (film)")* (stage, 1971; film, 1974), *[Otherwise Engaged](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otherwise_Engaged "Otherwise Engaged")* (1975), *The Rear Column* (stage, 1978; TV, 1980), *Close of Play* (NT, 1979), *[Quartermaine's Terms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quartermaine's_Terms "Quartermaine's Terms")* (1981), *Life Support* (1997), *The Late Middle Classes* (1999), and *The Old Masters* (2004). Several of those productions starred [Alan Bates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_Bates "Alan Bates") (1934–2003), who originated the stage and screen roles of not only Butley but also Mick in Pinter's first major commercial success, *[The Caretaker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Caretaker_(play) "The Caretaker (play)")* (stage, 1960; film, 1964); and in Pinter's double-bill produced at the [Lyric Hammersmith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyric_Hammersmith "Lyric Hammersmith") in 1984, he played Nicolas in *[One for the Road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One_for_the_Road_(Harold_Pinter_play) "One for the Road (Harold Pinter play)")* and the cab driver in *[Victoria Station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victoria_Station_(play) "Victoria Station (play)")*. Among over 35 plays that Pinter directed were *Next of Kin* (1974), by [John Hopkins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Hopkins_(screenwriter) "John Hopkins (screenwriter)"); *[Blithe Spirit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blithe_Spirit_(play) "Blithe Spirit (play)")* (1976), by [Noël Coward](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noël_Coward "Noël Coward"); *[The Innocents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Innocents_(play) "The Innocents (play)")* (1976), by [William Archibald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Archibald_(playwright) "William Archibald (playwright)"); *Circe and Bravo* (1986), by [Donald Freed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Freed "Donald Freed"); *[Taking Sides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taking_Sides_(play) "Taking Sides (play)")* (1995), by [Ronald Harwood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Harwood "Ronald Harwood"); and *[Twelve Angry Men](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twelve_Angry_Men_(1964_play) "Twelve Angry Men (1964 play)")* (1996), by [Reginald Rose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reginald_Rose "Reginald Rose").

### As playwright

Pinter was the author of 29 plays and 15 dramatic sketches and the co-author of two works for stage and radio. He was considered to have been one of the most influential modern British dramatists, Along with the 1967 [Tony Award for Best Play](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tony_Award_for_Best_Play "Tony Award for Best Play") for *The Homecoming* and several other American awards and award nominations, he and his plays received many awards in the UK and elsewhere throughout the world. His style has entered the English language as an adjective, "[Pinteresque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Characteristics_of_Harold_Pinter's_work#Pinteresque "Characteristics of Harold Pinter's work")", although Pinter himself disliked the term and found it meaningless.

#### "Comedies of menace" (1957–1968)

Pinter's first play, *[The Room](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Room_(play) "The Room (play)")*, written and first performed in 1957, was a student production at the [University of Bristol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Bristol "University of Bristol"), directed by his good friend, actor [Henry Woolf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Woolf "Henry Woolf"), who also originated the role of Mr. Kidd (which he reprised in 2001 and 2007). After Woolf mentioned that he had an idea for a play, he asked Pinter to write it so that he could direct it to fulfill a requirement for his postgraduate work. Pinter wrote it in three days. The production was described by Billington as "a staggeringly confident debut which attracted the attention of a young producer, [Michael Codron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Codron "Michael Codron"), who decided to present Pinter's next play, *[The Birthday Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Birthday_Party_(play) "The Birthday Party (play)")*, at the [Lyric Hammersmith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyric_Hammersmith "Lyric Hammersmith"), in 1958."

Written in 1957 and produced in 1958, Pinter's second play, *The Birthday Party*, one of his best-known works, was initially both a commercial and critical disaster, despite an enthusiastic review in *[The Sunday Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Sunday_Times "The Sunday Times")* by its influential drama critic [Harold Hobson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Hobson "Harold Hobson"), which appeared only after the production had closed and could not be reprieved. Critical accounts often quote Hobson:

> I am well aware that Mr Pinter[']s play received extremely bad notices last Tuesday morning. At the moment I write these [words] it is uncertain even whether the play will still be in the bill by the time they appear, though it is probable it will soon be seen elsewhere. Deliberately, I am willing to risk whatever reputation I have as a judge of plays by saying that *The Birthday Party* is not a Fourth, not even a Second, but a First [as in Class Honours]; and that Pinter, on the evidence of his work, possesses the most original, disturbing and arresting talent in theatrical London ... Mr Pinter and *The Birthday Party*, despite their experiences last week, will be heard of again. Make a note of their names.

Pinter himself and later critics generally credited Hobson as bolstering him and perhaps even rescuing his career.

In a review published in 1958, borrowing from the subtitle of *The Lunatic View: A Comedy of Menace*, a play by [David Campton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Campton "David Campton"), critic [Irving Wardle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irving_Wardle "Irving Wardle") called Pinter's early plays "[comedy of menace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comedy_of_menace "Comedy of menace")"—a label that people have applied repeatedly to his work. Such plays begin with an apparently innocent situation that becomes both threatening and "[absurd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absurdism "Absurdism")" as Pinter's characters behave in ways often perceived as inexplicable by his audiences and one another. Pinter acknowledges the influence of [Samuel Beckett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Beckett "Samuel Beckett"), particularly on his early work; they became friends, sending each other drafts of their works in progress for comments.

Pinter wrote *[The Hothouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Hothouse "The Hothouse")* in 1958, which he shelved for over 20 years (See "Overtly political plays and sketches" below). Next he wrote *[The Dumb Waiter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Dumb_Waiter "The Dumb Waiter")* (1959), which premièred in Germany and was then produced in a [double bill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_bill "Double bill") with *The Room* at the [Hampstead Theatre Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hampstead_Theatre "Hampstead Theatre"), in London, in 1960. It was then not produced often until the 1980s, and it has been revived more frequently since 2000, including the [West End](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_End_theatre "West End theatre") [Trafalgar Studios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trafalgar_Studios "Trafalgar Studios") production in 2007. The first production of *[The Caretaker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Caretaker_(play) "The Caretaker (play)")*, at the [Arts Theatre Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arts_Theatre "Arts Theatre"), in London, in 1960, established Pinter's theatrical reputation. The play transferred to the [Duchess Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duchess_Theatre "Duchess Theatre") in May 1960 and ran for 444 performances, receiving an [Evening Standard Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evening_Standard_Award "Evening Standard Award") for best play of 1960. Large radio and television audiences for his one-act play *[A Night Out](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Night_Out_(play) "A Night Out (play)")*, along with the popularity of his revue sketches, propelled him to further critical attention. In 1964, *The Birthday Party* was revived both on television (with Pinter himself in the role of Goldberg) and on stage (directed by Pinter at the [Aldwych Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aldwych_Theatre "Aldwych Theatre")) and was well received.

By the time Peter Hall's London production of *The Homecoming* (1964) reached [Broadway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broadway_theatre "Broadway theatre") in 1967, Pinter had become a celebrity playwright, and the play garnered four [Tony Awards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tony_Award "Tony Award"), among other awards. During this period, Pinter also wrote the radio play *[A Slight Ache](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Slight_Ache "A Slight Ache")*, first broadcast on the [BBC Third Programme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Third_Programme "BBC Third Programme") in 1959 and then adapted to the stage and performed at the [Arts Theatre Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arts_Theatre "Arts Theatre") in 1961. *A Night Out* (1960) was broadcast to a large audience on [ABC Weekend TV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ABC_Weekend_TV "ABC Weekend TV")'s television show *[Armchair Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armchair_Theatre "Armchair Theatre")*, after being transmitted on BBC Radio 3, also in 1960. His play *[Night School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Night_School_(play) "Night School (play)")* was first televised in 1960 on [Associated Rediffusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associated_Rediffusion "Associated Rediffusion"). *[The Collection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Collection_(play) "The Collection (play)")* premièred at the [Aldwych Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aldwych_Theatre "Aldwych Theatre") in 1962, and *The Dwarfs*, adapted from Pinter's then unpublished novel of the same title, was first broadcast on radio in 1960, then adapted for the stage (also at the Arts Theatre Club) in a double bill with *[The Lover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Lover_(play) "The Lover (play)")*, which had previously been televised by Associated Rediffusion in 1963; and *[Tea Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tea_Party_(play) "Tea Party (play)")*, a play that Pinter developed from his 1963 short story, first broadcast on [BBC TV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_TV "BBC TV") in 1965.

Working as both a screenwriter and as a playwright, Pinter composed a script called *[The Compartment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Basement_(play)#Origin:_"The_Compartment" "The Basement (play)")* (1966), for a trilogy of films to be contributed by [Samuel Beckett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Beckett "Samuel Beckett"), [Eugène Ionesco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugène_Ionesco "Eugène Ionesco"), and Pinter, of which only Beckett's film, titled *[Film](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Film_(film) "Film (film)")*, was actually produced. Then Pinter turned his unfilmed script into a television play, which was produced as *[The Basement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Basement_(play) "The Basement (play)")*, both on [BBC 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_2 "BBC 2") and also on stage in 1968.

#### "Memory plays" (1968–1982)

From the late 1960s through the early 1980s, Pinter wrote a series of plays and sketches that explore complex ambiguities, elegiac mysteries, comic vagaries, and other "quicksand-like" characteristics of [memory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memory "Memory") and which critics sometimes classify as Pinter's "[memory plays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memory_play "Memory play")". These include *[Landscape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landscape_(play) "Landscape (play)")* (1968), *[Silence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silence_(1969_play) "Silence (1969 play)")* (1969), *[Night](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Night_(sketch) "Night (sketch)")* (1969), *[Old Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Times "Old Times")* (1971), *[No Man's Land](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/No_Man's_Land_(play) "No Man's Land (play)")* (1975), *The Proust Screenplay* (1977), *[Betrayal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Betrayal_(play) "Betrayal (play)")* (1978), *[Family Voices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Family_Voices "Family Voices")* (1981), *[Victoria Station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victoria_Station_(play) "Victoria Station (play)")* (1982), and *[A Kind of Alaska](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Kind_of_Alaska "A Kind of Alaska")* (1982). Some of Pinter's later plays, including *Party Time* (1991), *[Moonlight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moonlight_(play) "Moonlight (play)")* (1993), *[Ashes to Ashes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashes_to_Ashes_(play) "Ashes to Ashes (play)")* (1996), and *[Celebration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celebration_(play) "Celebration (play)")* (2000), draw upon some features of his "memory" [dramaturgy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dramaturgy "Dramaturgy") in their focus on the past in the present, but they have personal and political resonances and other tonal differences from these earlier memory plays.

#### Overtly political plays and sketches (1980–2000)

Following a three-year period of creative drought in the early 1980s after his marriage to Antonia Fraser and the death of Vivien Merchant, Pinter's plays tended to become shorter and more overtly political, serving as critiques of [oppression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oppression "Oppression"), [torture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torture "Torture"), and other abuses of human rights, linked by the apparent "invulnerability of power." Just before this hiatus, in 1979, Pinter re-discovered his manuscript of *[The Hothouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Hothouse "The Hothouse")*, which he had written in 1958 but had set aside; he revised it and then directed its first production himself at [Hampstead Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hampstead_Theatre "Hampstead Theatre") in London, in 1980. Like his plays of the 1980s, *The Hothouse* concerns authoritarianism and the abuses of power politics, but it is also a comedy, like his earlier [comedies of menace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comedy_of_menace "Comedy of menace"). Pinter played the major role of Roote in a 1995 revival at the [Minerva Theatre, Chichester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minerva_Theatre,_Chichester "Minerva Theatre, Chichester").

Pinter's brief dramatic sketch *Precisely* (1983) is a duologue between two bureaucrats exploring the absurd power politics of mutual nuclear annihilation and [deterrence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deterrence_theory "Deterrence theory"). His first overtly political one-act play is *[One for the Road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One_for_the_Road_(Harold_Pinter_play) "One for the Road (Harold Pinter play)")* (1984). In 1985 Pinter stated that whereas his earlier plays presented metaphors for power and powerlessness, the later ones present literal realities of power and its abuse. Pinter's "political theatre dramatizes the interplay and conflict of the opposing poles of involvement and disengagement." *[Mountain Language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mountain_Language "Mountain Language")* (1988) is about the Turkish suppression of the [Kurdish language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurdish_language "Kurdish language"). The dramatic sketch *The New World Order* (1991) provides what Robert Cushman, writing in *[The Independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Independent "The Independent")* described as "10 nerve-wracking minutes" of two men threatening to torture a third man who is blindfolded, gagged and bound in a chair; Pinter directed the British première at the [Royal Court Theatre Upstairs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Court_Theatre "Royal Court Theatre"), where it opened on 9 July 1991, and the production then transferred to Washington, D.C., where it was revived in 1994. Pinter's longer [political satire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_satire "Political satire") *Party Time* (1991) premièred at the [Almeida Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almeida_Theatre "Almeida Theatre") in London, in a double-bill with *Mountain Language*. Pinter adapted it as a screenplay for television in 1992, directing that production, first broadcast in the UK on [Channel 4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Channel_4 "Channel 4") on 17 November 1992.

Intertwining political and personal concerns, his next full-length plays, *[Moonlight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moonlight_(play) "Moonlight (play)")* (1993) and *[Ashes to Ashes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashes_to_Ashes_(play) "Ashes to Ashes (play)")* (1996) are set in domestic households and focus on dying and death; in their personal conversations in *Ashes to Ashes*, Devlin and Rebecca allude to unspecified atrocities relating to the [Holocaust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Holocaust "The Holocaust"). After experiencing the deaths of first his mother (1992) and then his father (1997), again merging the personal and the political, Pinter wrote the poems "Death" (1997) and "The Disappeared" (1998).

Pinter's last stage play, *[Celebration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celebration_(play) "Celebration (play)")* (2000), is a social satire set in an opulent restaurant, which lampoons [The Ivy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Ivy_(United_Kingdom) "The Ivy (United Kingdom)"), a fashionable venue in London's West End theatre district, and its patrons who "have just come from performances of either the ballet or the opera. Not that they can remember a darn thing about what they saw, including the titles. [These] gilded, foul-mouthed souls are just as myopic when it comes to their own table mates (and for that matter, their food), with conversations that usually connect only on the surface, if there." On its surface the play may appear to have fewer overtly political resonances than some of the plays from the 1980s and 1990s; but its central male characters, brothers named Lambert and Matt, are members of the elite (like the men in charge in *Party Time*), who describe themselves as "peaceful strategy consultants [because] we don't carry guns." At the next table, Russell, a banker, describes himself as a "totally disordered personality ... a psychopath", while Lambert "vows to be reincarnated as '[a] more civilised, [a] gentler person, [a] nicer person'." These characters' deceptively smooth exteriors mask their extreme viciousness. *Celebration* evokes familiar [Pinteresque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Characteristics_of_Harold_Pinter's_work#Pinteresque "Characteristics of Harold Pinter's work") political contexts: "The ritzy loudmouths in 'Celebration' ... and the quieter working-class mumblers of 'The Room' ... have everything in common beneath the surface". "Money remains in the service of entrenched power, and the brothers in the play are 'strategy consultants' whose jobs involve force and violence ... It is tempting but inaccurate to equate the comic power inversions of the social behaviour in *Celebration* with lasting change in larger political structures", according to Grimes, for whom the play indicates Pinter's pessimism about the possibility of changing the status quo. Yet, as the Waiter's often comically unbelievable reminiscences about his grandfather demonstrate in *Celebration*, Pinter's final stage plays also extend some [expressionistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expressionism "Expressionism") aspects of his earlier "memory plays", while harking back to his "comedies of menace", as illustrated in the characters and in the Waiter's final speech:

> My grandfather introduced me to the mystery of life and I'm still in the middle of it. I can't find the door to get out. My grandfather got out of it. He got right out of it. He left it behind him and he didn't look back. He got that absolutely right. And I'd like to make one further interjection.
>  *He stands still. Slow fade*.

During 2000–2001, there were also simultaneous productions of *[Remembrance of Things Past](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Remembrance_of_Things_Past_(play) "Remembrance of Things Past (play)")*, Pinter's stage adaptation of his unpublished *Proust Screenplay*, written in collaboration with and directed by [Di Trevis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Di_Trevis "Di Trevis"), at the [Royal National Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_National_Theatre "Royal National Theatre"), and a revival of *[The Caretaker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Caretaker_(play) "The Caretaker (play)")* directed by [Patrick Marber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patrick_Marber "Patrick Marber") and starring [Michael Gambon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Gambon "Michael Gambon"), [Rupert Graves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rupert_Graves "Rupert Graves"), and [Douglas Hodge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Douglas_Hodge "Douglas Hodge"), at the [Comedy Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comedy_Theatre "Comedy Theatre").

Like *Celebration*, Pinter's penultimate sketch, *Press Conference* (2002), "invokes both torture and the fragile, circumscribed existence of dissent". In its première in the [National Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_National_Theatre "Royal National Theatre")'s two-part production of *Sketches*, despite undergoing chemotherapy at the time, Pinter played the ruthless Minister willing to murder little children for the benefit of "The State".

### As screenwriter

Pinter composed 27 screenplays and film scripts for cinema and television, many of which were filmed, or adapted as stage plays. His fame as a screenwriter began with his three screenplays written for films directed by [Joseph Losey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Losey "Joseph Losey"), leading to their close friendship: *[The Servant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Servant_(1963_film) "The Servant (1963 film)")* (1963), based on the novel by [Robin Maugham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robin_Maugham "Robin Maugham"); *[Accident](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accident_(1967_film) "Accident (1967 film)")* (1967), adapted from the novel by [Nicholas Mosley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicholas_Mosley "Nicholas Mosley"); and *[The Go-Between](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Go-Between_(1971_film) "The Go-Between (1971 film)")* (1971), based on the novel by [L. P. Hartley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L._P._Hartley "L. P. Hartley"). Films based on Pinter's adaptations of his own stage plays are: *[The Caretaker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Caretaker_(play) "The Caretaker (play)")* (1963), directed by [Clive Donner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clive_Donner "Clive Donner"); *[The Birthday Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Birthday_Party_(1968_film) "The Birthday Party (1968 film)")* (1968), directed by [William Friedkin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Friedkin "William Friedkin"); *[The Homecoming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Homecoming_(film) "The Homecoming (film)")* (1973), directed by Peter Hall; and *[Betrayal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Betrayal_(1983_film) "Betrayal (1983 film)")* (1983), directed by [David Jones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Jones_(director) "David Jones (director)").

Pinter also adapted other writers' novels to screenplays, including *[The Pumpkin Eater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Pumpkin_Eater "The Pumpkin Eater")* (1964), based on the novel by [Penelope Mortimer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penelope_Mortimer "Penelope Mortimer"), directed by [Jack Clayton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jack_Clayton "Jack Clayton"); *[The Quiller Memorandum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Quiller_Memorandum "The Quiller Memorandum")* (1966), from the 1965 spy novel *The Berlin Memorandum*, by [Elleston Trevor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elleston_Trevor "Elleston Trevor"), directed by [Michael Anderson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Anderson_(director) "Michael Anderson (director)"); *[The Last Tycoon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Last_Tycoon_(1976_film) "The Last Tycoon (1976 film)")* (1976), from the unfinished novel by [F. Scott Fitzgerald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F._Scott_Fitzgerald "F. Scott Fitzgerald"), directed by [Elia Kazan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elia_Kazan "Elia Kazan"); *[The French Lieutenant's Woman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_French_Lieutenant's_Woman_(film) "The French Lieutenant's Woman (film)")* (1981), from the novel by [John Fowles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Fowles "John Fowles"), directed by [Karel Reisz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karel_Reisz "Karel Reisz"); *[Turtle Diary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turtle_Diary "Turtle Diary")* (1985), based on the novel by [Russell Hoban](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russell_Hoban "Russell Hoban"); *The Heat of the Day* (1988), a television film, from the 1949 novel by [Elizabeth Bowen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_Bowen "Elizabeth Bowen"); *[The Comfort of Strangers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Comfort_of_Strangers_(film) "The Comfort of Strangers (film)")* (1990), from the novel by [Ian McEwan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ian_McEwan "Ian McEwan"), directed by [Paul Schrader](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Schrader "Paul Schrader"); and *[The Trial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Trial_(1993_film) "The Trial (1993 film)")* (1993), from the novel by [Franz Kafka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_Kafka "Franz Kafka"), directed by David Jones.

His commissioned screenplays of others' works for the films *[The Handmaid's Tale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Handmaid's_Tale_(film) "The Handmaid's Tale (film)")* (1990), *[The Remains of the Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Remains_of_the_Day_(film) "The Remains of the Day (film)")* (1990), and *[Lolita](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lolita_(1997_film) "Lolita (1997 film)")* (1997), remain unpublished and in the case of the latter two films, uncredited, though several scenes from or aspects of his scripts were used in these finished films. His screenplays *[The Proust Screenplay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Remembrance_of_Things_Past_(play) "Remembrance of Things Past (play)")* (1972), *Victory* (1982), and *[The Dreaming Child](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Dreaming_Child_(screenplay_by_Harold_Pinter) "The Dreaming Child (screenplay by Harold Pinter)")* (1997) and his unpublished screenplay *[The Tragedy of King Lear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Tragedy_of_King_Lear_(screenplay_by_Harold_Pinter) "The Tragedy of King Lear (screenplay by Harold Pinter)")* (2000) have not been filmed. A section of Pinter's *Proust Screenplay* was, however, released as the 1984 film *[Swann in Love](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/In_Search_of_Lost_Time#Adaptations "In Search of Lost Time")* (*Un amour de Swann*), directed by [Volker Schlöndorff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volker_Schlöndorff "Volker Schlöndorff"), and it was also adapted by [Michael Bakewell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Bakewell "Michael Bakewell") as a two-hour radio drama broadcast on [BBC Radio 3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Radio_3 "BBC Radio 3") in 1995, before Pinter and director Di Trevis collaborated to adapt it for the 2000 National Theatre production.

Pinter's last filmed screenplay was an adaptation of the 1970 [Tony Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tony_Award "Tony Award")-winning play *[Sleuth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sleuth_(play) "Sleuth (play)")*, by [Anthony Shaffer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthony_Shaffer_(writer) "Anthony Shaffer (writer)"), which was commissioned by [Jude Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jude_Law "Jude Law"), one of the film's producers. It is the basis for the 2007 film *[Sleuth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sleuth_(2007_film) "Sleuth (2007 film)")*, directed by [Kenneth Branagh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenneth_Branagh "Kenneth Branagh"). Pinter's screenplays for *[The French Lieutenant's Woman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_French_Lieutenant's_Woman_(film) "The French Lieutenant's Woman (film)")* and *Betrayal* were nominated for [Academy Awards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academy_Award "Academy Award") in 1981 and 1983, respectively.

### 2001–2008

From 16 to 31 July 2001, a Harold Pinter Festival celebrating his work, curated by [Michael Colgan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Colgan_(theatre_director) "Michael Colgan (theatre director)"), artistic director of the [Gate Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gate_Theatre "Gate Theatre"), Dublin, was held as part of the annual Lincoln Center Festival at [Lincoln Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lincoln_Center_for_the_Performing_Arts "Lincoln Center for the Performing Arts") in New York City. Pinter participated both as an actor, as Nicolas in *One for the Road*, and as a director of a double bill pairing his last play, *Celebration*, with his first play, *The Room*. As part of a two-week "Harold Pinter Homage" at the World Leaders Festival of Creative Genius, held from 24 September to 30 October 2001, at the Harbourfront Centre, in Toronto, Canada, Pinter presented a dramatic reading of *Celebration* (2000) and also participated in a public interview as part of the [International Festival of Authors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture_in_Toronto#Literature "Culture in Toronto").

In December 2001, Pinter was diagnosed with [oesophageal cancer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oesophageal_cancer "Oesophageal cancer"), for which, in 2002, he underwent an operation and [chemotherapy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemotherapy "Chemotherapy"). During the course of his treatment, he directed a production of his play *No Man's Land*, and wrote and performed in a new sketch, "Press Conference", for a production of his dramatic sketches at the National Theatre, and from 2002 on he was increasingly active in political causes, writing and presenting politically charged poetry, essays, speeches, as well as involved in developing his final two screenplay adaptations, *The Tragedy of King Lear* and *Sleuth*, whose drafts are in the British Library's [Harold Pinter Archive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Pinter_Archive "Harold Pinter Archive") (Add MS 88880/2).

From 9 to 25 January 2003, the Manitoba Theatre Centre, in [Manitoba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manitoba "Manitoba"), Canada, held a nearly month-long *PinterFest*, in which over 130 performances of twelve of Pinter's plays were performed by a dozen different theatre companies. Productions during the Festival included: *The Hothouse*, *Night School*, *The Lover*, *The Dumb Waiter*, *The Homecoming*, *The Birthday Party*, *Monologue*, *One for the Road*, *The Caretaker*, *Ashes to Ashes*, *Celebration*, and *No Man's Land*.

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/df/Harold-pinter-atp.jpg/250px-Harold-pinter-atp.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Harold-pinter-atp.jpg)

Pinter in 2005

In 2005, Pinter stated that he had stopped writing plays and that he would be devoting his efforts more to his political activism and writing poetry: "I think I've written 29 plays. I think it's enough for me ... My energies are going in different directions—over the last few years I've made a number of political speeches at various locations and ceremonies ... I'm using a lot of energy more specifically about political states of affairs, which I think are very, very worrying as things stand." Some of this later poetry included "The 'Special Relationship'", "Laughter", and "The Watcher".

From 2005, Pinter experienced ill health, including a rare skin disease called [pemphigus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pemphigus "Pemphigus") and "a form of [septicaemia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sepsis "Sepsis") that afflict[ed] his feet and made it difficult for him to walk." Yet, he completed his screenplay for the film of *Sleuth* in 2005. His last dramatic work for radio, *Voices* (2005), a collaboration with composer [James Clarke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Clarke_(composer) "James Clarke (composer)"), adapting selected works by Pinter to music, premièred on [BBC Radio 3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Radio_3 "BBC Radio 3") on his 75th birthday on 10 October 2005. Three days later, it was announced that he had won the 2005 Nobel Prize in Literature.

In an interview with Pinter in 2006, conducted by critic Michael Billington as part of the cultural programme of the [2006 Winter Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2006_Winter_Olympics "2006 Winter Olympics") in [Turin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turin "Turin"), Italy, Pinter confirmed that he would continue to write poetry but not plays. In response, the audience shouted *No* in unison, urging him to keep writing. Along with the international symposium on Pinter: Passion, Poetry, Politics, curated by Billington, the 2006 [Europe Theatre Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Europe_Theatre_Prize "Europe Theatre Prize") theatrical events celebrating Pinter included new productions (in French) of *Precisely* (1983), *One for the Road* (1984), *Mountain Language* (1988), *The New World Order* (1991), *Party Time* (1991), and *Press Conference* (2002) (French versions by Jean Pavans); and *Pinter Plays, Poetry & Prose*, an evening of dramatic readings, directed by [Alan Stanford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_Stanford "Alan Stanford"), of the [Gate Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gate_Theatre "Gate Theatre"), Dublin. In June 2006, the [British Academy of Film and Television Arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Academy_of_Film_and_Television_Arts "British Academy of Film and Television Arts") (BAFTA) hosted a celebration of Pinter's films curated by his friend, the playwright [David Hare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Hare_(playwright) "David Hare (playwright)"). Hare introduced the selection of film clips by saying: "To jump back into the world of Pinter's movies ... is to remind yourself of a literate mainstream cinema, focused as much as [Bergman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ingmar_Bergman "Ingmar Bergman")'s is on the human face, in which tension is maintained by a carefully crafted mix of image and dialogue."

After returning to London from the [Edinburgh International Book Festival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edinburgh_International_Book_Festival "Edinburgh International Book Festival"), in September 2006, Pinter began rehearsing for his performance of the role of [Krapp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krapp's_Last_Tape#Characters "Krapp's Last Tape") in [Samuel Beckett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Beckett "Samuel Beckett")'s one-act [monologue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monologue "Monologue") *[Krapp's Last Tape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krapp's_Last_Tape "Krapp's Last Tape")*, which he performed from a motorised wheelchair in a limited run the following month at the [Royal Court Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Court_Theatre "Royal Court Theatre") to sold-out audiences and "ecstatic" critical reviews. The production ran for only nine performances, as part of the 50th-anniversary celebration season of the [Royal Court Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Court_Theatre "Royal Court Theatre"); it sold out within minutes of the opening of the box office and tickets commanded large sums from [ticket resellers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ticket_resale "Ticket resale"). One performance was filmed and broadcast on [BBC Four](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Four "BBC Four") on 21 June 2007, and also screened later, as part of the memorial PEN Tribute to Pinter, in New York, on 2 May 2009.

In October and November 2006, [Sheffield Theatres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheffield_Theatres "Sheffield Theatres") hosted [Pinter: A Celebration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheffield_Theatres#Pinter:_A_Celebration "Sheffield Theatres"). It featured productions of seven of Pinter's plays: *The Caretaker*, *Voices*, *No Man's Land*, *Family Voices*, *Tea Party*, *The Room*, *One for the Road*, and *The Dumb Waiter*; and films (most his screenplays; some in which Pinter appears as an actor).

In February and March 2007, a 50th anniversary of *The Dumb Waiter*, was produced at the [Trafalgar Studios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trafalgar_Studios "Trafalgar Studios"). Later in February 2007, [John Crowley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Crowley_(director) "John Crowley (director)")'s film version of Pinter's play *Celebration* (2000) was shown on *[More4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/More4 "More4")* ([Channel 4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Channel_4 "Channel 4"), UK). On 18 March 2007, [BBC Radio 3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Radio_3 "BBC Radio 3") broadcast a new radio production of *The Homecoming*, directed by [Thea Sharrock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thea_Sharrock "Thea Sharrock") and produced by Martin J. Smith, with Pinter performing the role of Max (for the first time; he had previously played Lenny on stage in 1964). A revival of *The Hothouse* opened at the National Theatre, in London, in July 2007, concurrently with a revival of *Betrayal* at the [Donmar Warehouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donmar_Warehouse "Donmar Warehouse"), directed by [Roger Michell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roger_Michell "Roger Michell").

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a8/No_Mans_Land_Harold_Pinter_Duke_of_Yorks_Theatre_London.jpg/250px-No_Mans_Land_Harold_Pinter_Duke_of_Yorks_Theatre_London.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:No_Mans_Land_Harold_Pinter_Duke_of_Yorks_Theatre_London.jpg)

*[No Man's Land](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/No_Man's_Land_(play) "No Man's Land (play)")* revival at [Duke of York's Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duke_of_York's_Theatre "Duke of York's Theatre"), 30 December 2008

Revivals in 2008 included the 40th-anniversary production of the American première of *The Homecoming* on Broadway, directed by [Daniel J. Sullivan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_J._Sullivan "Daniel J. Sullivan"). From 8 to 24 May 2008, the [Lyric Hammersmith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyric_Hammersmith "Lyric Hammersmith") celebrated the 50th anniversary of *The Birthday Party* with a revival and related events, including a gala performance and reception hosted by Harold Pinter on 19 May 2008, exactly 50 years after its London première there. The final revival during Pinter's lifetime was a production of *No Man's Land*, directed by [Rupert Goold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rupert_Goold "Rupert Goold"), opening at the Gate Theatre, Dublin, in August 2008, and then transferring to the [Duke of York's Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duke_of_York's_Theatre "Duke of York's Theatre"), London, where it played until 3 January 2009. On the Monday before Christmas 2008, Pinter was admitted to [Hammersmith Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hammersmith_Hospital "Hammersmith Hospital"), where he died on Christmas Eve from liver cancer, aged 78.

On 26 December 2008, when *No Man's Land* reopened at the Duke of York's, the actors paid tribute to Pinter from the stage, with Michael Gambon reading Hirst's monologue about his "photograph album" from Act Two that Pinter had asked him to read at his funeral, ending with a standing ovation from the audience, many of whom were in tears:

> I might even show you my photograph album. You might even see a face in it which might remind you of your own, of what you once were. You might see faces of others, in shadow, or cheeks of others, turning, or jaws, or backs of necks, or eyes, dark under hats, which might remind you of others, whom once you knew, whom you thought long dead, but from whom you will still receive a sidelong glance if you can face the good ghost. Allow the love of the good ghost. They possess all that emotion ... trapped. Bow to it. It will assuredly never release them, but who knows ... what relief ... it may give them ... who knows how they may quicken ... in their chains, in their glass jars. You think it cruel ... to quicken them, when they are fixed, imprisoned? No ... no. Deeply, deeply, they wish to respond to your touch, to your look, and when you smile, their joy ... is unbounded. And so I say to you, tender the dead, as you would yourself be tendered, now, in what you would describe as your life.

## Posthumous events

### Funeral

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/0d/Grave_of_Harold_Pinter_in_Kensal_Green_Cemetery.jpg/250px-Grave_of_Harold_Pinter_in_Kensal_Green_Cemetery.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Grave_of_Harold_Pinter_in_Kensal_Green_Cemetery.jpg)

Grave of Harold Pinter in [Kensal Green Cemetery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kensal_Green_Cemetery "Kensal Green Cemetery")

Pinter's funeral was a private, half-hour secular ceremony conducted at the graveside at [Kensal Green Cemetery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kensal_Green_Cemetery "Kensal Green Cemetery"), 31 December 2008. The eight readings selected in advance by Pinter included passages from seven of his own writings and from the story "[The Dead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Dead_(Joyce_short_story) "The Dead (Joyce short story)")", by [James Joyce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Joyce "James Joyce"), which was read by actress [Penelope Wilton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penelope_Wilton "Penelope Wilton"). [Michael Gambon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Gambon "Michael Gambon") read the "photo album" speech from *No Man's Land* and three other readings, including Pinter's poem "Death" (1997). Other readings honoured Pinter's widow and his love of cricket. The ceremony was attended by many notable theatre people, including [Tom Stoppard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tom_Stoppard "Tom Stoppard"), but not by Pinter's son, Daniel Brand. At its end, Pinter's widow, Antonia Fraser, stepped forward to his grave and quoted from [Horatio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horatio_(character) "Horatio (character)")'s speech after the death of [Hamlet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prince_Hamlet "Prince Hamlet"): "Goodnight, sweet prince, / And flights of angels sing thee to thy rest."

### Memorial tributes

The night before Pinter's burial, theatre marquees on Broadway dimmed their lights for a minute in tribute, and on the final night of *No Man's Land* at the Duke of York's Theatre on 3 January 2009, all of the [Ambassador Theatre Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ambassador_Theatre_Group "Ambassador Theatre Group") in the West End dimmed their lights for an hour to honour the playwright.

[Diane Abbott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diane_Abbott "Diane Abbott"), the [Member of Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Member_of_Parliament_(UK) "Member of Parliament (UK)") for [Hackney North & Stoke Newington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hackney_North_and_Stoke_Newington_(UK_Parliament_constituency) "Hackney North and Stoke Newington (UK Parliament constituency)") proposed an [early day motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_day_motion "Early day motion") in the [House of Commons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Commons_of_the_United_Kingdom "House of Commons of the United Kingdom") to support a residents' campaign to restore the Clapton Cinematograph Theatre, established in [Lower Clapton Road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_Clapton "Lower Clapton") in 1910, and to turn it into a memorial to Pinter "to honour this Hackney boy turned literary great." On 2 May 2009, a free public memorial tribute was held at [The Graduate Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CUNY_Graduate_Center "CUNY Graduate Center") of The [City University of New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_University_of_New_York "City University of New York"). It was part of the 5th Annual [PEN World Voices Festival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PEN_World_Voices "PEN World Voices") of International Literature, taking place in New York City. Another memorial celebration, held in the Olivier Theatre, at the [Royal National Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_National_Theatre "Royal National Theatre"), in London, on the evening of 7 June 2009, consisted of excerpts and readings from Pinter's writings by nearly three dozen actors, many of whom were his friends and associates, including: [Eileen Atkins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eileen_Atkins "Eileen Atkins"), [David Bradley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Bradley_(English_actor) "David Bradley (English actor)"), [Colin Firth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colin_Firth "Colin Firth"), [Henry Goodman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Goodman "Henry Goodman"), [Sheila Hancock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheila_Hancock "Sheila Hancock"), [Alan Rickman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_Rickman "Alan Rickman"), [Penelope Wilton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penelope_Wilton "Penelope Wilton"), [Susan Wooldridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susan_Wooldridge "Susan Wooldridge"), and [Henry Woolf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Woolf "Henry Woolf"); and a troupe of students from the [London Academy of Music and Dramatic Art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Academy_of_Music_and_Dramatic_Art "London Academy of Music and Dramatic Art"), directed by Ian Rickson.

On 16 June 2009, Antonia Fraser officially opened a commemorative room at the [Hackney Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hackney_Empire "Hackney Empire"). The theatre also established a writer's residency in Pinter's name. Most of issue number 28 of [Craig Raine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Craig_Raine "Craig Raine")'s Arts Tri-Quarterly *[Areté](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Areté "Areté")* was devoted to pieces remembering Pinter, beginning with Pinter's 1987 unpublished love poem dedicated "To Antonia" and his poem "Paris", written in 1975 (the year in which he and Fraser began living together), followed by brief memoirs by some of Pinter's associates and friends, including [Patrick Marber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patrick_Marber "Patrick Marber"), [Nina Raine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nina_Raine "Nina Raine"), [Tom Stoppard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tom_Stoppard "Tom Stoppard"), [Peter Nichols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Nichols_(playwright) "Peter Nichols (playwright)"), [Susanna Gross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susanna_Gross "Susanna Gross"), [Richard Eyre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Eyre "Richard Eyre"), and David Hare.

A memorial cricket match at [Lord's Cricket Ground](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord's_Cricket_Ground "Lord's Cricket Ground") between the Gaieties Cricket Club and the Lord's Taverners, followed by performances of Pinter's poems and excerpts from his plays, took place on 27 September 2009.

In 2009, [English PEN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_PEN "English PEN") established the [PEN Pinter Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PEN/Pinter_Prize "PEN/Pinter Prize"), which is awarded annually to a British writer or a writer resident in Britain who, in the words of Pinter's Nobel speech, casts an 'unflinching, unswerving' gaze upon the world, and shows a 'fierce intellectual determination ... to define the real truth of our lives and our societies'. The prize is shared with an international writer of courage. The inaugural winners of the prize were [Tony Harrison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tony_Harrison "Tony Harrison") and the Burmese poet and comedian [Maung Thura (a.k.a. Zarganar)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zarganar "Zarganar").

### *Being Harold Pinter*

In January 2011 *Being Harold Pinter*, a theatrical collage of excerpts from Pinter's dramatic works, his Nobel Lecture, and letters of Belarusian prisoners, created and performed by the [Belarus Free Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belarus_Free_Theatre "Belarus Free Theatre"), evoked a great deal of attention in the [public media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_media "Public media"). The Free Theatre's members had to be smuggled out of [Minsk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minsk "Minsk"), owing to a government crackdown on dissident artists, to perform their production in a two-week sold-out engagement at [La MaMa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_MaMa "La MaMa") in New York as part of the 2011 [Under the Radar Festival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Under_the_Radar_Festival "Under the Radar Festival"). In an additional sold-out benefit performance at the [Public Theater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_Theater "Public Theater"), co-hosted by playwrights [Tony Kushner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tony_Kushner "Tony Kushner") and [Tom Stoppard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tom_Stoppard "Tom Stoppard"), the prisoner's letters were read by ten guest performers: [Mandy Patinkin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandy_Patinkin "Mandy Patinkin"), [Kevin Kline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kevin_Kline "Kevin Kline"), [Olympia Dukakis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympia_Dukakis "Olympia Dukakis"), [Lily Rabe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lily_Rabe "Lily Rabe"), [Linda Emond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linda_Emond "Linda Emond"), [Josh Hamilton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josh_Hamilton_(actor) "Josh Hamilton (actor)"), [Stephen Spinella](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Spinella "Stephen Spinella"), [Lou Reed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lou_Reed "Lou Reed"), [Laurie Anderson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laurie_Anderson "Laurie Anderson"), and [Philip Seymour Hoffman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_Seymour_Hoffman "Philip Seymour Hoffman"). In solidarity with the Belarus Free Theatre, collaborations of actors and theatre companies joined in offering additional benefit readings of *Being Harold Pinter* across the United States.

### The Harold Pinter Theatre, London

In September 2011, British Theatre owners, [Ambassador Theatre Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ambassador_Theatre_Group "Ambassador Theatre Group") (ATG) announced it was renaming its *Comedy Theatre*, Panton Street, London to become *The [Harold Pinter Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Pinter_Theatre "Harold Pinter Theatre")*. [Howard Panter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Howard_Panter "Howard Panter"), Joint CEO and Creative Director of ATG told the [BBC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC "BBC"), "The work of Pinter has become an integral part of the history of the Comedy Theatre. The re-naming of one of our most successful West End theatres is a fitting tribute to a man who made such a mark on British theatre who, over his 50 year career, became recognised as one of the most influential modern British dramatists."

## Accolades and honours

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a9/Harold_Pinter.JPG/250px-Harold_Pinter.JPG?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Harold_Pinter.JPG)

Study of Pinter by [Reginald Gray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reginald_Gray_(artist) "Reginald Gray (artist)"), 2007. (*[New Statesman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Statesman "New Statesman")*, 12 January 2009)

An Honorary Associate of the [National Secular Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Secular_Society "National Secular Society"), a Fellow of the [Royal Society of Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society_of_Literature "Royal Society of Literature"), and an Honorary Fellow of the [Modern Language Association of America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_Language_Association "Modern Language Association") (1970), Pinter was appointed [CBE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_the_British_Empire "Order of the British Empire") in 1966 and became a [Member of the Order of the Companions of Honour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Member_of_the_Order_of_the_Companions_of_Honour "Member of the Order of the Companions of Honour") in 2002, having declined a knighthood in 1996. In 1995, he accepted the [David Cohen Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Cohen_Prize "David Cohen Prize"), in recognition of a lifetime of literary achievement. In 1996, he received a [Laurence Olivier Special Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laurence_Olivier_Awards "Laurence Olivier Awards") for lifetime achievement in the theatre. In 1997 he became a [BAFTA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Academy_of_Film_and_Television_Arts "British Academy of Film and Television Arts") Fellow. He received the World Leaders Award for "Creative Genius" as the subject of a week-long "Homage" in Toronto, in October 2001. In 2004, he received the [Wilfred Owen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilfred_Owen "Wilfred Owen") Award for Poetry for his "lifelong contribution to literature, 'and specifically for his collection of poetry entitled *War*, published in 2003'". In March 2006, he was awarded the [Europe Theatre Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Europe_Theatre_Prize "Europe Theatre Prize") in recognition of lifetime achievements pertaining to drama and theatre. In conjunction with that award, the critic Michael Billington coordinated an international conference on Pinter: Passion, Poetry, Politics, including scholars and critics from Europe and the Americas, held in [Turin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turin "Turin"), Italy, from 10 to 14 March 2006.

In October 2008, the [Central School of Speech and Drama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_School_of_Speech_and_Drama "Central School of Speech and Drama") announced that Pinter had agreed to become its president and awarded him an [honorary fellowship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honorary_title_(academic) "Honorary title (academic)") at its graduation ceremony. On his appointment, Pinter commented: "I was a student at Central in 1950–51. I enjoyed my time there very much and I am delighted to become president of a remarkable institution." But he had to receive that honorary degree, his 20th, in absentia owing to ill health. His presidency of the school was brief; he died just two weeks after the graduation ceremony, on 24 December 2008.

In 2013, he was posthumously awarded the [Sretenje Order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sretenje_Order "Sretenje Order") of [Serbia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serbia "Serbia").

### Légion d'honneur

On 18 January 2007, French Prime Minister [Dominique de Villepin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominique_de_Villepin "Dominique de Villepin") presented Pinter with France's highest civil honour, the [Légion d'honneur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Légion_d'honneur "Légion d'honneur"), at a ceremony at the French embassy in London. De Villepin praised Pinter's poem "American Football" (1991) stating: "With its violence and its cruelty, it is for me one of the most accurate images of war, one of the most telling metaphors of the temptation of imperialism and violence." In response, Pinter praised France's opposition to the war in Iraq. M. de Villepin concluded: "The poet stands still and observes what doesn't deserve other men's attention. Poetry teaches us how to live and you, Harold Pinter, teach us how to live." He said that Pinter received the award particularly "because in seeking to capture all the facets of the human spirit, [Pinter's] works respond to the aspirations of the French public, and its taste for an understanding of man and of what is truly universal". Lawrence Pollard observed that "the award for the great playwright underlines how much Mr Pinter is admired in countries like France as a model of the uncompromising radical intellectual".

## Scholarly response

Some scholars and critics challenge the validity of Pinter's critiques of what he terms "the modes of thinking of those in power" or dissent from his retrospective viewpoints on his own work. In 1985, Pinter recalled that his early act of conscientious objection resulted from being "terribly disturbed as a young man by the Cold War. And McCarthyism ... A profound hypocrisy. 'They' the monsters, 'we' the good. In 1948, the Russian suppression of Eastern Europe was an obvious and brutal fact, but I felt very strongly then and feel as strongly now that we have an obligation to subject our own actions and attitudes to an equivalent critical and moral scrutiny." Scholars agree that Pinter's dramatic rendering of power relations results from this scrutiny.

Pinter's aversion to any censorship by "the authorities" is epitomised in Petey's line at the end of *The Birthday Party*. As the broken-down and reconstituted Stanley is being carted off by the figures of authority Goldberg and McCann, Petey calls after him, "Stan, don't let them tell you what to do!" Pinter told Gussow in 1988, "I've lived that line all my damn life. Never more than now." The example of Pinter's stalwart opposition to what he termed "the modes of thinking of those in power"—the "brick wall" of the "minds" perpetuating the "status quo"—infused the "vast political pessimism" that some academic critics may perceive in his artistic work, its "drowning landscape" of harsh contemporary realities, with some residual "hope for restoring the dignity of man."

As Pinter's long-time friend David Jones reminded analytically inclined scholars and dramatic critics, Pinter was one of the "great comic writers":

> The trap with Harold's work, for performers and audiences, is to approach it too earnestly or portentously. I have always tried to interpret his plays with as much humour and humanity as possible. There is always mischief lurking in the darkest corners. The world of *The Caretaker* is a bleak one, its characters damaged and lonely. But they are all going to survive. And in their dance to that end they show a frenetic vitality and a wry sense of the ridiculous that balance heartache and laughter. Funny, but not too funny. As Pinter wrote, back in 1960: "As far as I am concerned *The Caretaker* IS funny, up to a point. Beyond that point, it ceases to be funny, and it is because of that point that I wrote it."

His dramatic conflicts present serious implications for his characters and his audiences, leading to sustained inquiry about "the point" of his work and multiple "critical strategies" for developing interpretations and stylistic analyses of it.
