**Knut Hamsun** ([/ˈhɑːmsʊn/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English"); 4 August 1859 – 19 February 1952) was a Norwegian writer who was awarded the [1920 Nobel Prize in Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1920_Nobel_Prize_in_Literature "1920 Nobel Prize in Literature"). Hamsun's work spans more than 70 years and shows variation with regard to [consciousness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consciousness "Consciousness"), subject, [perspective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_of_view_(literature) "Point of view (literature)") and [environment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_environment "Natural environment"). He published more than 23 [novels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Novel "Novel"), a collection of [poetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poetry "Poetry"), some [short stories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Short_story "Short story") and [plays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Play_(theatre) "Play (theatre)"), a [travelogue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Travel_literature "Travel literature"), works of non-fiction and some [essays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Essay "Essay").

Hamsun is considered "one of the most influential and innovative literary stylists of the past hundred years" (*ca.* 1890–1990). He pioneered psychological literature with techniques of [stream of consciousness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stream_of_consciousness_(narrative_mode) "Stream of consciousness (narrative mode)") and [interior monologue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monologue "Monologue"), and influenced authors such as [Thomas Mann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Mann "Thomas Mann"), [Franz Kafka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_Kafka "Franz Kafka"), [Maxim Gorky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxim_Gorky "Maxim Gorky"), [Stefan Zweig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stefan_Zweig "Stefan Zweig"), [Henry Miller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Miller "Henry Miller"), [Hermann Hesse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann_Hesse "Hermann Hesse"), [John Fante](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Fante "John Fante"), [James Kelman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Kelman "James Kelman"), [Charles Bukowski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Bukowski "Charles Bukowski") and [Ernest Hemingway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_Hemingway "Ernest Hemingway"). [Isaac Bashevis Singer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Bashevis_Singer "Isaac Bashevis Singer") called Hamsun "the father of the modern school of literature in his every aspect—his subjectiveness, his fragmentariness, his use of flashbacks, his [lyricism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyricism "Lyricism"). The whole modern school of fiction in the twentieth century stems from Hamsun". Since 1916, several of Hamsun's works have been adapted into [motion pictures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motion_pictures "Motion pictures"). On 4 August 2009, the [Knut Hamsun Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knut_Hamsun_Centre "Knut Hamsun Centre") was opened in [Hamarøy Municipality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamarøy_Municipality "Hamarøy Municipality").

The young Hamsun objected to [realism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literary_realism "Literary realism") and [naturalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naturalism_(literature) "Naturalism (literature)"). He argued that the main object of [modernist literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modernist_literature "Modernist literature") should be the intricacies of the human mind, that writers should describe the "whisper of blood, and the pleading of bone marrow". Hamsun is considered the "leader of the Neo-Romantic revolt at the turn of the 20th century", with works such as *[Hunger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hunger_(Hamsun_novel) "Hunger (Hamsun novel)")* (1890), *[Mysteries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mysteries_(novel) "Mysteries (novel)")* (1892), *[Pan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pan_(Hamsun_novel) "Pan (Hamsun novel)")* (1894), and *[Victoria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victoria_(novel) "Victoria (novel)")* (1898). His later works—in particular his "[Nordland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nordland "Nordland") novels"—were influenced by the [Norwegian new realism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norwegian_new_realism "Norwegian new realism"), portraying everyday life in rural Norway and often employing local dialect, irony, and humour. Hamsun published only one poetry collection, *[The Wild Choir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Wild_Choir "The Wild Choir")*, which has been set to music by several composers.

Hamsun held strong [Anglophobic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglophobic "Anglophobic") views, and openly supported [Adolf Hitler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adolf_Hitler "Adolf Hitler") and [Nazi Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi_Germany "Nazi Germany"): he donated his [Nobel Prize medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_medal "Nobel Prize medal") to [Joseph Goebbels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Goebbels "Joseph Goebbels"), propaganda Minister for the [Third Reich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_Reich "Third Reich") and met Hitler during the [German occupation of Norway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_occupation_of_Norway "German occupation of Norway"). Due to his professed support for the occupation of Norway and the [Quisling regime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quisling_regime "Quisling regime"), he was charged with [treason](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treason "Treason") after the war. Due to alleged psychological problems and issues relating to old age, he was not convicted, but in 1948 he was heavily [fined](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fine_(penalty) "Fine (penalty)"). Hamsun's last book, *[On Overgrown Paths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_Overgrown_Paths "On Overgrown Paths")*, authored in semi-imprisonment in [Landvik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landvik_Municipality "Landvik Municipality"), concerned his postwar treatment and his rebuttal to accusations of mental ineptness.

## Biography

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/c/c0/Knut_Hamsun.jpeg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail_unscaled)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Knut_Hamsun.jpeg)

Hamsun in 1890, the year he published his first major work, *[Hunger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hunger_(Hamsun_novel) "Hunger (Hamsun novel)")*

### Early life

Knut Hamsun was born as Knud Pedersen in [Lom Municipality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lom_Municipality_(Norway) "Lom Municipality (Norway)") in the [Gudbrandsdalen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gudbrandsdalen "Gudbrandsdalen") valley, [Norway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norway "Norway"). He was the fourth son among the seven children of Tora Olsdatter and Peder Pedersen. When he was three, the family moved to Hamsund in [Hamarøy Municipality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamarøy_Municipality "Hamarøy Municipality") in [Nordland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nordland "Nordland") county. They were poor and an uncle had invited them to farm his land for him.

At nine Knut was separated from his family and lived with his uncle Hans Olsen, who needed help with the post office he ran. Olsen used to beat and starve his nephew, and Hamsun later stated that his chronic nervous difficulties were due to how his uncle treated him.

In 1874 he finally escaped back to Lom. For the next five years he did any job for money: shop assistant, peddler, shoemaker's apprentice, sheriff's assistant, and an elementary-school teacher.

At 17 he became a ropemaker's apprentice. At about the same time he started to write. He asked businessman [Erasmus Zahl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erasmus_Zahl "Erasmus Zahl") to give him monetary support, and he agreed. Hamsun later used Zahl as a model for the character *Mack* appearing in his novels *[Pan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pan_(Hamsun_novel) "Pan (Hamsun novel)")* (1894), *Dreamers* (1904), *Benoni* (1908) and *Rosa* (1908).

He spent several years in America, traveling and working at various jobs, and published in 1889 his impressions under the title *Fra det moderne Amerikas Aandsliv* ("From the Spiritual Life of Modern America").

### Married life

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/86/Portrett_av_Knut_Hamsun_med_familie%2C_Larvik%2C_1917.jpg/250px-Portrett_av_Knut_Hamsun_med_familie%2C_Larvik%2C_1917.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Portrett_av_Knut_Hamsun_med_familie,_Larvik,_1917.jpg)

Family portrait on the stairs of "Villa Havgløtt"; left to right: [Tore Hamsun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tore_Hamsun "Tore Hamsun"), [Marie Hamsun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marie_Hamsun "Marie Hamsun"), [Arild Hamsun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arild_Hamsun?action=edit&redlink=1 "Arild Hamsun"), Knut Hamsun and Ellinor Hamsun.

In 1898, Hamsun married Bergljot Bech (later Göpfert), who bore daughter Victoria, but the marriage ended in 1906. Hamsun then married [Marie Andersen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marie_Hamsun "Marie Hamsun") (1881–1969) in 1909 and she was his companion until the end of his life. They had four children: sons [Tore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tore_Hamsun "Tore Hamsun") and [Arild](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arild_Hamsun?action=edit&redlink=1 "Arild Hamsun") and daughters Ellinor and Cecilia.

Marie wrote about her life with Hamsun in two [memoirs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memoir "Memoir"). She was a promising actress when she met Hamsun but ended her career and traveled with him to Hamarøy. They bought a farm, planning "to earn their living as farmers, with his writing providing some additional income".

After a few years they moved south, to [Larvik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Larvik_(town) "Larvik (town)"). In 1918 they bought [Nørholm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nørholm_(Norway) "Nørholm (Norway)"), an old, somewhat dilapidated manor house in [Landvik Municipality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landvik_Municipality "Landvik Municipality"), between [Lillesand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lillesand_(town) "Lillesand (town)") and [Grimstad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grimstad_(town) "Grimstad (town)"). The main residence was restored and redecorated. Here Hamsun could occupy himself with writing undisturbed, although he often travelled to write in other cities and places, preferring spartan housing.

### Early literary career

[![Photogravure made after the portrait of Hamsun by Edvard Munch in the collection of the National Gallery of Art, Washington DC..](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/ad/After_Edvard_Munch%2C_Knut_Hamsun%2C_1896%2C_NGA_40158.jpg/250px-After_Edvard_Munch%2C_Knut_Hamsun%2C_1896%2C_NGA_40158.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:After_Edvard_Munch,_Knut_Hamsun,_1896,_NGA_40158.jpg)

After Edvard Munch, *Knut Hamsun*, 1896, photogravure, National Gallery of Art, Washington, Rosenwald Collection, 1951.10.360

In 1877, he published his first book: *Den Gaadefulde: En Kjærlighedshistorie fra Nordland* (*The Enigmatic Man: A Love Story from Northern Norway*). It was inspired by his odd job experiences and struggles.

In his second novel *Bjørger* (1878), he attempted to imitate [Bjørnstjerne Bjørnson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bjørnstjerne_Bjørnson "Bjørnstjerne Bjørnson")'s writing style. The melodramatic story follows a poet, Bjørger, and his love for Laura. This book was published under the pseudonym Knud Pedersen Hamsund. This book served as the basis for Hamsun's later novel *Victoria: En Kærligheds Historie* (1898; translated as *Victoria: A Love Story*, 1923).

As of 1898 Hamsun was among the contributors of *[Ringeren](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ringeren "Ringeren")*, a political and cultural magazine established by [Sigurd Ibsen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigurd_Ibsen "Sigurd Ibsen").

### Major works

Hamsun first received wide acclaim with his 1890 novel [*Hunger* (*Sult*)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hunger_(Hamsun_novel) "Hunger (Hamsun novel)"). The semiautobiographical work described a young writer's descent into near madness as a result of hunger and poverty in the Norwegian capital of [Kristiania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kristiania "Kristiania") (modern name [Oslo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oslo "Oslo")). To many, the novel presages the writings of [Franz Kafka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_Kafka "Franz Kafka") and other twentieth-century novelists with its [internal monologue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internal_monologue "Internal monologue") and bizarre logic.

A theme to which Hamsun often returned is that of the perpetual wanderer, an itinerant stranger (often the narrator) who insinuates himself into the life of small rural communities. This theme is central to the novels *[Mysteries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mysteries_(novel) "Mysteries (novel)")*, *[Pan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pan_(Hamsun_novel) "Pan (Hamsun novel)")*, *[Under the Autumn Star](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Under_the_Autumn_Star "Under the Autumn Star")*, *[The Last Joy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Last_Joy "The Last Joy")*, *Vagabonds*, *Rosa*, and others.

Hamsun's prose often contains rapturous depictions of the natural world, with intimate reflections on the Norwegian woodlands and coastline. For this reason, he has been linked with the spiritual movement known as [pantheism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pantheism "Pantheism") ("No one knows God," he once wrote, "man knows only gods."). Hamsun saw mankind and nature united in a strong, sometimes mystical bond. This connection between the characters and their natural environment is exemplified in the novels *Pan*, *A Wanderer Plays on Muted Strings*, and *[Growth of the Soil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Growth_of_the_Soil "Growth of the Soil")*, "his monumental work" credited with securing him the [Nobel Prize in Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Literature "Nobel Prize in Literature") in 1920.

### World War II, arrest and trial

As early as 1918 during [World War I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I "World War I"), Hamsun's pro-German attitude had been noted and *The Atlantic* speculated that the cause may have been "a love of opposition to average opinion." During [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II"), Hamsun supported the German war effort. He courted and met with high-ranking Nazi officers, including [Adolf Hitler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adolf_Hitler "Adolf Hitler"). Nazi Minister of Propaganda [Joseph Goebbels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Goebbels "Joseph Goebbels") wrote a long and enthusiastic diary entry concerning a private meeting with Hamsun; according to Goebbels, Hamsun's "faith in German victory is unshakable". In 1940 Hamsun wrote that "the Germans are fighting for us".

On 13 June 1945, after the war, he was detained by police for treason, then committed to a hospital in [Grimstad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grimstad_(town) "Grimstad (town)") (*[Grimstad sykehus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grimstad_sykehus?action=edit&redlink=1 "Grimstad sykehus")*) "due to his advanced age", according to [Einar Kringlen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Einar_Kringlen "Einar Kringlen"), a professor and medical doctor. In 1947 he was tried in Grimstad and fined. Norway's supreme court reduced the fine from 575,000 to 325,000 [Norwegian kroner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norwegian_krone "Norwegian krone").

After the war, Norwegians were torn between aversion to Hamsun's Nazi sympathies and regard for his achievements and fame as a writer. At his trial Hamsun had pleaded ignorance. Other explanations have cited his contradictory personality, his distaste for *[hoi polloi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoi_polloi "Hoi polloi")*, his inferiority complex, his distress at the spread of indiscipline, his dislike of Norway's interwar democracy, and especially his [Anglophobia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglophobia "Anglophobia").

### Death

Knut Hamsun died on 19 February 1952, aged 92, in Grimstad. His ashes are buried in the garden of his home at [Nørholm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nørholm_(Norway) "Nørholm (Norway)") in [Landvik Municipality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landvik_Municipality "Landvik Municipality") (now part of [Grimstad Municipality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grimstad_Municipality "Grimstad Municipality")).

## Style and themes

Scholar and translator [Sverre Lyngstad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sverre_Lyngstad "Sverre Lyngstad") explains that Hamsun had asserted in an 1890 article that for literature to accurately represent mental life, writers must explore "the [mimosa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mimosa "Mimosa")-like sensitivities of the psyche and the secret stirrings in the remote parts of the mind, the incalculable welter of emotions, the random wanderings of thought and feeling, the uncharted, trackless journeys of heart and brain, the mysterious activities of the nerves, the whisper of the blood, the entreaty of the bones, all the unconscious life of the mind."

At the time of composing *Mysteries* (1892), Hamsun in essays and lectures was criticizing the state of Norwegian literature and promoting a new form of unpredictable psychological writing. Some of Hamsun's critical views received hostile reactions at the time, and he continued his political and cultural stance in dialog of the fictional character Nagel (who for example calls [Ibsen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henrik_Ibsen "Henrik Ibsen")'s plays “dramatized wood pulp”), and used the book itself as an embodiment of his theory of literature. Hamsun had specifically criticized [stereotypes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stereotypes "Stereotypes") of elder authors and called for characters of "changeable and divided mind" in whom "inconsistency is their fundamental trait."

Along with [August Strindberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/August_Strindberg "August Strindberg"), [Henrik Ibsen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henrik_Ibsen "Henrik Ibsen"), and [Sigrid Undset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigrid_Undset "Sigrid Undset"), Hamsun formed a quartet of Scandinavian authors who became internationally known for their works. Hamsun pioneered psychological literature with techniques of [stream of consciousness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stream_of_consciousness_(narrative_mode) "Stream of consciousness (narrative mode)") and [interior monologue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monologue "Monologue"), as found in material by, for example, [Joyce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Joyce "James Joyce"), [Proust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcel_Proust "Marcel Proust"), [Mansfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Katherine_Mansfield "Katherine Mansfield") and [Woolf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virginia_Woolf "Virginia Woolf"). His writing also had a major influence on [Franz Kafka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_Kafka "Franz Kafka"). Hamsun's works were translated into [German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_language "German language") by [Cläre Mjøen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cläre_Mjøen "Cläre Mjøen").

[Walter Benjamin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Benjamin "Walter Benjamin") wrote in 1929, "it is well known that the Germanic languages are particularly rich in heroes who are windbags, wastrels, and thieves, and who in general have gone to the dogs. Knut Hamsun, a master of such
characters, has recently been discovered and celebrated."

## Reception

[Thomas Mann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Mann "Thomas Mann") described Hamsun as a "descendant of [Fyodor Dostoyevsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fyodor_Dostoyevsky "Fyodor Dostoyevsky") and [Friedrich Nietzsche](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Nietzsche "Friedrich Nietzsche")." [Arthur Koestler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Koestler "Arthur Koestler") was a fan of his love stories. [H.G. Wells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H.G._Wells "H.G. Wells") praised *[Growth of the Soil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Growth_of_the_Soil "Growth of the Soil")*, for which Hamsun was awarded the [Nobel Prize in Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Literature "Nobel Prize in Literature"). [Isaac Bashevis Singer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Bashevis_Singer "Isaac Bashevis Singer") was a fan of Hamsun's modern subjectivism, flashbacks, fragmentation, and lyricism. A character in [Charles Bukowski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Bukowski "Charles Bukowski")'s book *[Women](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women_(Bukowski_novel) "Women (Bukowski novel)")* referred to Hamsun as the greatest writer who ever lived.

Hamsun's works remain popular. In 2009, a Norwegian biographer stated, "We can't help loving him, though we have hated him all these years.... That's our Hamsun trauma. He's a ghost that won't stay in the grave."

Scholar Reinhard Friederich stated that Hamsun's [late-life political attitudes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knut_Hamsun#Racism_and_admiration_for_Hitler) caused discussion of his earlier literary reputation and influence to become reduced to shallow half-hearted mention.

## Legacy

A fifteen-volume edition of Hamsun's complete works was published in 1954. In 2009, to mark the 150th anniversary of his birth, a new 27-volume edition of his complete works was published, including short stories, poetry, plays, and articles not included in the 1954 edition. For this new edition, all of Hamsun's works underwent slight linguistic modifications to make them more accessible to contemporary Norwegian readers. New English translations of two of his major works, *Growth of the Soil* and *Pan*, were published in 1998.

Three of Hamsun's homes (Hamsund gård in [Hamarøy Municipality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamarøy_Municipality "Hamarøy Municipality"), Hamsunstugu in Garmo in [Lom Municipality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lom_Municipality_(Norway) "Lom Municipality (Norway)"), and [Nørholm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nørholm_(Norway) "Nørholm (Norway)") in [Grimstad Municipality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grimstad_Municipality "Grimstad Municipality")) are open to the public as museums, as is the [Knut Hamsun Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knut_Hamsun_Centre "Knut Hamsun Centre") in Hamarøy.

### Studies on Hamsun's writings

Hamsun's writings have been the subject of numerous books and journal articles. Some of these writings compare and contrast Hamsun's literary works with the political and cultural ideas expressed in his non-fiction.

Hamsun produced a voluminous correspondence during his lifetime. Norwegian scholar and Hamsun expert [Harald Næss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harald_Næss "Harald Næss") spent four decades tracking these letters down in both the United States and Europe, producing a collection of thousands of letters. He published a selection in various volumes between 1994 and 2000.

## Racism and admiration for Hitler

From his youth onward, Hamsun espoused [racist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racism "Racism") and [anti-egalitarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-egalitarian "Anti-egalitarian") beliefs. In The Cultural Life of Modern America (*Fra det moderne Amerikas Aandsliv*, 1889), he denounced [miscegenation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miscegenation "Miscegenation"): "The [Negros](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_Americans "African Americans") are and will remain Negros, a nascent human form the tropics, rudimentary organs on the body of white society. Instead of founding an intellectual elite, America has established a [mulatto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mulatto "Mulatto") studfarm."

Hamsun wrote several newspaper articles in the course of the Second World War, including his notorious 1940 assertion that "the Germans are fighting for us, and now are crushing England's tyranny over us and all neutrals". In 1943, he sent Germany's minister of [propaganda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propaganda "Propaganda") [Joseph Goebbels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Goebbels "Joseph Goebbels") his [Nobel Prize medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_medal "Nobel Prize medal") as a gift. Its whereabouts since then are unknown. Hamsun's biographer [Thorkild Hansen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thorkild_Hansen "Thorkild Hansen") interpreted this as part of the strategy to get an audience with [Hitler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adolf_Hitler "Adolf Hitler"). Hamsun was eventually invited to meet with Hitler; during the meeting, Hamsun complained about the German civilian administrator in Norway, [Josef Terboven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josef_Terboven "Josef Terboven") and asked that imprisoned Norwegian citizens be released, enraging Hitler. [Otto Dietrich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otto_Dietrich "Otto Dietrich") describes the meeting in his memoirs as the only time that another person was able to get a word in edgeways with Hitler. He attributes this to Hamsun's deafness. Regardless, Dietrich notes that it took Hitler three days to get over his anger. Hamsun also on other occasions helped Norwegians who had been imprisoned for resistance activities and tried to influence German policies in Norway.

Nevertheless, a week after Hitler's death, [Hamsun wrote a eulogy for him](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knut_Hamsun's_obituary_of_Adolf_Hitler "Knut Hamsun's obituary of Adolf Hitler"), saying "He was a warrior, a warrior for mankind, and a prophet of the gospel of justice for all nations." Following the end of the war, angry crowds [burned his books](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_burning "Book burning") in public in major Norwegian cities and Hamsun was confined for several months in a psychiatric hospital.

Hamsun was forced to undergo a psychiatric examination, which concluded that he had "permanently impaired mental faculties," and on that basis the charges of treason were dropped. Instead, a [civil liability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_liability "Civil liability") case was raised against him, and in 1948 he had to pay the ruinous sum of 325,000 [kroner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norwegian_krone "Norwegian krone") ($65,000 or £16,250 at that time) to the Norwegian government for his alleged membership in [Nasjonal Samling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nasjonal_Samling "Nasjonal Samling") and for the moral support he gave to the Germans, but was cleared of any direct Nazi affiliation. Whether he was a member of Nasjonal Samling or not and whether his mental abilities were impaired is debated even today. Hamsun stated he was never a member of any political party. He wrote his last book *Paa giengrodde Stier* (*[On Overgrown Paths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_Overgrown_Paths "On Overgrown Paths")*) in 1949, a book many take as evidence of his functioning mental capabilities. In it, he harshly criticizes the psychiatrists and the judges and, in his view, proves that he is not mentally ill.

Danish author [Thorkild Hansen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thorkild_Hansen "Thorkild Hansen") investigated the trial and wrote *The Hamsun Trial* (1978), which created a storm in Norway. Hansen viewed Hamsun's treatment as outrageous, writing, "If you want to meet idiots, go to Norway." In 1996, Swedish filmmaker [Jan Troell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jan_Troell "Jan Troell") based the movie *Hamsun* on Hansen's book. In *Hamsun*, Swedish actor [Max von Sydow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_von_Sydow "Max von Sydow") plays Knut Hamsun; his wife Marie is played by Danish actress [Ghita Nørby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghita_Nørby "Ghita Nørby").

## Film and TV adaptations

A [biopic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biopic "Biopic"), *[Hamsun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamsun_(film) "Hamsun (film)")*, was released in 1996, directed by [Jan Troell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jan_Troell "Jan Troell"). It stars [Max von Sydow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_von_Sydow "Max von Sydow") as Hamsun.

Hamsun's works have been the basis of 25 films and television mini-series adaptations, starting in 1916.

- 1922 - *[Pan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pan_(Hamsun_novel) "Pan (Hamsun novel)")* was the basis of a 1922 film, and three more adaptations later.
- 1966 film *[Hunger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hunger_(1966_film) "Hunger (1966 film)")*,starring Per Oscarsson, directed by [Henning Carlsen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henning_Carlsen "Henning Carlsen"). A Danish, Norwegian and Swedish coproduction. It is considered one of the best film adaptations of a Hamsun work.
- 1978 - [Mysteries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mysteries_(film)?action=edit&redlink=1 "Mysteries (film)") by the Dutch film company [Sigma Pictures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigma_Pictures?action=edit&redlink=1 "Sigma Pictures")), directed by [Paul de Lussanet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_de_Lussanet?action=edit&redlink=1 "Paul de Lussanet"), starring [Sylvia Kristel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sylvia_Kristel "Sylvia Kristel"), [Rutger Hauer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rutger_Hauer "Rutger Hauer"), [Andrea Ferreol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrea_Ferreol "Andrea Ferreol") and [Rita Tushingham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rita_Tushingham "Rita Tushingham"). Based on *[Mysteries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mysteries_(novel) "Mysteries (novel)")*
- 1990 - *[Landstrykere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landstrykere "Landstrykere")* (*Wayfarers*), a Norwegian film directed by Ola Solum.
- 1993 - *[The Telegraphist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Telegraphist "The Telegraphist")*, a Norwegian film directed by Erik Gustavson, based on the novel *[Dreamers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dreamers_(novel) "Dreamers (novel)")* (*Sværmere*, also published in English as *Mothwise*).
- 1995 - [film of the same name](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pan_(1995_film) "Pan (1995 film)"), a Danish production directed by [Henning Carlsen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henning_Carlsen "Henning Carlsen")

[Remodernist filmmaker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Remodernist_film "Remodernist film") [Jesse Richards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesse_Richards "Jesse Richards") has announced he is in preparations to direct an adaptation of Hamsun's short story *The Call of Life*.
