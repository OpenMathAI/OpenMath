**Pearl Comfort Sydenstricker Buck** (June 26, 1892 – March 6, 1973) was an American writer and humanitarian. She is best known for *[The Good Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Good_Earth "The Good Earth")*, the best-selling novel in the United States in 1931 and 1932, which won her the [Pulitzer Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulitzer_Prize_for_Fiction "Pulitzer Prize for Fiction") in 1932. In 1938, Buck became the first American woman to win the [Nobel Prize in Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1938_Nobel_Prize_in_Literature "1938 Nobel Prize in Literature") "for her rich and truly epic descriptions of [peasant life in China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rural_society_in_China "Rural society in China")" and for her "masterpieces", two memoir-biographies of her missionary parents.

Buck was born in West Virginia. She was an infant when, in October 1892, her family moved to China. As the daughter of [missionaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Missionary "Missionary") and later as a missionary herself, Buck spent most of her life before 1934 in [Zhenjiang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zhenjiang "Zhenjiang") and in [Nanjing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanjing "Nanjing") with her first husband. She and her parents spent their summers in a villa in [Kuling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuling,_Jiujiang "Kuling, Jiujiang"), [Mount Lu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Lu "Mount Lu"), [Jiujiang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jiujiang "Jiujiang"), and it was during this annual pilgrimage that the young girl decided to become a writer. She graduated from [Randolph-Macon Woman's College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Randolph-Macon_Woman's_College "Randolph-Macon Woman's College") in Lynchburg, Virginia, then returned to China. From 1914 to 1932, after marrying [John Lossing Buck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Lossing_Buck "John Lossing Buck"), she served as a Presbyterian missionary, but she came to doubt the need for foreign missions. Her views became controversial during the [Fundamentalist–modernist controversy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamentalist–modernist_controversy "Fundamentalist–modernist controversy"), leading to her resignation.

After returning to the United States in 1935, Buck married the publisher [Richard J. Walsh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_J._Walsh "Richard J. Walsh") and continued writing prolifically. She became an activist and prominent advocate of the [rights of women](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women's_rights "Women's rights") and [racial equality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minority_rights "Minority rights"), and wrote widely on [Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_culture "Chinese culture") and [Asian cultures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture_of_Asia "Culture of Asia"), becoming particularly well known for her efforts on behalf of Asian and [mixed-race adoption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interracial_adoption "Interracial adoption").

## Early life and education

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4a/Pearl_Buck_Birthplace_2.jpg/250px-Pearl_Buck_Birthplace_2.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Pearl_Buck_Birthplace_2.jpg)

The Stulting House at the [Pearl Buck Birthplace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pearl_Buck_Birthplace "Pearl Buck Birthplace") in [Hillsboro, West Virginia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hillsboro,_West_Virginia "Hillsboro, West Virginia")

Pearl Comfort Sydenstricker was born in [Hillsboro, West Virginia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hillsboro,_West_Virginia "Hillsboro, West Virginia"), to [Absalom Sydenstricker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absalom_Sydenstricker "Absalom Sydenstricker") (1852–1931) and Caroline Maude (Stulting) (1857–1921), of German and Dutch descent respectively. Her parents, [Southern Presbyterian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Southern_Presbyterian_Mission "American Southern Presbyterian Mission") [missionaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mission_(Christian) "Mission (Christian)"), were married on July 8, 1880, and moved to [China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/China "China") shortly thereafter, but returned to the United States for Pearl's birth. When Pearl was five months old, the family returned to China, living first in [Huai'an](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huai'an "Huai'an") and then in 1896 moving to [Zhenjiang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zhenjiang "Zhenjiang"), which was then known as Chingkiang in the [Chinese postal romanization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_postal_romanization "Chinese postal romanization") system, near the major city of [Nanjing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanjing "Nanjing"). In summer, she and her family spent time in [Kuling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuling,_Jiujiang "Kuling, Jiujiang"). Her father built a stone villa in Kuling in 1897, and lived there until his death in 1931. It was during this annual summer pilgrimage in Kuling that the young girl decided to become a writer.

Of her siblings who survived into adulthood, [Edgar Sydenstricker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edgar_Sydenstricker "Edgar Sydenstricker") had a distinguished career with the [U.S. Public Health Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Public_Health_Service "United States Public Health Service") and later the [Milbank Memorial Fund](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milbank_Quarterly "Milbank Quarterly"), and [Grace Sydenstricker Yaukey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grace_Sydenstricker_Yaukey?action=edit&redlink=1 "Grace Sydenstricker Yaukey") (1899–1994) wrote young adult books and books about Asia under the pen name Cornelia Spencer.

Pearl recalled in her memoir that she lived in "several worlds", one a "small, white, clean [Presbyterian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presbyterianism "Presbyterianism") world of my parents", and the other the "big, loving merry not-too-clean Chinese world", and there was no communication between them. The [Boxer Uprising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boxer_Uprising "Boxer Uprising") (1899–1901) greatly affected the family; their Chinese friends deserted them, and Western visitors decreased. Her father, convinced that no Chinese could wish him harm, stayed behind as the rest of the family went to [Shanghai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shanghai "Shanghai") for safety. A few years later, Buck was enrolled in Miss Jewell's School in Shanghai, and was dismayed at the [racist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racism "Racism") attitudes there of other students, few of whom could speak any Chinese. Both of her parents felt strongly that Chinese were their equals; they forbade the use of the word *heathen*, and she was raised in a bilingual environment: tutored in English by her mother, in the local dialect by her Chinese playmates, and in classical Chinese by a Chinese scholar named Mr. Kung. She also read voraciously, especially, in spite of her father's disapproval, the novels of [Charles Dickens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Dickens "Charles Dickens"), which she later said she read through once a year for the rest of her life.

In 1911, Buck left China to attend [Randolph-Macon Woman's College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Randolph-Macon_Woman's_College "Randolph-Macon Woman's College") in [Lynchburg, Virginia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lynchburg,_Virginia "Lynchburg, Virginia"), where she graduated [Phi Beta Kappa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phi_Beta_Kappa "Phi Beta Kappa") in 1914 and was a member of [Kappa Delta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kappa_Delta "Kappa Delta") sorority.

## Career

### China

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/27/Pearl_Buck.jpg/250px-Pearl_Buck.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Pearl_Buck.jpg)

Buck photographed in 1932, about the time *The Good Earth* was published

Although Buck had not intended to return to China, much less become a missionary, she quickly applied to the [Presbyterian Board](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presbyterian_Mission_Agency "Presbyterian Mission Agency") when her father wrote that her mother was seriously ill. In 1914, Buck returned to China. She married an [agricultural economist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agricultural_economics "Agricultural economics") missionary, [John Lossing Buck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Lossing_Buck "John Lossing Buck"), on May 13, 1917, and they moved to [Suzhou, Anhui](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suzhou,_Anhui "Suzhou, Anhui") Province, a small town on the [Huai River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huai_River "Huai River") (not to be confused with the better-known [Suzhou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suzhou,_Jiangsu "Suzhou, Jiangsu") in [Jiangsu Province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jiangsu_Province "Jiangsu Province")). This is the region she describes in her books *The Good Earth* and *Sons*.

From 1920 to 1933, the Bucks made their home in Nanjing, on the campus of the [University of Nanking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Nanking_(defunct_in_1952) "University of Nanking (defunct in 1952)"), where they both had teaching positions. She taught [English literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_literature "English literature") at this private, church-run university, and also at [Ginling College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ginling_College "Ginling College") and at the [National Central University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanjing_University#History_2 "Nanjing University"). In 1920, the Bucks had a daughter, Carol, who was afflicted with [phenylketonuria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phenylketonuria "Phenylketonuria") that left her severely [developmentally disabled](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Developmentally_disabled "Developmentally disabled"). Buck had to have a [hysterectomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hysterectomy "Hysterectomy") due to complications of Carol's birth, leaving her unable to have more biological children. In 1921, Buck's mother died of a tropical disease, [sprue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropical_sprue "Tropical sprue"), and shortly afterward her father moved in. In 1924, they left China for John Buck's year of sabbatical and returned to the United States for a short time, during which Pearl Buck earned a master's degree from [Cornell University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornell_University "Cornell University"). In 1925, the Bucks adopted a child named Janice (later surnamed Walsh). That autumn, they returned to China.

The tragedies and dislocations that Buck suffered in the 1920s reached a climax in March 1927, during the "[Nanking Incident](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanking_incident_of_1927 "Nanking incident of 1927")". In a confused battle involving elements of [Chiang Kai-shek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chiang_Kai-shek "Chiang Kai-shek")'s [Nationalist troops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Revolutionary_Army "National Revolutionary Army"), [Communist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communism_in_China "Communism in China") forces, and assorted warlords, several Westerners were murdered. Her father insisted that the family should stay in Nanjing until the battle reached the city, as he had in 1900 in the face of the Boxers. When violence broke out, a poor Chinese family invited them to hide in their hut while the family's house was looted. The family spent a day terrified and in hiding, after which American gunboats rescued them. They traveled to Shanghai and then sailed to Japan, where they stayed for a year, after which they moved back to Nanjing. Buck later said that this year in Japan showed her that not all Japanese were militarists. When she returned from Japan in late 1927, Buck devoted herself in earnest to the vocation of writing. Friendly relations with prominent Chinese writers of the time, such as [Xu Zhimo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xu_Zhimo "Xu Zhimo") and [Lin Yutang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lin_Yutang "Lin Yutang"), encouraged her to think of herself as a professional writer. She wanted to fulfill the ambitions denied to her mother, but she also needed money to support herself if she left her marriage, which had become increasingly lonely. Since the mission board could not provide it, she also needed money for Carol's specialized care.

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/3a/Richard_J._Walsh%2C_portrait_photograph_agc.7a13018.jpg/250px-Richard_J._Walsh%2C_portrait_photograph_agc.7a13018.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Richard_J._Walsh,_portrait_photograph_agc.7a13018.jpg)

Buck married her publisher, [Richard J. Walsh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_J._Walsh "Richard J. Walsh"), the same day she divorced [John Lossing Buck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Lossing_Buck "John Lossing Buck").

Buck traveled once more to the United States in 1929 to find long-term care for Carol, eventually placing her in the [Vineland Training School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vineland_Training_School "Vineland Training School") in New Jersey. Buck served on the board of trustees for the school, where Carol would live until she died in 1992 at age 72. While Buck was in the United States, Richard J. Walsh, editor at [John Day publishers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Day_Company "John Day Company") in New York, accepted her novel *[East Wind: West Wind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Wind:_West_Wind "East Wind: West Wind").* She and Walsh began a relationship that would eventually lead to marriage and many years of professional collaboration.

Back in Nanking, Buck retreated every morning to the attic of her university house, and within the year, completed the manuscript for *[The Good Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Good_Earth "The Good Earth")*. She was involved in the charity relief campaign for the victims of the [1931 China floods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1931_China_floods "1931 China floods"), writing a series of short stories describing the plight of refugees, which were broadcast on the radio in the United States and later published in her collected volume *The First Wife and Other Stories*.

When her husband took the family to [Ithaca, New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ithaca,_New_York "Ithaca, New York"), the following year, Buck accepted an invitation to address a luncheon of Presbyterian women at the [Hotel Astor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hotel_Astor_(New_York_City) "Hotel Astor (New York City)") in New York City. Her talk was titled "Is There a Case for the Foreign Missionary?" and her answer was a barely qualified "no". She told her American audience that she welcomed Chinese to share her Christian faith, but argued that China did not need an institutional church dominated by missionaries who were too often ignorant of China and arrogant in their attempts to control it. When the talk was published in *[Harper's Magazine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harper's_Magazine "Harper's Magazine")*, the scandalized reaction led Buck to resign from the Presbyterian Board. In 1934, Buck left China, believing she would return, while her husband remained.

### United States

Buck divorced her husband John in [Reno, Nevada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reno,_Nevada "Reno, Nevada"), on June 11, 1935, and she married Richard Walsh that same day. He reportedly offered her advice and affection that, her biographer concludes, "helped make Pearl's prodigious activity possible". The couple moved with Janice to [Green Hills Farm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pearl_S._Buck_House_National_Historic_Landmark "Pearl S. Buck House National Historic Landmark") in [Bucks County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bucks_County,_Pennsylvania "Bucks County, Pennsylvania"), [Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pennsylvania "Pennsylvania"), which they quickly set about filling with adopted children. Two sons were brought home as infants in 1936 and followed by another son and daughter in 1937.

After the [Communist Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_Communist_Revolution "Chinese Communist Revolution") in 1949, Buck was repeatedly refused all attempts to return to her beloved China. Her 1962 novel *[Satan Never Sleeps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satan_Never_Sleeps "Satan Never Sleeps")* is heavily anti-communist and filled with religious themes, and was adapted into a film in the same year. During the [Cultural Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultural_Revolution "Cultural Revolution"), Buck, as a preeminent American writer of Chinese village life, was denounced as an "American [cultural imperialist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultural_imperialism "Cultural imperialism")". Buck was "heartbroken" when she was prevented from visiting China with [Richard Nixon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Nixon "Richard Nixon") in 1972, reportedly due to political interference by [Jiang Qing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jiang_Qing "Jiang Qing"), a prominent figure in the denunciation of Buck.

### Nobel Prize in Literature

In 1938, the [Nobel Prize committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Committee_for_Literature "Nobel Committee for Literature") wrote:

> By awarding this year's Prize to Pearl Buck for the notable works which pave the way to a human sympathy passing over widely separated racial boundaries and for the studies of human ideals which are a great and living art of portraiture, the Swedish Academy feels that it acts in harmony and accord with the aim of Alfred Nobel's dreams for the future.

In her speech to the academy, Buck took as her topic "The Chinese Novel". She explained, "I am an American by birth and by ancestry", but "my earliest knowledge of story, of how to tell and write stories, came to me in China." After an extensive discussion of classic Chinese novels, especially *[Romance of the Three Kingdoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romance_of_the_Three_Kingdoms "Romance of the Three Kingdoms")*, *[All Men Are Brothers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_Margin "Water Margin")*, and *[Dream of the Red Chamber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_of_the_Red_Chamber "Dream of the Red Chamber")*, she concluded that in China "the novelist did not have the task of creating art but of speaking to the people." Her own ambition, she continued, had not been trained toward "the beauty of letters or the grace of art." In China, the task of the novelist differed from the Western artist: "To farmers he must talk of their land, and to old men he must speak of peace, and to old women he must tell of their children, and to young men and women he must speak of each other." And like the Chinese novelist, she concluded, "I have been taught to want to write for these people. If they are reading their magazines by the million, then I want my stories there rather than in magazines read only by a few."

### Humanitarian efforts

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/70/Pearl_Buck_and_Swedish_King.gif/250px-Pearl_Buck_and_Swedish_King.gif?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Pearl_Buck_and_Swedish_King.gif)

Pearl S. Buck receives the Nobel Prize for Literature from [King Gustav V of Sweden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gustaf_V "Gustaf V") in the Stockholm Concert Hall in 1938

Buck was committed to a range of issues that were largely ignored by her generation. Many of her life experiences and political views are described in her novels, short stories, fiction, children's stories, and the biographies of her parents entitled *[Fighting Angel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fighting_Angel "Fighting Angel")* (on Absalom) and *[The Exile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Exile_(Buck_book) "The Exile (Buck book)")* (on Carrie). She wrote on diverse subjects, including [women's rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women's_rights "Women's rights"), Asian cultures, immigration, adoption, missionary work, war, the [atomic bomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_bomb "Atomic bomb") (*Command the Morning*), and violence. Long before it was considered fashionable or politically safe to do so, Buck challenged the American public by raising consciousness on topics such as racism, sex discrimination and the plight of Asian [war children](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_children "War children"). Buck combined the careers of wife, mother, author, editor, international spokesperson, and political activist. Buck became well known as an advocate for civil rights, women's rights, and [disability rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disability_rights_movement "Disability rights movement").

In 1949, after finding that existing adoption services considered Asian and [mixed-race](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiracial "Multiracial") children unadoptable, Buck founded the first permanent [foster home](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foster_home "Foster home") for US-born mixed-race children of Asian descent, naming it The Welcome Home. The foster home was located in a 16-room farmhouse in Pennsylvania next door to Buck's own home, Green Hill Farm, and Buck was actively involved in everything from planning the children's diets to buying their clothing. Among the home's Board of Directors were librettist [Oscar Hammerstein II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oscar_Hammerstein_II "Oscar Hammerstein II") and his second wife, interior designer [Dorothy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dorothy_Hammerstein "Dorothy Hammerstein"), composer [Richard Rodgers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Rodgers "Richard Rodgers"), seed company tycoon [David Burpee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Burpee "David Burpee") and his wife Lois and author [James A. Michener](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_A._Michener "James A. Michener"). As more and more children were referred to the foster home, however, it quickly became apparent that it couldn't accommodate them all and adoptive homes were needed. Welcome Home was turned into the first international, [interracial adoption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interracial_adoption "Interracial adoption") agency, and Buck began actively promoting the adoption of mixed-race children to the American public. In an effort to overcome the longstanding public view that such children were inferior and undesirable, Buck claimed in interviews and speeches that "hybrid" children of interracial backgrounds were actually genetically superior to other children in terms of intelligence and health. She and her husband Richard then adopted two mixed-race daughters from overseas themselves: an Afro-German girl in 1951 and an Afro-Japanese girl in 1957, giving her eight children in total. In 1967 she turned over most of her earnings—more than $7 million— to the adoption agency to help with costs.

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7a/Smithsonian_-_NPG_-_Pearl_S._Buck_-_NPG.87.168.jpg/250px-Smithsonian_-_NPG_-_Pearl_S._Buck_-_NPG.87.168.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Smithsonian_-_NPG_-_Pearl_S._Buck_-_NPG.87.168.jpg)

Portrait of Buck by Samuel Johnson Woolf

Buck established the Pearl S. Buck Foundation (name changed to Pearl S. Buck International in 1999) to "address poverty and discrimination faced by children in Asian countries." In 1964, she opened the Opportunity Center and Orphanage in South Korea, and later offices were opened in Thailand, the Philippines, and Vietnam. When establishing Opportunity House, Buck said, "The purpose ... is to publicize and eliminate injustices and prejudices suffered by children, who, because of their birth, are not permitted to enjoy the educational, social, economic and civil privileges normally accorded to children." In 1960, after a long decline in health that included a series of [strokes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stroke "Stroke"), Buck's husband Richard Walsh died. She renewed a warm relationship with [William Ernest Hocking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Ernest_Hocking "William Ernest Hocking"), who died in 1966. Buck then withdrew from many of her old friends and quarreled with others.

In 1962 Buck asked the Israeli Government for [clemency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clemency "Clemency") for [Adolf Eichmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adolf_Eichmann "Adolf Eichmann"), the [Nazi war criminal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_crimes_of_the_Wehrmacht "War crimes of the Wehrmacht") who was one of the main architects of the murder of six million Jews during World War II, as she and others believed that carrying out [capital punishment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capital_punishment "Capital punishment") against Eichmann could be seen as an act of vengeance, especially since the war had ended.

During a December 17, 1962 visit to the Kennedy White House, Buck urged the Kennedy administration to help resolve [People's Republic of China-Taiwan relations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross-Strait_relations "Cross-Strait relations") by supporting de facto independence of Taiwan for a 10 to 25-year period with an agreement that afterwards a plebiscite could be held based on a negotiated settlement.

Buck's ties with her native state remained strong. In the title essay of My Mother's House, a small book written by Buck and others to help raise funds for the Birthplace Museum, she paid tribute to the house her mother had cherished while living far away: "For me it was a living heart in the country I knew was my own but which was strange to me until I returned to the house where I was born." In the late 1960s, Buck toured West Virginia to raise money to preserve her family farm in [Hillsboro, West Virginia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hillsboro,_West_Virginia "Hillsboro, West Virginia"). Today the [Pearl S. Buck Birthplace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pearl_S._Buck_Birthplace "Pearl S. Buck Birthplace") is a [historic house museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historic_house_museum "Historic house museum") and cultural center. She hoped the house would "belong to everyone who cares to go there," and serve as a "gateway to new thoughts and dreams and ways of life." Former U.S. President [George H. W. Bush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_H._W._Bush "George H. W. Bush") toured the Pearl S. Buck House in October 1998. He expressed that he, like millions of other Americans, had gained an appreciation for the Chinese people through Buck's writing.

## Final years and death

In the mid-1960s, Buck increasingly came under the influence of Theodore Harris, a former dance instructor, who became her confidant, co-author, and financial advisor. She soon depended on him for all her daily routines, and placed him in control of Welcome House and the Pearl S. Buck Foundation. Harris, who was given a lifetime salary as head of the foundation, created a scandal for Buck when he was accused of mismanaging the foundation, diverting large amounts of the foundation's funds for his friends' and his own personal expenses, and treating staff poorly. Buck defended Harris, stating that he was "very brilliant, very high strung and artistic." Before her death, Buck signed over her foreign royalties and her personal possessions to Creativity Inc., a foundation controlled by Harris.

Pearl S. Buck died of [lung cancer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lung_cancer "Lung cancer") on March 6, 1973, in [Danby, Vermont](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danby,_Vermont "Danby, Vermont"). She was interred on [Green Hills Farm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_Hills_Farm "Green Hills Farm") in [Perkasie, Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perkasie,_Pennsylvania "Perkasie, Pennsylvania"). She designed her own tombstone. Her name was not inscribed in English on her tombstone. Instead, the grave marker is inscribed with the Chinese characters **賽珍珠** ([pinyin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pinyin "Pinyin"): *Sài Zhēnzhū*) in [seal script](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seal_script "Seal script"), representing the name "Pearl Sydenstricker", specifically, *Sài* is the Sinicised first syllable of her surname (Chinese family names come first), and *Zhēnzhū* is the Mandarin word for "pearl".

Buck left behind three contradictory wills, resulting in a three-way legal dispute over her estate between her financial advisor Theodore Harris, the nonprofit Pearl Buck Foundation, and her seven adopted children. After a six-year battle, the dispute was settled in her children's favor after both Harris and the Pearl Buck Foundation dropped their claims (the latter in return for a financial settlement from Buck's children).

## Legacy

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/11/Pearl_Buck_Former_Residence_in_Nanjing_University.jpg/250px-Pearl_Buck_Former_Residence_in_Nanjing_University.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Pearl_Buck_Former_Residence_in_Nanjing_University.jpg)

Pearl S. Buck's former residence at [Nanjing University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanjing_University "Nanjing University")

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b2/Statue_of_Pearl_S._Buck.jpg/250px-Statue_of_Pearl_S._Buck.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Statue_of_Pearl_S._Buck.jpg)

A statue of Pearl S. Buck stands in front of the former residence at Nanjing University

Many contemporary reviewers praised Buck's "beautiful prose", even though her "style is apt to degenerate into over-repetition and confusion". [Robert Benchley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Benchley "Robert Benchley") wrote a parody of *The Good Earth* that emphasised these qualities. Peter Conn, in his biography of Buck, argues that despite the accolades awarded to her, Buck's contribution to literature has been mostly forgotten or deliberately ignored by America's cultural gatekeepers. Kang Liao argues that Buck played a "pioneering role in demythologizing China and the Chinese people in the American mind". [Phyllis Bentley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phyllis_Bentley "Phyllis Bentley"), in an overview of Buck's work published in 1935, was altogether impressed: "But we may say at least that for the interest of her chosen material, the sustained high level of her technical skill, and the frequent universality of her conceptions, Mrs. Buck is entitled to take rank as a considerable artist. To read her novels is to gain not merely knowledge of China but wisdom about life." These works aroused considerable popular sympathy for China, and helped foment a more critical view of Japan and its aggression.

Chinese-American author [Anchee Min](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anchee_Min "Anchee Min") said she "broke down and sobbed" after reading *The Good Earth* for the first time as an adult, which she had been forbidden to read growing up in China during the [Cultural Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultural_Revolution "Cultural Revolution"). Min said Buck portrayed the Chinese peasants "with such love, affection and humanity" and it inspired Min's novel *[Pearl of China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pearl_of_China "Pearl of China")* (2010).

In 1973, Buck was inducted into the [National Women's Hall of Fame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Women's_Hall_of_Fame "National Women's Hall of Fame"). Buck was honored in 1983 with a 5¢ Great Americans series [postage stamp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postage_stamp "Postage stamp") issued by the [United States Postal Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Postal_Service "United States Postal Service") In 1999 she was designated a [Women's History Month](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women's_History_Month "Women's History Month") Honoree by the [National Women's History Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Women's_History_Project "National Women's History Project").

Buck's former residence at [Nanjing University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanjing_University "Nanjing University") is now the **Pearl S. Buck Memorial House** ([Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_Chinese_characters "Traditional Chinese characters"): 賽珍珠紀念館; [pinyin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pinyin "Pinyin"): *Sai Zhenzhu Jinianguan*) along the West Wall of the university's north campus.

Pearl Buck's papers and literary manuscripts are currently housed at Pearl S. Buck International and the [West Virginia & Regional History Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Virginia_&_Regional_History_Center "West Virginia & Regional History Center").
