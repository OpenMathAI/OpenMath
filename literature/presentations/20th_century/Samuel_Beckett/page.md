**Samuel Barclay Beckett** ([/ˈbɛkɪt/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English") [ⓘ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:En-us-Samuel_Barclay_Beckett.oga "File:En-us-Samuel Barclay Beckett.oga"); 13 April 1906 – 22 December 1989) was an Irish playwright, novelist, and poet. Written in both English and French, his literary and theatrical works feature bleak, impersonal, and [tragicomic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tragicomedy "Tragicomedy") episodes of life, coupled with [black comedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_comedy "Black comedy") and [literary nonsense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literary_nonsense "Literary nonsense"). As a key proponent of [Irish literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_literature "Irish literature"), he is best known for his [tragicomedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tragicomedy "Tragicomedy") play *[Waiting for Godot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waiting_for_Godot "Waiting for Godot")* (1953), and is widely regarded as one of the most influential and important writers of the 20th century, credited with transforming modern [theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre "Theatre"). For his foundational contribution to both literature and theatre, Beckett received the [1969 Nobel Prize in Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1969_Nobel_Prize_in_Literature "1969 Nobel Prize in Literature"), "for his writing, which—in new forms for the novel and drama—in the destitution of modern man acquires its elevation."

During his early career, Beckett worked as a literary critic and commentator, and in 1930 he took up a role as a lecturer in Dublin. He wrote his first novel *[Dream of Fair to Middling Women](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_of_Fair_to_Middling_Women "Dream of Fair to Middling Women")* in 1932, which influenced many of his later works, but it was not published until after his death. Around this time, Beckett also began studying artistic expressions and art history, particularly of paintings displayed at the [National Gallery of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Gallery_of_Ireland "National Gallery of Ireland"). He was a close friend of the Irish writer [James Joyce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Joyce "James Joyce"), and cited him as a major inspiration for his works. As a resident of Paris for most of his adult life, Beckett wrote in both French and English, sometimes under the pseudonym Andrew Belis. His later literary works, especially his plays, became increasingly austere and [minimalistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimalism "Minimalism") as his career progressed, involving more aesthetic and linguistic experimentation, with techniques of [stream of consciousness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stream_of_consciousness "Stream of consciousness"), repetition and [self-reference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-reference "Self-reference"). During the [Second World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_World_War "Second World War"), Beckett became a member of the [French Resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Resistance "French Resistance") group Gloria SMH ([Réseau Gloria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Réseau_Gloria "Réseau Gloria")) and was awarded the [Croix de Guerre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Croix_de_Guerre_1939–1945 "Croix de Guerre 1939–1945") in 1945.

His works were well received by critics and theatre audiences during his own lifetime, and his career spanned both Ireland and France, with short stints in Germany and Italy. During these terms, Beckett collaborated with many leading actors, actresses and theatre directors for his plays. Beckett's works are known for their [existential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Existential "Existential") themes, and these made them an important part of [20th-century plays and dramas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twentieth-century_theatre "Twentieth-century theatre"). In 1961, he shared the inaugural [Prix International](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prix_Formentor "Prix Formentor") with [Jorge Luis Borges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jorge_Luis_Borges "Jorge Luis Borges"). He was also the first [Saoi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saoi "Saoi") of the [Aosdána](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aosdána "Aosdána"), having been elected to the position in 1984.

Beckett is considered to be one of the last [modernist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modernism "Modernism") writers and a key figure in what [Martin Esslin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Esslin "Martin Esslin") called the "[Theatre of the Absurd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_of_the_Absurd "Theatre of the Absurd")." He died in 1989 and was buried at the [Cimetière du Montparnasse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cimetière_du_Montparnasse "Cimetière du Montparnasse"). He posthumously become one of the most prized and widely discussed Irish authors, and several landmarks have been named in his honor, with the most notable being the [Samuel Beckett Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Beckett_Bridge "Samuel Beckett Bridge") in Dublin. His best-known play, *Waiting for Godot*, has since become a centrepiece of modernist literature, and in a public poll by London's [Royal National Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_National_Theatre "Royal National Theatre") in 1998, it was voted as "the most significant English-language play of the 20th century."

## Early life

Samuel Barclay Beckett was born on 13 April 1906 in [Foxrock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foxrock "Foxrock"), a suburb of [Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin "Dublin"), to William Frank Beckett (1871–1933), a [quantity surveyor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantity_surveyor "Quantity surveyor") of French [Huguenot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huguenots "Huguenots") descent, and Maria Jones Roe, an [Irish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_people "Irish people") nurse. His parents were both 35 when he was born, and had married in 1901. Beckett had one elder brother named Frank Edward (1902–1954). At the age of five, he attended a local playschool in Dublin, where he started to learn music, and then moved to Earlsfort House School near [Harcourt Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harcourt_Street "Harcourt Street") in Dublin. The Becketts were members of the [Church of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_Ireland "Church of Ireland"); raised as an [Anglican](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglicanism "Anglicanism"), Beckett later became [agnostic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agnosticism "Agnosticism"), a perspective which informed his writing.

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/9b/House_39_-_1.jpg/250px-House_39_-_1.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:House_39_-_1.jpg)

Beckett's residence at Trinity College Dublin, pictured in 2021

Beckett's family home, Cooldrinagh, was a large house and garden complete with a tennis court built in 1903 by Beckett's father. The house and garden, its surrounding countryside where he often went walking with his father, the nearby [Leopardstown Racecourse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leopardstown_Racecourse "Leopardstown Racecourse"), the Foxrock railway station, and [Harcourt Street station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harcourt_Street_station "Harcourt Street station") would all feature in his prose and plays.

Around 1919 or 1920, he went to [Portora Royal School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portora_Royal_School "Portora Royal School") in [Enniskillen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enniskillen "Enniskillen"), which [Oscar Wilde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oscar_Wilde "Oscar Wilde") had also attended. He left in 1923 and entered [Trinity College Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College_Dublin "Trinity College Dublin"), where he studied [modern literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literary_modernism "Literary modernism") and Romance languages, and received his bachelor's degree in 1927. A natural athlete, he excelled at [cricket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cricket "Cricket") as a left-handed batsman and a left-arm medium-pace [bowler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bowling_(cricket) "Bowling (cricket)"). Later, he played for [Dublin University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_University_Cricket_Club "Dublin University Cricket Club") and played two [first-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First-class_cricket "First-class cricket") games against [Northamptonshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northamptonshire_County_Cricket_Club "Northamptonshire County Cricket Club"). As a result, he became the only Nobel literature laureate to have played first-class cricket and thus to appear in [Wisden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wisden_Cricketers'_Almanack "Wisden Cricketers' Almanack").

## Early writings

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c3/Walk_Samuel_Beckett%2C_Paris_%28France%29_-_Philippe_Binant_Archives.JPG/250px-Walk_Samuel_Beckett%2C_Paris_%28France%29_-_Philippe_Binant_Archives.JPG?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Walk_Samuel_Beckett,_Paris_(France)_-_Philippe_Binant_Archives.JPG)

Samuel Beckett Walk in Paris

Beckett studied French, Italian, and English at [Trinity College Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College_Dublin "Trinity College Dublin") from 1923 to 1927 (one of his tutors – not a teaching role in TCD – was the [Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Berkeley "George Berkeley") scholar [A. A. Luce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A._A._Luce "A. A. Luce"), who introduced him to the work of [Henri Bergson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_Bergson "Henri Bergson")). He was [elected a Scholar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Scholars_of_Trinity_College_Dublin "List of Scholars of Trinity College Dublin") in Modern Languages in 1926. Beckett graduated with a [BA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_Arts "Bachelor of Arts") and, after teaching briefly at [Campbell College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Campbell_College "Campbell College") in [Belfast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belfast "Belfast"), took up the post of *lecteur d'anglais* at the [École Normale Supérieure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/École_Normale_Supérieure "École Normale Supérieure") in Paris from November 1928 to 1930. While there, he was introduced to Irish author [James Joyce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Joyce "James Joyce") by [Thomas MacGreevy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_MacGreevy "Thomas MacGreevy"), a poet and close confidant of Beckett who also worked there. This meeting had a profound effect on the young man. Beckett assisted Joyce in various ways, one of which was research towards the book that became *[Finnegans Wake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finnegans_Wake "Finnegans Wake")*.

In 1929, Beckett published his first work, a critical essay titled "Dante... Bruno. Vico.. Joyce". The essay defends Joyce's work and method, chiefly from allegations of wanton obscurity and dimness, and was Beckett's contribution to *[Our Exagmination Round His Factification for Incamination of Work in Progress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Our_Exagmination_Round_His_Factification_for_Incamination_of_Work_in_Progress "Our Exagmination Round His Factification for Incamination of Work in Progress")* (a book of essays on Joyce which also included contributions by [Eugene Jolas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugene_Jolas "Eugene Jolas"), [Robert McAlmon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_McAlmon "Robert McAlmon"), and [William Carlos Williams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Carlos_Williams "William Carlos Williams")). Beckett's close relationship with Joyce and his family cooled, however, when he rejected the advances of Joyce's daughter [Lucia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucia_Joyce "Lucia Joyce"). Beckett's first short story, "Assumption", was published in Jolas's periodical [*transition*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transition_(literary_journal) "Transition (literary journal)"). The next year he won a small literary prize for his hastily composed poem "Whoroscope", which draws on a biography of [René Descartes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/René_Descartes "René Descartes") that Beckett happened to be reading when he was encouraged to submit.

In 1930, Beckett returned to Trinity College as a lecturer. In November 1930, he presented a paper in French to the Modern Languages Society of Trinity on the [Toulouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toulouse "Toulouse") poet Jean du Chas, founder of a movement called *le Concentrisme*. It was a literary parody, for Beckett had in fact invented the poet and his movement that claimed to be "at odds with all that is clear and distinct in [Descartes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Descartes "Descartes")". Beckett later insisted that he had not intended to fool his audience. When Beckett resigned from Trinity at the end of 1931, his brief academic career was at an end. He commemorated it with the poem "Gnome", which was inspired by his reading of [Johann Wolfgang Goethe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Wolfgang_Goethe "Johann Wolfgang Goethe")'s *[Wilhelm Meister's Apprenticeship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilhelm_Meister's_Apprenticeship "Wilhelm Meister's Apprenticeship")* and eventually published in *[The Dublin Magazine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Dublin_Magazine "The Dublin Magazine")* in 1934:

> Spend the years of learning squandering
> Courage for the years of wandering
> Through a world politely turning
> From the loutishness of learning

Beckett travelled throughout Europe. He spent some time in London, where in 1931 he published *[Proust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proust_(Beckett_essay) "Proust (Beckett essay)")*, his critical study of French author [Marcel Proust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcel_Proust "Marcel Proust"). Two years later, following his father's death, he began two years' treatment with [Tavistock Clinic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tavistock_Clinic "Tavistock Clinic") psychoanalyst [Dr. Wilfred Bion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilfred_Bion "Wilfred Bion"). Aspects of it became evident in Beckett's later works, such as *[Watt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watt_(novel) "Watt (novel)")* and *[Waiting for Godot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waiting_for_Godot "Waiting for Godot")*. In 1932, he wrote his first novel, *[Dream of Fair to Middling Women](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dream_of_Fair_to_Middling_Women "Dream of Fair to Middling Women")*, but after many rejections from publishers decided to abandon it (it was eventually published in 1992). Despite his inability to get it published, however, the novel served as a source for many of Beckett's early poems, as well as for his first full-length book, the 1933 [short-story](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Short_story "Short story") collection *[More Pricks Than Kicks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/More_Pricks_Than_Kicks "More Pricks Than Kicks")*.

Beckett published essays and reviews, including "Recent Irish Poetry" (in *[The Bookman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Bookman_(literary_magazine) "The Bookman (literary magazine)")*, August 1934) and "Humanistic Quietism", a review of his friend Thomas MacGreevy's *Poems* (in *[The Dublin Magazine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Dublin_Magazine "The Dublin Magazine")*, July–September 1934). They focused on the work of MacGreevy, [Brian Coffey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brian_Coffey "Brian Coffey"), [Denis Devlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denis_Devlin "Denis Devlin") and [Blanaid Salkeld](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blanaid_Salkeld "Blanaid Salkeld"), despite their slender achievements at the time, comparing them favourably with their [Celtic Revival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celtic_Revival "Celtic Revival") contemporaries and invoking [Ezra Pound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ezra_Pound "Ezra Pound"), [T. S. Eliot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T._S._Eliot "T. S. Eliot"), and the [French symbolists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbolism_(arts) "Symbolism (arts)") as their precursors. In describing these poets as forming "the nucleus of a living poetic in Ireland", Beckett was tracing the outlines of an Irish poetic [modernist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modernism "Modernism") canon.

In 1935 – the year that he successfully published a book of his poetry, *Echo's Bones and Other Precipitates* – Beckett worked on his novel *[Murphy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Murphy_(novel) "Murphy (novel)")*. In May, he wrote to MacGreevy that he had been reading about film and wished to go to Moscow to study with [Sergei Eisenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sergei_Eisenstein "Sergei Eisenstein") at the [Gerasimov Institute of Cinematography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerasimov_Institute_of_Cinematography "Gerasimov Institute of Cinematography"). In mid-1936 he wrote to Eisenstein and [Vsevolod Pudovkin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vsevolod_Pudovkin "Vsevolod Pudovkin") to offer himself as their apprentice. Nothing came of this, however, as Beckett's letter was lost owing to Eisenstein's quarantine during the [smallpox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smallpox "Smallpox") outbreak, as well as his focus on a script re-write of his postponed film production. In 1936, a friend had suggested he look up the works of [Arnold Geulincx](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arnold_Geulincx "Arnold Geulincx"), which Beckett did and he took many notes. The philosopher's name is mentioned in *Murphy* and the reading apparently left a strong impression. *Murphy* was finished in 1936 and Beckett departed for extensive travel around Germany, during which time he filled several notebooks with lists of noteworthy artwork that he had seen and noted his distaste for the [Nazi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi_Germany "Nazi Germany") savagery that was overtaking the country. Returning to Ireland briefly in 1937, he oversaw the publication of *Murphy* (1938), which he translated into French the following year. He fell out with his mother, which contributed to his decision to settle permanently in Paris. Beckett remained in Paris following the outbreak of [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II") in 1939, preferring, in his own words, "France at war to Ireland at peace". His was soon a known face in and around [Left Bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rive_Gauche "Rive Gauche") cafés, where he strengthened his allegiance with Joyce and forged new ones with artists [Alberto Giacometti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alberto_Giacometti "Alberto Giacometti") and [Marcel Duchamp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcel_Duchamp "Marcel Duchamp"), with whom he regularly played [chess](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chess "Chess"). Sometime around December 1937, Beckett had a brief affair with [Peggy Guggenheim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peggy_Guggenheim "Peggy Guggenheim"), who nicknamed him "Oblomov" (after the character in [Ivan Goncharov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivan_Goncharov "Ivan Goncharov")'s [novel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oblomov "Oblomov")).

In January 1938 in Paris, Beckett was stabbed in the chest and nearly killed when he refused the solicitations of a notorious [pimp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pimp "Pimp") (who went by the name of Prudent). Joyce arranged a private room for Beckett at the hospital. The publicity surrounding the stabbing attracted the attention of [Suzanne Dechevaux-Dumesnil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suzanne_Dechevaux-Dumesnil "Suzanne Dechevaux-Dumesnil"), who knew Beckett slightly from his first stay in Paris. This time, however, the two would begin a lifelong companionship. At a preliminary hearing, Beckett asked his attacker for the motive behind the stabbing. Prudent replied: "Je ne sais pas, Monsieur. Je m'excuse" ["I do not know, sir. I apologise"]. Beckett eventually dropped the charges against his attacker – partially to avoid further formalities, partly because he found Prudent likeable and well-mannered. After [his own near-fatal stabbing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stabbing_of_Salman_Rushdie "Stabbing of Salman Rushdie") in 2022, author [Salman Rushdie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salman_Rushdie "Salman Rushdie") referenced Beckett's example when talking about his reasons for not interviewing his attacker.

For Beckett, the 1930s was a decade of artistic exploration. He started to take a serious interest in art history, frequenting Ireland's [National Gallery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Gallery_of_Ireland "National Gallery of Ireland"), studying a range of painters and movements (specifically the [Dutch Golden Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Golden_Age_painting "Dutch Golden Age painting")), and even visiting private collections. In 1933 Beckett applied for the position of assistant curator at [London's National Gallery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Gallery "National Gallery"). Later, in the winter of 1936–37, having sailed from Cobh in East Cork to Hamburg on 26 September 1936, he took a deep dive into Germany's galleries and underground collections. This lasting engagement with the visual arts seeped into his creative process, often shaping his literary output and incentivising him to collaborate with artists such as [Joan Mitchell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joan_Mitchell "Joan Mitchell") and [Geneviève Asse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geneviève_Asse "Geneviève Asse").

## World War II and French Resistance

After the German occupation of France in 1940, Beckett joined the [French Resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Resistance "French Resistance"), working as a courier for the *[Réseau Gloria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Réseau_Gloria "Réseau Gloria")* network. On several occasions over the next two years he was nearly caught by the [Gestapo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gestapo "Gestapo"). In August 1942, his network was betrayed and he and Suzanne fled south on foot to the safety of the small village of [Roussillon, Vaucluse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roussillon,_Vaucluse "Roussillon, Vaucluse"). During the two years that Beckett stayed in Roussillon he indirectly helped the [Maquis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maquis_(World_War_II) "Maquis (World War II)") engage in sabotage operations against German occupational forces in the Vaucluse mountains, though Beckett rarely spoke about his wartime work in later life. He was awarded the [Croix de Guerre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Croix_de_Guerre_1939–1945 "Croix de Guerre 1939–1945") on 30 March 1945, and later the [Médaille de la Reconnaissance Française](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Médaille_de_la_Reconnaissance_Française "Médaille de la Reconnaissance Française"), by the French government for his efforts in fighting the German occupation; to the end of his life, however, Beckett would refer to his work with the French Resistance as "boy scout stuff".

While in hiding in Roussillon, Beckett continued work on the novel [*Watt*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watt_(novel) "Watt (novel)"). He started the novel in 1941 and completed it in 1945, but it was not published until 1953; however, an extract had appeared in the Dublin literary periodical [*Envoy*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Envoy,_A_Review_of_Literature_and_Art "Envoy, A Review of Literature and Art"). After the war, he returned to France in 1946 where he worked as a stores manager at the Irish Red Cross Hospital based in [Saint-Lô](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint-Lô "Saint-Lô"). Beckett described his experiences in an untransmitted radio script, "[The Capital of the Ruins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Capital_of_the_Ruins "The Capital of the Ruins")".

## Fame: novels and the theatre

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/9b/Samuel_Beckett.jpg/250px-Samuel_Beckett.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Samuel_Beckett.jpg)

Portrait of Samuel Beckett by [Reginald Gray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reginald_Gray_(artist) "Reginald Gray (artist)"), painted in Paris, 1961 (from the collection of Ken White, Dublin)

In 1945, Beckett returned to Dublin for a brief visit. During his stay, he had a revelation in his mother's room: his entire future direction in literature appeared to him. Beckett had felt that he would remain forever in the shadow of Joyce, certain to never beat him at his own game. His revelation prompted him to change direction and acknowledge both his own stupidity and his interest in ignorance and impotence:

> "I realised that Joyce had gone as far as one could in the direction of knowing more, [being] in control of one's material. He was always adding to it; you only have to look at his proofs to see that. I realised that my own way was in impoverishment, in lack of knowledge and in taking away, in subtracting rather than in adding."

Knowlson argues that "Beckett was rejecting the Joycean principle that knowing more was a way of creatively understanding the world and controlling it ... In future, his work would focus on poverty, failure, exile and loss – as he put it, on man as a 'non-knower' and as a 'non-can-er.'" The revelation "has rightly been regarded as a pivotal moment in his entire career". Beckett fictionalised the experience in his play *[Krapp's Last Tape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krapp's_Last_Tape "Krapp's Last Tape")* (1958). While listening to a tape he made earlier in his life, Krapp hears his younger self say "clear to me at last that the dark I have always struggled to keep under is in reality my most...", at which point Krapp fast-forwards the tape (before the audience can hear the complete revelation). Beckett later explained to Knowlson that the missing words on the tape are "precious ally".

In 1946, [Jean-Paul Sartre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Paul_Sartre "Jean-Paul Sartre")'s magazine [Les Temps modernes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Les_Temps_modernes "Les Temps modernes") published the first part of Beckett's short story "*Suite*" (later to be called "*La Fin*", or "The End"), not realising that Beckett had only submitted the first half of the story; co-editor [Simone de Beauvoir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simone_de_Beauvoir "Simone de Beauvoir") refused to publish the second part. Beckett also began to write his fourth novel, [*Mercier et Camier*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercier_and_Camier "Mercier and Camier"), which was not published until 1970. The novel preceded his most famous work, the play *En attendant Godot* (*[Waiting for Godot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waiting_for_Godot "Waiting for Godot"))*, which was written not long afterwards. More importantly, *Mercier and Camier* was Beckett's first long work written in French, the language of most of his subsequent works which were strongly supported by [Jérôme Lindon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jérôme_Lindon "Jérôme Lindon"), director of his Parisian publishing house [Les Éditions de Minuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Les_Éditions_de_Minuit "Les Éditions de Minuit"), including the [poioumenon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poioumenon "Poioumenon") "trilogy" of novels: [*Molloy*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molloy_(novel) "Molloy (novel)") (1951); *Malone meurt* (1951), *[Malone Dies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malone_Dies "Malone Dies")* (1958); *L'innommable* (1953), *[The Unnamable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Unnamable_(novel) "The Unnamable (novel)")* (1960). Despite being a native English speaker, Beckett wrote in French because, as he himself claimed, it was easier for him thus to write "without style".

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f9/Samuel_Beckett_01-2.jpg/250px-Samuel_Beckett_01-2.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Samuel_Beckett_01-2.jpg)

Portrait, circa 1970

*Waiting for Godot*, like most of his works after 1947, was first written in French. Beckett worked on the play between October 1948 and January 1949. His partner, [Suzanne Dechevaux-Dumesnil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suzanne_Dechevaux-Dumesnil "Suzanne Dechevaux-Dumesnil"), was integral to its success. Dechevaux-Dumesnil became his agent and sent the manuscript to multiple producers until they met [Roger Blin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roger_Blin "Roger Blin"), the soon-to-be director of the play.

Blin's knowledge of French theatre and vision, alongside Beckett's knowing what he wanted the play to represent, contributed greatly to its success. In a much-quoted article, the critic [Vivian Mercier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vivian_Mercier "Vivian Mercier") wrote that Beckett "has achieved a theoretical impossibility—a play in which nothing happens, that yet keeps audiences glued to their seats. What's more, since the second act is a subtly different reprise of the first, he has written a play in which nothing happens, twice." The play was published in 1952 and premièred in 1953 in Paris; an English translation was performed two years later. The play was a critical, popular, and controversial success in Paris. It opened in London in 1955 to mainly negative reviews, but the tide turned with positive reactions from Harold Hobson in *[The Sunday Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Sunday_Times_(UK) "The Sunday Times (UK)")* and, later, [Kenneth Tynan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenneth_Tynan "Kenneth Tynan"). After the showing in Miami, the play became extremely popular, with highly successful performances in the US and Germany. The play is a favourite: it is not only performed frequently but has globally inspired playwrights to emulate it. This is the sole play the manuscript of which Beckett never sold, donated or gave away. He refused to allow the play to be translated into film but did allow it to be played on television.

During this time in the 1950s, Beckett became one of several adults who sometimes drove local children to school; one such child was André Roussimoff, who later became a famous professional wrestler under the name [André the Giant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/André_the_Giant "André the Giant"). They had a surprising amount of common ground and bonded over their love of cricket, with Roussimoff later recalling that the two rarely talked about anything else. Beckett translated all of his works into English himself, with the exception of *Molloy*, for which he collaborated with Patrick Bowles. The success of *Waiting for Godot* opened up a career in theatre for its author. Beckett went on to write successful full-length plays, including *Fin de partie* (*[Endgame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endgame_(play) "Endgame (play)")*) (1957), *Krapp's Last Tape* (1958, written in English), *[Happy Days](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Happy_Days_(play) "Happy Days (play)")* (1961, also written in English), and *[Play](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Play_(play) "Play (play)")* (1963). In 1961, Beckett received the International Publishers' Formentor Prize in recognition of his work, which he shared that year with [Jorge Luis Borges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jorge_Luis_Borges "Jorge Luis Borges").

## Later life and death

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/40/Beckett-grave-paris.jpg/250px-Beckett-grave-paris.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Beckett-grave-paris.jpg)

Tomb of Samuel Beckett at the [cimetière du Montparnasse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montparnasse_Cemetery "Montparnasse Cemetery")

The 1960s were a time of change for Beckett, both on a personal level and as a writer. In 1961, he married Suzanne in a secret civil ceremony in England (its secrecy due to reasons relating to French inheritance law). The success of his plays led to invitations to attend rehearsals and productions around the world, leading eventually to a new career as a theatre director. In 1957, he had his first commission from the [BBC Third Programme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Third_Programme "BBC Third Programme") for a radio play, *[All That Fall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All_That_Fall "All That Fall").* He continued writing sporadically for radio and extended his scope to include cinema and television. He began to write in English again, although he also wrote in French until the end of his life. He bought some land in 1953 near a hamlet about 60 kilometres (40 mi) northeast of Paris and built a cottage for himself with the help of some locals.

From the late 1950s until his death, Beckett had a relationship with [Barbara Bray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbara_Bray "Barbara Bray"), a widow who worked as a script editor for the [BBC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC "BBC"). Knowlson wrote of them: "She was small and attractive, but, above all, keenly intelligent and well-read. Beckett seems to have been immediately attracted by her and she to him. Their encounter was highly significant for them both, for it represented the beginning of a relationship that was to last, in parallel with that with Suzanne, for the rest of his life." Bray died in [Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edinburgh "Edinburgh") on 25 February 2010.

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f4/%22Samuel_Beckett%22_by_Javad_Alizadeh.jpg/250px-%22Samuel_Beckett%22_by_Javad_Alizadeh.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:"Samuel_Beckett"_by_Javad_Alizadeh.jpg)

Caricature of Samuel Beckett by [Javad Alizadeh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Javad_Alizadeh "Javad Alizadeh")

In 1969 the [avant-garde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avant-garde "Avant-garde") filmmaker [Rosa von Praunheim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosa_von_Praunheim "Rosa von Praunheim") shot an experimental short film portrait about Beckett, which he named after the writer.

In October 1969 while on holiday in [Tunis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tunis "Tunis") with Suzanne, Beckett heard that he had won the [1969 Nobel Prize in Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1969_Nobel_Prize_in_Literature "1969 Nobel Prize in Literature"). Anticipating that her intensely private husband would be saddled with fame from that moment on, Suzanne called the award a "catastrophe". While Beckett did not devote much time to interviews, he sometimes met the artists, scholars, and admirers who sought him out in the anonymous lobby of the Hotel PLM Saint-Jacques in Paris – where he arranged his appointments and often had lunch – near his [Montparnasse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montparnasse "Montparnasse") home. Although Beckett was an intensely private man, a review of the second volume of his letters by Roy Foster on 15 December 2011 issue of *The New Republic* reveals Beckett to be not only unexpectedly amiable but frequently prepared to talk about his work and the process behind it.

Suzanne died on 17 July 1989. Confined to a nursing home and suffering from [emphysema](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emphysema "Emphysema") and possibly [Parkinson's disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parkinson's_disease "Parkinson's disease"), Beckett died on 22 December 1989. The two were interred together in the [cimetière du Montparnasse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montparnasse_Cemetery "Montparnasse Cemetery") in Paris and share a simple granite gravestone that follows Beckett's directive that it should be "any colour, so long as it's grey".

## Works

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/87/Samuel_Beckett_by_Edmund_S._Valtman_ppmsc.07951.jpg/250px-Samuel_Beckett_by_Edmund_S._Valtman_ppmsc.07951.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Samuel_Beckett_by_Edmund_S._Valtman_ppmsc.07951.jpg)

Caricature of Beckett by [Edmund S. Valtman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmund_S._Valtman "Edmund S. Valtman")

Beckett's career as a writer can be roughly divided into three periods: his early works, up until the end of World War II in 1945; his middle period, stretching from 1945 until the early 1960s, during which he wrote what are probably his best-known works; and his late period, from the early 1960s until Beckett's death in 1989, during which his works tended to become shorter and his style more [minimalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimalism "Minimalism").

### Early works

Beckett's earliest works are generally considered to have been strongly influenced by the work of his friend [James Joyce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Joyce "James Joyce"). They are erudite and seem to display the author's learning merely for its own sake, resulting in several obscure passages. The opening phrases of the short-story collection *[More Pricks than Kicks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/More_Pricks_than_Kicks "More Pricks than Kicks")* (1934) afford a representative sample of this style:

> It was morning and Belacqua was stuck in the first of the canti in the moon. He was so bogged that he could move neither backward nor forward. Blissful Beatrice was there, Dante also, and she explained the spots on the moon to him. She shewed him in the first place where he was at fault, then she put up her own explanation. She had it from God, therefore he could rely on its being accurate in every particular.

The passage makes reference to [Dante](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dante_Alighieri "Dante Alighieri")'s *[Commedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Divine_Comedy "The Divine Comedy")*, which can serve to confuse readers not familiar with that work. It also anticipates aspects of Beckett's later work: the physical inactivity of the character Belacqua; the character's immersion in his own head and thoughts; the somewhat irreverent comedy of the final sentence.

Similar elements are present in Beckett's first published novel, *Murphy* (1938), which also explores the themes of insanity and chess (both of which would be recurrent elements in Beckett's later works). The novel's opening sentence hints at the somewhat pessimistic undertones and [black](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_comedy "Black comedy") [humour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gallows_humor "Gallows humor") that animate many of Beckett's works: "The sun shone, having no alternative, on the nothing new". *[Watt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watt_(novel) "Watt (novel)")*, written while Beckett was in hiding in Roussillon during World War II, is similar in terms of themes but less exuberant in its style. It explores human movement as if it were a [mathematical permutation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permutation "Permutation"), presaging Beckett's later preoccupation—in both his novels and dramatic works—with precise movement.

Beckett's 1930 essay *[Proust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proust_(Beckett_essay) "Proust (Beckett essay)")* was strongly influenced by [Schopenhauer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Schopenhauer "Arthur Schopenhauer")'s [pessimism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophical_pessimism "Philosophical pessimism") and laudatory descriptions of saintly asceticism. At this time Beckett began to write creatively in the French language. In the late 1930s, he wrote a number of short poems in that language and their sparseness—in contrast to the density of his English poems of roughly the same period, collected in *Echo's Bones and Other Precipitates* (1935)—seems to show that Beckett, albeit through the medium of another language, was in process of simplifying his style, a change also evidenced in *[Watt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watt_(novel) "Watt (novel)")*.

### Middle period

> who may tell the tale
> of the old man?
> weigh absence in a scale?
> mete want with a span?
> the sum assess
> of the world's woes?
> nothingness
> in words enclose?

— From *[Watt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watt_(novel) "Watt (novel)")* (1953)

After World War II, Beckett turned definitively to the French language as a vehicle. It was this, together with the "revelation" experienced in his mother's room in Dublin—in which he realised that his art must be subjective and drawn wholly from his own inner world—that would result in the works for which Beckett is best remembered today.

During the 15 years following the war, Beckett produced four major full-length stage plays: *En attendant Godot* (written 1948–1949; *[Waiting for Godot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waiting_for_Godot "Waiting for Godot")*), *Fin de partie* (1955–1957; *[Endgame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endgame_(play) "Endgame (play)")*), *[Krapp's Last Tape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krapp's_Last_Tape "Krapp's Last Tape")* (1958), and *[Happy Days](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Happy_Days_(play) "Happy Days (play)")* (1961). These plays—which are often considered, rightly or wrongly, to have been instrumental in the so-called "[Theatre of the Absurd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatre_of_the_Absurd "Theatre of the Absurd")"—deal in a [darkly humorous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_comedy "Black comedy") way with themes similar to those of the roughly contemporary [existentialist thinkers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Existentialism "Existentialism"). The term "Theatre of the Absurd" was coined by Martin Esslin in a book of the same name; Beckett and *Godot* were centrepieces of the book. Esslin argued these plays were the fulfilment of [Albert Camus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Camus "Albert Camus")'s concept of "the absurd"; this is one reason Beckett is often falsely labelled as an existentialist (this is based on the assumption that Camus was an existentialist, though he in fact broke off from the existentialist movement and founded [his own philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absurdism "Absurdism")). Though many of the themes are similar, Beckett had little affinity for existentialism as a whole.

Broadly speaking, the plays deal with the subject of despair and the will to survive in spite of that despair, in the face of an uncomprehending and incomprehensible world. The words of Nell—one of the two characters in *Endgame* who are trapped in ashbins, from which they occasionally peek their heads to speak—can best summarise the themes of the plays of Beckett's middle period: "Nothing is funnier than unhappiness, I grant you that. ... Yes, yes, it's the most comical thing in the world. And we laugh, we laugh, with a will, in the beginning. But it's always the same thing. Yes, it's like the funny story we have heard too often, we still find it funny, but we don't laugh any more."

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f1/Waiting_for_Godot_in_Doon_School.jpg/250px-Waiting_for_Godot_in_Doon_School.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Waiting_for_Godot_in_Doon_School.jpg)

Beckett's *[Waiting for Godot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waiting_for_Godot "Waiting for Godot")* is considered a hallmark of the Theatre of the Absurd. The play's two protagonists, Vladimir and Estragon (pictured, in a 2010 production at [The Doon School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Doon_School "The Doon School"), India), give voice to Beckett's existentialism.

Beckett's outstanding achievements in prose during the period were the three novels [*Molloy*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molloy_(novel) "Molloy (novel)") (1951), *Malone meurt* (1951; *[Malone Dies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malone_Dies "Malone Dies")*) and *L'innommable* (1953: *[The Unnamable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Unnamable_(novel) "The Unnamable (novel)")*). In these novels—sometimes referred to as a "trilogy", though this is against the author's own explicit wishes—the prose becomes increasingly bare and stripped down. *Molloy*, for instance, still retains many of the characteristics of a conventional novel (time, place, movement, and plot) and it makes use of the structure of a [detective novel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Detective_novel "Detective novel"). In *Malone Dies*, movement and plot are largely dispensed with, though there is still some indication of place and the passage of time; the "action" of the book takes the form of an [interior monologue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_monologue "Inner monologue"). Finally, in *The Unnamable*, almost all sense of place and time are abolished, and the essential theme seems to be the conflict between the voice's drive to continue speaking so as to continue existing, and its almost equally strong urge towards silence and oblivion. Despite the widely held view that Beckett's work, as exemplified by the novels of this period, is essentially pessimistic, the will to live seems to win out in the end; witness, for instance, the famous final phrase of *The Unnamable*: "you must go on, I can't go on, I'll go on".

After these three novels, Beckett struggled for many years to produce a sustained work of prose, a struggle evidenced by the brief "stories" later collected as *Texts for Nothing*. In the late 1950s, however, he created one of his most radical prose works, *Comment c'est* (1961; *[How It Is](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/How_It_Is "How It Is")*). An early variant version of *Comment c'est*, *L'Image*, was published in the British arts review, [*X: A Quarterly Review*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X_(magazine) "X (magazine)") (1959), and is the first appearance of the novel in any form. This work relates the adventures of an unnamed narrator crawling through the mud while dragging a sack of canned food. It was written as a sequence of unpunctuated paragraphs in a style approaching [telegraphese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telegraphese "Telegraphese"): "You are there somewhere alive somewhere vast stretch of time then it's over you are there no more alive no more than again you are there again alive again it wasn't over an error you begin again all over more or less in the same place or in another as when another image above in the light you come to in hospital in the dark". Following this work, it was almost another decade before Beckett produced a work of non-dramatic prose. *How It Is* is generally considered to mark the end of his middle period as a writer.

### Late works

> time she stopped
> sitting at her window
> quiet at her window
> only window
> facing other windows
> other only windows
> all eyes
> all sides
> high and low
> time she stopped

— From *Rockaby* (1980)

Throughout the 1960s and into the 1970s, Beckett's works exhibited an increasing tendency—already evident in much of his work of the 1950s—towards compactness. This has led to his work sometimes being described as [minimalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimalism "Minimalism"). The extreme example of this, among his dramatic works, is the 1969 piece *[Breath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breath_(play) "Breath (play)")*, which lasts for only 35 seconds and has no characters (though it was likely intended to offer ironic comment on *[Oh! Calcutta!](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oh!_Calcutta! "Oh! Calcutta!")*, the theatrical [revue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revue "Revue") for which it served as an introductory piece).

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d5/Samuel_Beckett_DCP_1341.JPG/250px-Samuel_Beckett_DCP_1341.JPG?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Samuel_Beckett_DCP_1341.JPG)

Portrait by [Reginald Gray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reginald_Gray_(artist) "Reginald Gray (artist)")

In his theatre of the late period, Beckett's characters—already few in number in the earlier plays—are whittled down to essential elements. The ironically titled *[Play](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Play_(play) "Play (play)")* (1962), for instance, consists of three characters immersed up to their necks in large funeral urns. The television drama *[Eh Joe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eh_Joe "Eh Joe")* (1963), which was written for the actor [Jack MacGowran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jack_MacGowran "Jack MacGowran"), is animated by a camera that steadily closes into a tight focus upon the face of the title character. The play *[Not I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Not_I "Not I")* (1972) consists almost solely of, in Beckett's words, "a moving mouth with the rest of the stage in darkness". Following from *Krapp's Last Tape*, many of these later plays explore memory, often in the form of a forced recollection of haunting past events in a moment of stillness in the present. They also deal with the theme of the self-confined and observed, with a voice that either comes from outside into the protagonist's head (as in *Eh Joe*) or else another character comments on the protagonist silently, by means of gesture (as in *Not I*). Beckett's most politically charged play, *[Catastrophe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catastrophe_(play) "Catastrophe (play)")* (1982), which was dedicated to [Václav Havel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Václav_Havel "Václav Havel"), deals relatively explicitly with the idea of dictatorship. After a long period of inactivity, Beckett's poetry experienced a revival during this period in the ultra-terse French poems of *mirlitonnades*, with some as short as six words. These defied Beckett's usual scrupulous concern to translate his work from its original into the other of his two languages; several writers, including [Derek Mahon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derek_Mahon "Derek Mahon"), have attempted translations, but no complete version of the sequence has been published in English.

Beckett's late style saw him experiment with technology to create increasingly transdisciplinary works. This sampling of a range of artistic mediums and styles – classical music, painting, sculpture, television, and literature – to create a new and original form, or genre, is evident in his television plays. In works like *[Ghost Trio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghost_Trio_(play) "Ghost Trio (play)")* (broadcast in 1977) and *[Nacht und Träume](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nacht_und_Träume_(play) "Nacht und Träume (play)")* (broadcast in 1983) Beckett uses a musical frame (taking excerpts from [Beethoven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludwig_van_Beethoven "Ludwig van Beethoven") and [Schubert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_Schubert "Franz Schubert"), respectively) to structure his text and borrows well-known images from art history to create evocative stills that suggest themes of longing, ambiguity, hope, and suffering. Such experimentation with genre, music, and the visual arts, characterises Beckett's work during the 1970s and '80s.

Beckett's prose pieces during the late period were not as prolific as his theatre, as suggested by the title of the 1976 collection of short prose texts *Fizzles* (which the American artist [Jasper Johns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jasper_Johns "Jasper Johns") illustrated). Beckett experienced something of a renaissance with the novella *[Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Company_(short_story) "Company (short story)")* (1980), which continued with *[Ill Seen Ill Said](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ill_Seen_Ill_Said "Ill Seen Ill Said")* (1982) and *[Worstward Ho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worstward_Ho "Worstward Ho")* (1983), later collected in *[Nohow On](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nohow_On "Nohow On")*. In these three "'closed space' stories," Beckett continued his pre-occupation with memory and its effect on the confined and observed self, as well as with the positioning of bodies in space, as the opening phrases of *Company* make clear: "A voice comes to one in the dark. Imagine." "To one on his back in the dark. This he can tell by the pressure on his hind parts and by how the dark changes when he shuts his eyes and again when he opens them again. Only a small part of what is said can be verified. As for example when he hears, You are on your back in the dark. Then he must acknowledge the truth of what is said." Themes of aloneness and the doomed desire to successfully connect with other human beings are expressed in several late pieces, including *[Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Company_(short_story) "Company (short story)")* and *[Rockaby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rockaby "Rockaby")*.

In the hospital and nursing home where he spent his final days, Beckett wrote his last work, the 1988 poem "What is the Word" ("Comment dire"). The poem grapples with an inability to find words to express oneself, a theme echoing Beckett's earlier work, though possibly amplified by the sickness he experienced late in life.

## Collaborators

### Jack MacGowran

[Jack MacGowran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jack_MacGowran "Jack MacGowran") was the first actor to perform a one-man show based on the works of Beckett. He debuted *End of Day* in Dublin in 1962, revising it as *Beginning To End* (1965). The show went through further revisions before Beckett directed it in Paris in 1970; MacGowran won the 1970–1971 [Obie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obie_Award "Obie Award") for Best Performance By an Actor when he performed the show off-Broadway as *Jack MacGowran in the Works of Samuel Beckett.* Beckett wrote the radio play *[Embers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embers "Embers")* and the teleplay *[Eh Joe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eh_Joe "Eh Joe")* specifically for MacGowran. The actor also appeared in various productions of *[Waiting for Godot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waiting_for_Godot "Waiting for Godot")* and *[Endgame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endgame_(play) "Endgame (play)"),* and did several readings of Beckett's plays and poems on BBC Radio; he also recorded the LP *MacGowran Speaking Beckett* for [Claddagh Records](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claddagh_Records "Claddagh Records") in 1966.

### Billie Whitelaw

[Billie Whitelaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Billie_Whitelaw "Billie Whitelaw") worked with Beckett for 25 years on such plays as *[Not I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Not_I "Not I")*, *[Eh Joe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eh_Joe "Eh Joe")*, *[Footfalls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Footfalls "Footfalls")* and *[Rockaby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rockaby "Rockaby").* She first met Beckett in 1963. In her autobiography *Billie Whitelaw... Who He?,* she describes their first meeting in 1963 as "trust at first sight". Beckett went on to write many of his experimental theatre works for her. She came to be regarded as his muse, the "supreme interpreter of his work", perhaps most famous for her role as the mouth in *[Not I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Not_I "Not I")*. She said of the play *Rockaby*: "I put the tape in my head. And I sort of look in a particular way, but not at the audience. Sometimes as a director, Beckett comes out with absolute gems and I use them a lot in other areas. We were doing *Happy Days* and I just did not know where in the theatre to look during this particular section. And I asked, and he thought for a bit and then said, 'Inward' ". She said of her role in *Footfalls*: "I felt like a moving, musical [Edvard Munch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edvard_Munch "Edvard Munch") painting and, in fact, when Beckett was directing *Footfalls* he was not only using me to play the notes but I almost felt that he did have the paintbrush out and was painting." "Sam knew that I would turn myself inside out to give him what he wanted", she explained. "With all of Sam's work, the scream was there, my task was to try to get it out." She stopped performing his plays in 1989 when he died.

### Jocelyn Herbert

The English stage designer [Jocelyn Herbert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jocelyn_Herbert "Jocelyn Herbert") was a close friend and influence on Beckett until his death. She worked with him on such plays as *[Happy Days](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Happy_Days_(play) "Happy Days (play)")* (their third project) and *[Krapp's Last Tape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krapp's_Last_Tape "Krapp's Last Tape")* at the [Royal Court Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Court_Theatre "Royal Court Theatre"). Beckett said that Herbert became his closest friend in England: "She has a great feeling for the work and is very sensitive and doesn't want to bang the nail on the head. Generally speaking, there is a tendency on the part of designers to overstate, and this has never been the case with Jocelyn."

### Walter Asmus

The German director [Walter D. Asmus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_D._Asmus "Walter D. Asmus") began his working relationship with Beckett in the Schiller Theatre in Berlin in 1974 and continued until 1989, the year of the playwright's death. Asmus has directed many of Beckett's plays internationally.

## Legacy

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/thumb/c/c8/Sam_beck_20euro_gold_Reverse.JPG/250px-Sam_beck_20euro_gold_Reverse.JPG?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Sam_beck_20euro_gold_Reverse.JPG)

Samuel Beckett depicted on an [Irish commemorative coin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euro_gold_and_silver_commemorative_coins_(Ireland) "Euro gold and silver commemorative coins (Ireland)") celebrating the 100th anniversary of his birth

Of all the English-language [modernists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modernism "Modernism"), Beckett's work represents the most sustained attack on the [realist tradition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Realism_(literature) "Realism (literature)"). He opened up the possibility of theatre and fiction that dispense with conventional plot and the unities of time and place to focus on essential components of the [human condition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_condition "Human condition"). [Václav Havel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Václav_Havel "Václav Havel"), [John Banville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Banville "John Banville"), [Aidan Higgins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aidan_Higgins "Aidan Higgins"), [Tom Stoppard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tom_Stoppard "Tom Stoppard"), [Harold Pinter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Pinter "Harold Pinter") and [Jon Fosse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jon_Fosse "Jon Fosse") have publicly stated their indebtedness to Beckett's example. He has had a wider influence on [experimental writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Experimental_literature "Experimental literature") since the 1950s, from the [Beat Generation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beat_Generation "Beat Generation") to the happenings of the 1960s and after. In an Irish context, he has exerted great influence on poets such as [Derek Mahon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derek_Mahon "Derek Mahon") and [Thomas Kinsella](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Kinsella "Thomas Kinsella"), as well as writers like [Trevor Joyce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trevor_Joyce "Trevor Joyce") and [Catherine Walsh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catherine_Walsh_(poet) "Catherine Walsh (poet)") who proclaim their adherence to the modernist tradition as an alternative to the dominant realist mainstream.

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/23/SamuelBecketBridge.jpg/250px-SamuelBecketBridge.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:SamuelBecketBridge.jpg)

The Samuel Beckett Bridge, Dublin

Many major 20th-century composers including [Luciano Berio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luciano_Berio "Luciano Berio"), [György Kurtág](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/György_Kurtág "György Kurtág"), [Morton Feldman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morton_Feldman "Morton Feldman"), [Pascal Dusapin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pascal_Dusapin "Pascal Dusapin"), [Philip Glass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_Glass "Philip Glass"), [Roman Haubenstock-Ramati](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Haubenstock-Ramati "Roman Haubenstock-Ramati") and [Heinz Holliger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heinz_Holliger "Heinz Holliger") have created musical works based on Beckett's texts. His work has also influenced numerous international writers, artists and filmmakers including [Edward Albee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Albee "Edward Albee"), [Sam Shepard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sam_Shepard "Sam Shepard"), [Avigdor Arikha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avigdor_Arikha "Avigdor Arikha"), [Paul Auster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Auster "Paul Auster"), [J. M. Coetzee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._M._Coetzee "J. M. Coetzee"), [Richard Kalich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Kalich "Richard Kalich"), [Douglas Gordon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Douglas_Gordon "Douglas Gordon"), [Bruce Nauman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bruce_Nauman "Bruce Nauman"), [Anthony Minghella](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthony_Minghella "Anthony Minghella"), [Damian Pettigrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Damian_Pettigrew "Damian Pettigrew"), [Charlie Kaufman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlie_Kaufman "Charlie Kaufman") and [Brian Patrick Butler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brian_Patrick_Butler "Brian Patrick Butler").

Beckett is one of the most influential, widely discussed, and highly prized of 20th-century authors, inspiring a critical industry to rival that which has sprung up around James Joyce. He has divided critical opinion. Some early philosophical critics, such as [Sartre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Paul_Sartre "Jean-Paul Sartre") and [Theodor Adorno](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodor_Adorno "Theodor Adorno"), praised him, one for his revelation of absurdity, the other for his works' critical refusal of simplicities; others such as [Georg Lukács](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georg_Lukács "Georg Lukács") condemned him for 'decadent' lack of [realism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophical_realism "Philosophical realism").

Since Beckett's death, all rights for the performance of his plays are handled by the Beckett estate, currently managed by Edward Beckett (the author's nephew). The estate has a controversial reputation for maintaining firm control over how Beckett's plays are performed and does not grant licences to productions that do not adhere to the writer's stage directions.

Historians interested in tracing Beckett's bloodline were, in 2004, granted access to confirmed trace samples of his [DNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA "DNA") to conduct molecular genealogical studies to facilitate precise lineage determination.

Some of the best-known pictures of Beckett were taken by photographer [John Minihan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Minihan_(photographer) "John Minihan (photographer)"), who photographed him between 1980 and 1985 and developed such a good relationship with the writer that he became, in effect, his official photographer. Some consider one of these to be among the top three photographs of the 20th century. It was the theatre photographer, John Haynes, however, who took possibly the most widely reproduced image of Beckett: it is used on the cover of the Knowlson biography, for instance. This portrait was taken during rehearsals of the San Quentin Drama Workshop at the Royal Court Theatre in London, where Haynes photographed many productions of Beckett's work. [An Post](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/An_Post "An Post"), the Irish postal service, issued a [commemorative stamp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_people_on_stamps_of_Ireland "List of people on stamps of Ireland") of Beckett in 1994. The [Central Bank of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Bank_of_Ireland "Central Bank of Ireland") launched two Samuel Beckett Centenary [commemorative coins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euro_gold_and_silver_commemorative_coins_(Ireland) "Euro gold and silver commemorative coins (Ireland)") on 26 April 2006: €10 Silver Coin and €20 Gold Coin.

On 10 December 2009, the new bridge across the [River Liffey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/River_Liffey "River Liffey") in Dublin was opened and named the [Samuel Beckett Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Beckett_Bridge "Samuel Beckett Bridge") in his honour. Reminiscent of a harp on its side, it was designed by the celebrated Spanish architect [Santiago Calatrava](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santiago_Calatrava "Santiago Calatrava"), who had also designed the [James Joyce Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Joyce_Bridge "James Joyce Bridge") situated further upstream and opened on [Bloomsday](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bloomsday "Bloomsday") (16 June) 2003. Attendees at the official opening ceremony included Beckett's niece Caroline Murphy, his nephew Edward Beckett, poet [Seamus Heaney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seamus_Heaney "Seamus Heaney") and [Barry McGovern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barry_McGovern "Barry McGovern"). A ship of the [Irish Naval Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Naval_Service "Irish Naval Service"), the [LÉ *Samuel Beckett* (P61)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LÉ_Samuel_Beckett_(P61) "LÉ Samuel Beckett (P61)"), is named for Beckett. An Ulster History Circle blue plaque in his memory is located at Portora Royal School, Enniskillen, County Fermanagh.

In La Ferté-sous-Jouarre, the town where Beckett had a cottage, the public library and one of the local high schools bear his name.

Happy Days Enniskillen International Beckett Festival is an annual multi-arts festival celebrating the work and influence of Beckett. The festival, founded in 2011, is held at [Enniskillen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enniskillen "Enniskillen"), Northern Ireland where Beckett spent his formative years studying at [Portora Royal School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portora_Royal_School "Portora Royal School").

In 1983, the [Samuel Beckett Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Beckett_Award "Samuel Beckett Award") was established for writers who, in the opinion of a committee of critics, producers and publishers, showed innovation and excellence in writing for the performing arts. In 2003, [The Oxford Samuel Beckett Theatre Trust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Beckett_Award "Samuel Beckett Award") was formed to support the showcasing of new innovative theatre at the [Barbican Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbican_Centre "Barbican Centre") in the City of London.

Music for three Samuel Beckett plays (*Words and Music*, *Cascando*, and *...but the clouds...*), was composed by [Martin Pearlman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Pearlman "Martin Pearlman") which was commissioned by the 92nd Street Y in New York for the Beckett centennial and produced there and at [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University "Harvard University").

In 2022 [James Marsh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Marsh_(director) "James Marsh (director)") directed a [biopic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biopic "Biopic") of Beckett written by [Neil Forsyth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neil_Forsyth "Neil Forsyth") and entitled *[Dance First](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dance_First "Dance First")*, with [Gabriel Byrne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gabriel_Byrne "Gabriel Byrne") and [Fionn O'Shea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fionn_O'Shea "Fionn O'Shea") playing Beckett at different stages of his life. The film was made available through [Sky Cinema](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sky_Cinema "Sky Cinema") in 2023.

## Archives

Samuel Beckett's prolific career is spread across archives around the world. Significant collections include those at the [Harry Ransom Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harry_Ransom_Center "Harry Ransom Center"), [Washington University in St. Louis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington_University_in_St._Louis "Washington University in St. Louis"), the [University of Reading](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Reading "University of Reading"), [Trinity College Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College_Dublin "Trinity College Dublin"), and [Houghton Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Houghton_Library "Houghton Library"). Given the scattered nature of these collections, an effort has been made to create a digital repository through the [University of Antwerp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Antwerp "University of Antwerp").
