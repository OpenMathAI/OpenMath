**François Charles Mauriac** (French: [[fʁɑ̃swa ʃaʁl(ə) moʁjak]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/French "Help:IPA/French"); [Occitan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occitan_language "Occitan language"): *Francés Carles Mauriac*; 11 October 1885 – 1 September 1970) was a French novelist, dramatist, critic, poet, and journalist, a member of the [Académie française](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Académie_française "Académie française") (from 1933), and laureate of the [Nobel Prize in Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1952_Nobel_Prize_in_Literature "1952 Nobel Prize in Literature") (1952). He was awarded the Grand Cross of the [Légion d'honneur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Légion_d'honneur "Légion d'honneur") in 1958.

## Early life and education

François Charles Mauriac was born on 11 October 1885 in [Bordeaux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bordeaux "Bordeaux"), France.

He studied literature at the [University of Bordeaux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Bordeaux "University of Bordeaux"), graduating in 1905, after which he moved to [Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paris "Paris") and briefly studied at the [École des Chartes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/École_des_Chartes "École des Chartes") in 1908.

## Career

On 1 June 1933, he was elected a member of the *Académie française*, succeeding [Eugène Brieux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugène_Brieux "Eugène Brieux").

A former [Action française](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_française "Action française") supporter, he turned to the left during the [Spanish Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_Civil_War "Spanish Civil War"), criticising the Catholic Church for its support of Franco. After the fall of France to the [Axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axis_powers "Axis powers") during the [Second World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II"), he briefly supported the collaborationist régime of Marshal [Pétain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philippe_Pétain "Philippe Pétain"), but joined the [Resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Resistance "French Resistance") as early as December 1941. He was the only member of the [Académie française](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Académie_française "Académie française") to publish a Resistance text with the [Editions de Minuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Editions_de_Minuit "Editions de Minuit").

Mauriac had a bitter dispute with [Albert Camus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Camus "Albert Camus") immediately following the [Liberation of France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberation_of_France "Liberation of France"). At that time, Camus edited the Resistance paper *[Combat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combat_(newspaper) "Combat (newspaper)")* (thereafter an overt daily, until 1947), while Mauriac wrote a column for *[Le Figaro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Le_Figaro "Le Figaro")*. Camus said newly liberated France should [purge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Épuration_légale "Épuration légale") all [Nazi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazism "Nazism") collaborator elements, but Mauriac warned that such disputes should be set aside in the interests of national reconciliation. Mauriac also doubted that justice would be impartial or dispassionate, given the emotional turmoil of the Liberation. Despite having been viciously criticised by [Robert Brasillach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Brasillach "Robert Brasillach"), he campaigned against his execution.

He published a series of personal [memoirs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memoir "Memoir") and a [biography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biography "Biography") of [Charles de Gaulle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_de_Gaulle "Charles de Gaulle"). Mauriac's complete works were published in twelve volumes between 1950 and 1956. He encouraged [Elie Wiesel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elie_Wiesel "Elie Wiesel") to write about his experiences as a [Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jew "Jew") teenager during the [Holocaust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holocaust "Holocaust"), and wrote the foreword to Elie Wiesel's book *[Night](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Night_(memoir) "Night (memoir)")*.

Mauriac also had a bitter public dispute with [Roger Peyrefitte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roger_Peyrefitte "Roger Peyrefitte"), who criticised the [Vatican](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_See "Holy See") in books such as *Les Clés de saint Pierre* (1953). Mauriac threatened to resign from the paper he was working with at the time (*L'Express*) if they did not stop carrying advertisements for Peyrefitte's books. The quarrel was exacerbated by the release of the film adaptation of Peyrefitte's *Les Amitiés particulières*, and culminated in a virulent open letter by Peyrefitte in which he accused Mauriac of homosexual tendencies and called him a [Tartuffe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tartuffe "Tartuffe"), hypocrite.

Mauriac was opposed to [French rule in Vietnam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Indochina "French Indochina"), and strongly condemned the use of torture by the French army in [Algeria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torture_during_the_Algerian_War "Torture during the Algerian War").

## Awards and honours

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/51/Le_dipl%C3%B4me_de_Fran%C3%A7ois_Mauriac.JPG/250px-Le_dipl%C3%B4me_de_Fran%C3%A7ois_Mauriac.JPG?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Le_diplôme_de_François_Mauriac.JPG)

The [Nobel Prize in Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Literature "Nobel Prize in Literature") diploma awarded to François Mauriac

In 1952, he was awarded the [Nobel Prize in Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1952_Nobel_Prize_in_Literature "1952 Nobel Prize in Literature") "for the deep spiritual insight and the artistic intensity with which he has in his novels penetrated the drama of human life". He was awarded the Grand Cross of the *[Légion d'honneur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Légion_d'honneur "Légion d'honneur")* in 1958.

Other honours and awards include:

- 1926 — *Grand Prix du roman de l'Académie française*
- 1933 — Member of the *Académie française*
- 1958 — Grand Cross of the *Légion d'honneur*

## Personal life

He was the father of writer [Claude Mauriac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claude_Mauriac "Claude Mauriac") and grandfather of [Anne Wiazemsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anne_Wiazemsky "Anne Wiazemsky"), a French actress and author who worked with and married French director [Jean-Luc Godard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Luc_Godard "Jean-Luc Godard").

## Death and legacy

Mauriac died in Paris on 1 September 1970, and was interred in the Cimetière de Vemars, [Val d'Oise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Val_d'Oise "Val d'Oise"), France.

His former home, the [Domaine de Malagar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domaine_de_Malagar?action=edit&redlink=1 "Domaine de Malagar") (Malagar Estate), around 50 km (31 mi) south of [Bordeaux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bordeaux "Bordeaux"), France, is now an historic site, which houses the Centre François Mauriac.

There are two literary prizes awarded annually that honour Mauriac:

- [Prix François Mauriac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prix_François_Mauriac_(Académie_française) "Prix François Mauriac (Académie française)"), awarded by the [Académie Française](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Académie_Française "Académie Française") since 1994
- [Prix Francois-Mauriac de la région Aquitaine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prix_Francois-Mauriac_de_la_région_Aquitaine "Prix Francois-Mauriac de la région Aquitaine"), originally founded in 1985 and revived after an hiatus in 2002, awarded by the Aquitaine regional authority at the Centre François Mauriac at Malagar
