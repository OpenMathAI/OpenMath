**Octavio Paz Lozano** (March 31, 1914 – April 19, 1998) was a Mexican philosopher, poet, and diplomat. For his body of work, he was awarded the 1977 [Jerusalem Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerusalem_Prize "Jerusalem Prize"), the 1981 [Miguel de Cervantes Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miguel_de_Cervantes_Prize "Miguel de Cervantes Prize"), the 1982 [Neustadt International Prize for Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neustadt_International_Prize_for_Literature "Neustadt International Prize for Literature"), and the [1990 Nobel Prize in Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1990_Nobel_Prize_in_Literature "1990 Nobel Prize in Literature").

## Early life

Octavio Paz was born near [Mexico City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexico_City "Mexico City"). His family was a prominent [liberal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberalism_in_Mexico "Liberalism in Mexico") political family in Mexico, with [Spanish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spaniards "Spaniards") and [indigenous Mexican](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_peoples_of_Mexico "Indigenous peoples of Mexico") roots. His grandfather, [Ireneo Paz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ireneo_Paz "Ireneo Paz"), the family's patriarch, fought in the [War of the Reform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_of_the_Reform "War of the Reform") against conservatives, and then became a staunch supporter of liberal war hero [Porfirio Díaz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porfirio_Díaz "Porfirio Díaz") up until just before the 1910 outbreak of the [Mexican Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexican_Revolution "Mexican Revolution"). Ireneo Paz became an intellectual and journalist, starting several newspapers, where he was publisher and printer. Ireneo's son, [Octavio Paz Solórzano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octavio_Paz_Solórzano "Octavio Paz Solórzano"), supported [Emiliano Zapata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emiliano_Zapata "Emiliano Zapata") during the Revolution, and published an early biography of him and the Zapatista movement. Octavio was named after his father but spent much of his childhood with his grandfather Ireneo, as his namesake father was active in the Mexican Revolution. His father later died violently. The family experienced financial ruin after the Mexican Revolution; they briefly relocated to Los Angeles, before returning to Mexico. Paz had blue eyes and was often mistaken for a foreigner by other children—according to a biography written by his long-time associate, historian [Enrique Krauze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enrique_Krauze "Enrique Krauze"), when Zapatista revolutionary [Antonio Díaz Soto y Gama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonio_Díaz_Soto_y_Gama "Antonio Díaz Soto y Gama") met young Octavio, he said, "*Caramba*, you didn't tell me you had a [Visigoth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visigoths "Visigoths") for a son!" Krauze quotes Paz as saying, "I felt myself Mexican but they wouldn't let me be one."

Paz was introduced to literature early in his life through the influence of his grandfather Ireneo's library, filled with classic [Mexican](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexican_literature "Mexican literature") and [European literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_literature "Western literature"). During the 1920s, he discovered [Gerardo Diego](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerardo_Diego "Gerardo Diego"), [Juan Ramón Jiménez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juan_Ramón_Jiménez "Juan Ramón Jiménez"), and [Antonio Machado](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonio_Machado "Antonio Machado"); these Spanish writers had a great influence on his early writings.

As a teenager in 1931, Paz published his first poems, including "Cabellera". Two years later, at the age of nineteen, he published *Luna Silvestre* (*Wild Moon*), a collection of poems. Between those publications, in 1932 at the age of 18, he founded his first literary review, *[Barandal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barandal_(magazine) "Barandal (magazine)")* with some friends.

For a few years, Paz studied law and literature at [National University of Mexico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Autonomous_University_of_Mexico "National Autonomous University of Mexico"). During this time, he became familiar with [leftist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Left-wing_politics "Left-wing politics") poets, such as Chilean [Pablo Neruda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pablo_Neruda "Pablo Neruda"). In 1936, Paz abandoned his law studies, and left Mexico City for [Yucatán](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yucatán_(state) "Yucatán (state)") to work at a school in [Mérida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mérida,_Yucatán "Mérida, Yucatán"). The school was set up for the sons of [peasants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peasant "Peasant") and workers. There, he began working on the first of his long, ambitious poems, "Entre la piedra y la flor" ("Between the Stone and the Flower," 1941, revised 1976); influenced by the work of [T. S. Eliot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T._S._Eliot "T. S. Eliot"), it explores the situation of the Mexican peasant under the domineering landlords of the day.

In July 1937, he attended the Second International Writers' Congress—the purpose of which was to discuss the attitude of intellectuals to [the war in Spain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_Civil_War "Spanish Civil War")—held in [Valencia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valencia "Valencia"), [Barcelona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barcelona "Barcelona") and [Madrid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madrid "Madrid") and attended by many writers, including [André Malraux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/André_Malraux "André Malraux"), [Ernest Hemingway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_Hemingway "Ernest Hemingway"), [Stephen Spender](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Spender "Stephen Spender"), and [Pablo Neruda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pablo_Neruda "Pablo Neruda"). Paz showed his solidarity with the Republican side, and against the [fascists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fascism "Fascism") led by [Francisco Franco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francisco_Franco "Francisco Franco"). While in Europe he also visited Paris, where he encountered the [surrealist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surrealism "Surrealism") movement, which left a profound impact upon him. After his return to Mexico, in 1938 Paz co-funded a literary journal, *[Taller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taller_(magazine)?action=edit&redlink=1 "Taller (magazine)")* ("Workshop") and wrote for that magazine until 1941. In 1937 he married [Elena Garro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elena_Garro "Elena Garro"), considered to be one of Mexico's finest writers; they had met in 1935. They had one daughter, Helena, and were divorced in 1959.

In 1943, Paz received a [Guggenheim Fellowship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guggenheim_Fellowship "Guggenheim Fellowship") and used it to study at the [University of California at Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Berkeley "University of California, Berkeley") in the United States. Two years later, he entered the Mexican diplomatic service, and was assigned for a time to New York City. In 1945, he was sent to Paris, where he wrote *[El Laberinto de la Soledad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Labyrinth_of_Solitude "The Labyrinth of Solitude")* (*The Labyrinth of Solitude*); *The New York Times* later described it as "an analysis of modern Mexico and the Mexican personality in which he described his fellow countrymen as instinctive nihilists who hide behind masks of solitude and ceremoniousness." In 1952, he travelled to India for the first time, and that same year went to [Tōkyō](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo "Tokyo") as *[chargé d'affaires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chargé_d'affaires "Chargé d'affaires")*. He next was assigned to [Geneva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geneva "Geneva"), Switzerland. He returned to Mexico City in 1954, where he wrote his great poem "Piedra de sol" ("Sunstone") in 1957, and published *Libertad bajo palabra* (*Liberty under Oath*), a compilation of his poetry up to that time. He was again sent to Paris in 1959, and in 1962, he was named Mexico's ambassador to India.

## Later life

In [New Delhi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Delhi "New Delhi"), as Ambassador of Mexico to [India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/India "India"), Paz completed several works, including *El mono gramático* (*The Monkey Grammarian*) and *Ladera este* (*Eastern Slope*). While in India, he met numerous writers of a group known as the [Hungry Generation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungry_Generation "Hungry Generation") and had a profound influence on them.

In 1965, he married [Marie-José Tramini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marie-José_Tramini?action=edit&redlink=1 "Marie-José Tramini"), a French woman who would be his wife for the rest of his life. That fall, he went to [Cornell University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornell_University "Cornell University") and taught two courses, one in Spanish and the other in English—the magazine *LIFE en Español* published a piece, illustrated with several pictures, about his tenure there in their July 4, 1966 issue. He subsequently returned to Mexico.

In 1968, Paz resigned from the diplomatic service in protest against the Mexican government's [massacre of student demonstrators in Tlatelolco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tlatelolco_massacre "Tlatelolco massacre"); after seeking refuge in Paris, he again returned to Mexico in 1969, where he founded his magazine *Plural* (1970–1976) with a group of liberal Mexican and Latin American writers.
From 1969 to 1970, Paz was [Simón Bolívar Professor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simón_Bolívar_Professor_of_Latin-American_Studies "Simón Bolívar Professor of Latin-American Studies") at the [University of Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Cambridge "University of Cambridge"). He was also a visiting lecturer during the late 1960s, and the [A. D. White](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A._D._White "A. D. White") Professor-at-Large from 1972 to 1974 at Cornell. In 1974, he was the [Charles Eliot Norton Professor of Poetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Eliot_Norton_Lectures "Charles Eliot Norton Lectures") at [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University "Harvard University"); his book *Los hijos del limo* (*Children of the Mire*) was the result of his lectures. After the Mexican government closed *Plural* in 1975, Paz founded *[Vuelta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vuelta_(magazine) "Vuelta (magazine)")*, another cultural magazine. He was editor of that until his death in 1998, when the magazine closed.

Paz won the 1977 [Jerusalem Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerusalem_Prize "Jerusalem Prize") for literature on the theme of individual freedom. In 1980, he was awarded an honorary doctorate from Harvard, and in 1982, he won the [Neustadt Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neustadt_Prize "Neustadt Prize"). Once good friends with novelist [Carlos Fuentes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carlos_Fuentes "Carlos Fuentes"), Paz became estranged from him in the 1980s in a disagreement over the [Sandinistas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sandinistas "Sandinistas"), whom Paz opposed and Fuentes supported.; in 1988, Paz's magazine *[Vuelta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vuelta_(magazine) "Vuelta (magazine)")* published criticism of Fuentes by [Enrique Krauze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enrique_Krauze "Enrique Krauze"), resulting in the estrangement.

A collection of Paz's poems (written between 1957 and 1987) was published in 1990, and in that year, he was awarded the [Nobel Prize in Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Literature "Nobel Prize in Literature").

Paz died of cancer on April 19, 1998, in Mexico City. His ashes, along with those of his spouse, Marie-José Tramini, are kept at a memorial in the [Colegio de San Ildefonso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colegio_de_San_Ildefonso "Colegio de San Ildefonso") in Mexico City.

[Guillermo Sheridan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guillermo_Sheridan "Guillermo Sheridan"), who in 1998 was named by Paz as director of the Octavio Paz Foundation, published a book, *Poeta con paisaje* (2004), with several biographical essays about the poet.

## Aesthetics

"The poetry of Octavio Paz," wrote the critic [Ramón Xirau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramón_Xirau "Ramón Xirau"), "does not hesitate between language and silence; it leads into the realm of silence where true language lives."

## Writings

A prolific author and poet, Paz published scores of works during his lifetime, many of which have been translated into other languages. His poetry has been translated into English by [Samuel Beckett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Beckett "Samuel Beckett"), [Charles Tomlinson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Tomlinson "Charles Tomlinson"), [Elizabeth Bishop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_Bishop "Elizabeth Bishop"), [Muriel Rukeyser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muriel_Rukeyser "Muriel Rukeyser") and [Mark Strand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Strand "Mark Strand"). His early poetry was influenced by [Marxism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marxism "Marxism"), [surrealism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surrealism "Surrealism"), and [existentialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Existentialism "Existentialism"), as well as religions such as [Buddhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhism "Buddhism") and [Hinduism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinduism "Hinduism"). His poem, "Piedra de sol" ("Sunstone"), written in 1957, was praised as a "magnificent" example of surrealist poetry in the presentation speech of his Nobel Prize.

His later poetry dealt with love and eroticism, the nature of time, and Buddhism. He also wrote poetry about his other passion, modern painting, dedicating poems to the work of [Balthus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balthus "Balthus"), [Joan Miró](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joan_Miró "Joan Miró"), [Marcel Duchamp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcel_Duchamp "Marcel Duchamp"), [Antoni Tàpies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antoni_Tàpies "Antoni Tàpies"), [Robert Rauschenberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Rauschenberg "Robert Rauschenberg"), and [Roberto Matta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roberto_Matta "Roberto Matta"). As an essayist, Paz wrote on topics such as [Mexican politics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Politics_of_Mexico "Politics of Mexico") and [economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_Mexico "Economy of Mexico"), [Aztec art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Columbian_art "Pre-Columbian art"), [anthropology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropology "Anthropology"), and [sexuality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_sexuality "Human sexuality"). His book-length essay, *[The Labyrinth of Solitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Labyrinth_of_Solitude "The Labyrinth of Solitude")*, delves into the minds of his countrymen, describing them as hidden behind masks of solitude; due to their [history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Mexico "History of Mexico"), their identity is lost between a pre-Columbian and a Spanish culture, negating either. A key work in understanding [Mexican culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture_of_Mexico "Culture of Mexico"), the essay greatly influenced other Mexican writers, such as [Carlos Fuentes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carlos_Fuentes "Carlos Fuentes"). Ilan Stavans wrote that Paz was "the quintessential surveyor, a Renaissance man".

Octavio Paz

Paz wrote the play *La hija de Rappaccini* in 1956. The plot centers around a young Italian student who wanders about Professor Rappaccini's beautiful gardens, where he espies the professor's daughter, Beatrice. He is horrified to discover the poisonous nature of the garden's beauty. Paz adapted the play from an 1844 short story by American writer [Nathaniel Hawthorne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nathaniel_Hawthorne "Nathaniel Hawthorne"), which was also entitled "[Rappaccini's Daughter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rappaccini's_Daughter "Rappaccini's Daughter")"; he combined Hawthorne's story with sources from the Indian poet [Vishakadatta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vishakadatta "Vishakadatta") and influences from Japanese [Noh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noh "Noh") theatre, Spanish *[autos sacramentales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autos_sacramentales "Autos sacramentales")*, and the poetry of [William Butler Yeats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Butler_Yeats "William Butler Yeats"). The play's opening performance was designed by the Mexican painter [Leonora Carrington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonora_Carrington "Leonora Carrington"). In 1972, Surrealist author [André Pieyre de Mandiargues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/André_Pieyre_de_Mandiargues "André Pieyre de Mandiargues") translated the play into French as *La fille de Rappaccini*  (Editions Mercure de France). First performed in English in 1996 at the [Gate Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gate_Theatre "Gate Theatre") in London, the play was translated and directed by [Sebastian Doggart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sebastian_Doggart "Sebastian Doggart") and starred [Sarah Alexander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarah_Alexander "Sarah Alexander") as Beatrice. The Mexican composer Daniel Catán adapted the play as an opera in 1992.

Paz's other works translated into English include several volumes of essays, some of the more prominent of which are *Alternating Current* (tr. 1973), *Configurations* (tr. 1971), in the [UNESCO Collection of Representative Works](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UNESCO_Collection_of_Representative_Works "UNESCO Collection of Representative Works"), *The Other Mexico* (tr. 1972); and *El Arco y la Lira* (1956; tr. *The Bow and the Lyre*, 1973). In the United States, [Helen Lane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helen_Lane "Helen Lane")'s translation of *Alternating Current* won a [National Book Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Book_Award "National Book Award").
Along with these are volumes of critical studies and biographies, including of [Claude Lévi-Strauss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claude_Lévi-Strauss "Claude Lévi-Strauss") and [Marcel Duchamp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcel_Duchamp "Marcel Duchamp") (both, tr. 1970), and *The Traps of Faith*, an analytical biography of [Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sor_Juana_Inés_de_la_Cruz "Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz"), the Mexican, seventeenth-century nun, [feminist poet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_feminist_poets "List of feminist poets"), mathematician, and thinker.

Paz's works include the poetry collections *¿Águila o sol?* (1951), *La Estación Violenta*, (1956), *Piedra de Sol* (1957). In English, *Early Poems: 1935–1955* (tr. 1974) and *Collected Poems, 1957–1987* (1987) have been edited and translated by [Eliot Weinberger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eliot_Weinberger "Eliot Weinberger"), Paz's principal translator into American English.

## Political thought

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/45/II_Congreso_internacional_de_escritores_para_la_defensa_de_la_cultura%2C_1937.jpg/250px-II_Congreso_internacional_de_escritores_para_la_defensa_de_la_cultura%2C_1937.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:II_Congreso_internacional_de_escritores_para_la_defensa_de_la_cultura,_1937.jpg)

II International Congress of Writers for the Defense of Culture.

Originally, Paz supported the Republicans during the [Spanish Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_Civil_War "Spanish Civil War"), but after learning of the murder of one of his friends by the Stalinist secret police, he became gradually disillusioned. While in Paris in the early 1950s, influenced by [David Rousset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Rousset "David Rousset"), [André Breton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/André_Breton "André Breton") and [Albert Camus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Camus "Albert Camus"), he started publishing his critical views on totalitarianism in general, and particularly against [Joseph Stalin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Stalin "Joseph Stalin"), leader of the Soviet Union.

In his magazines *Plural* and *Vuelta*, Paz exposed the [violations of human rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_rights "Human rights") in Communist regimes, including [Castro's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fidel_Castro "Fidel Castro") [Cuba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuba "Cuba"). This elicited much animosity from sectors of the Latin American Left: in the prologue to Volume IX of his complete works, Paz stated that from the time when he abandoned Communist dogma, the mistrust of many in the Mexican [intelligentsia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intelligentsia "Intelligentsia") started to transform into an intense and open enmity. Paz continued to consider himself a man of the left—the democratic, "liberal" left, not the dogmatic and illiberal one. He also criticized the Mexican government and leading party that dominated the nation for most of the twentieth century.

Politically, Paz was a [social democrat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_democracy "Social democracy"), who became increasingly supportive of liberal ideas without ever renouncing his initial leftist and romantic views. In fact, Paz was "very slippery for anyone thinking in rigid ideological categories," Yvon Grenier wrote in his book on Paz's political thought. "Paz was simultaneously a romantic who spurned materialism and reason, a liberal who championed freedom and democracy, a conservative who respected tradition, and a socialist who lamented the withering of fraternity and equality. An advocate of fundamental transformation in the way we see ourselves and modern society, Paz was also a promoter of incremental change, not revolution."

> There can be no society without poetry, but society can never be realized as poetry, it is never poetic. Sometimes the two terms seek to break apart. They cannot.

— Octavio Paz

In 1990, during the aftermath of the [fall of the Berlin wall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fall_of_the_Berlin_Wall "Fall of the Berlin Wall"), Paz and his *Vuelta* colleagues invited several of the world's writers and intellectuals to Mexico City to discuss the collapse of Communism; writers included [Czesław Miłosz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Czesław_Miłosz "Czesław Miłosz"), [Hugh Thomas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugh_Thomas_(writer) "Hugh Thomas (writer)"), [Daniel Bell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Bell "Daniel Bell"), [Ágnes Heller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ágnes_Heller "Ágnes Heller"), [Cornelius Castoriadis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornelius_Castoriadis "Cornelius Castoriadis"), [Hugh Trevor-Roper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugh_Trevor-Roper "Hugh Trevor-Roper"), [Jean-François Revel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-François_Revel "Jean-François Revel"), [Michael Ignatieff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Ignatieff "Michael Ignatieff"), [Mario Vargas Llosa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mario_Vargas_Llosa "Mario Vargas Llosa"), [Jorge Edwards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jorge_Edwards "Jorge Edwards") and [Carlos Franqui](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carlos_Franqui "Carlos Franqui"). The encounter was called *The Experience of Freedom* (Spanish: *La experiencia de la libertad*), and broadcast on Mexican television from 27 August to 2 September.

Paz said that the literature on Spanish and Portuguese colonialism is biased and "is full of somber details and harsh judgments". He said that there were also immense gains:

> "Not all was horror: over the ruins of the pre-Columbian world the Spanish and Portuguese raised a grandiose historical construction, much of which is still in place. They united many peoples who spoke different languages, worshiped different gods, fought among themselves, or were ignorant of one another. These peoples became united by laws and judicial institutions, but, above all, by language, culture, and religion. Although the losses were enormous, the gains were immense.
> To measure fairly the effect of the Spanish in Mexico, one must emphasize that without them—that is, without the Catholic religion and the culture the Spanish implanted in our country—we would not be what we are. We would probably be a collection of peoples divided by different beliefs, languages, and cultures."

Paz criticized the [Zapatista uprising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zapatista_uprising "Zapatista uprising") in 1994. He spoke broadly in favor of a "military solution" to the uprising of January 1994, and hoped that the "army would soon restore order in the region". With respect to President Zedillo's offensive in February 1995, he signed an open letter that described the offensive as a "legitimate government action" to re-establish the "sovereignty of the nation" and to bring "[Chiapas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chiapas "Chiapas") peace and Mexicans tranquility".

### First literary experiences

Paz was dazzled by *[The Waste Land](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Waste_Land "The Waste Land")* by [T. S. Eliot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T._S._Eliot "T. S. Eliot"), in Enrique Munguia's translation as *El Páramo* which was published in the magazine *Contemporaries* in 1930. As a result of this, although he maintained his primary interest in poetry, Paz also had an unavoidable outlook on prose: "Literally, this dual practice was for me a game of reflections between poetry and prose".

Worried about confirming the existence of a link between [morals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morals "Morals") and [poetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poetry "Poetry"), in 1931, at the age of sixteen, he wrote what would be his first published article, "Ethics of the Artist", in which he posed the question of the duty of an artist among what would be deemed "art of thesis," or pure art, which disqualifies the second as a result of the teaching of tradition. Employing language that resembles a religious style and, paradoxically, a [Marxist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marxism "Marxism") one, Paz finds the true value of art in its purpose and meaning, for which the followers of pure art—of whom he is *not* one—are found in an isolated position and favor the [Kantian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kantianism "Kantianism") idea of the "man that loses all relation with the world".

The magazine *Barandal* appeared in August 1931, put together by [Rafael López Malo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rafael_López_Malo?action=edit&redlink=1 "Rafael López Malo"), Salvador Toscano, [Arnulfo Martínez Lavalle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arnulfo_Martínez_Lavalle?action=edit&redlink=1 "Arnulfo Martínez Lavalle") and Paz; all of them were not yet in their youth, except for Salvador Toscano, who was a renowned writer thanks to his parents. Rafael López participated in the magazine "Modern" and, along with [Miguel D. Martínez Rendón](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miguel_D._Martínez_Rendón?action=edit&redlink=1 "Miguel D. Martínez Rendón"), in the [movimiento de los agoristas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Movimiento_de_los_agoristas?action=edit&redlink=1 "Movimiento de los agoristas"), although it was more commented on and known by high-school students, over all for his poem, "The Golden Beast". Octavio Paz Solórzano became known in his circle as the occasional author of literary narratives that appeared in the Sunday newspaper add-in [El Universal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El_Universal_(México) "El Universal (México)"), as well as [Ireneo Paz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ireneo_Paz "Ireneo Paz") which was the name that gave a street in [Mixcoac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mixcoac "Mixcoac") identity.
