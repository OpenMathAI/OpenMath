**Oliver Eaton Williamson** (September 27, 1932 – May 21, 2020) was an American [economist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economist "Economist"), a professor at the [University of California, Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Berkeley "University of California, Berkeley"), and recipient of the 2009 [Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Memorial_Prize_in_Economic_Sciences "Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences"), which he shared with [Elinor Ostrom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elinor_Ostrom "Elinor Ostrom").

His contributions to [transaction cost economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transaction_cost "Transaction cost") and the [theory of the firm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theory_of_the_firm "Theory of the firm") have been influential in the [social sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_sciences "Social sciences"), [law and economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_and_economics "Law and economics"). Williamson described his work as "a blend of soft social science and abstract economic theory".

## Life and career

Oliver "Olly" Williamson was born in [Superior, Wisconsin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superior,_Wisconsin "Superior, Wisconsin"), on 27 September 1932. He was the son of Sara Lucille (Dunn) and Scott Williamson, both of whom were high school teachers. As a child, Williamson attended [Central High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superior_High_School_(Wisconsin) "Superior High School (Wisconsin)") in Superior.

Williamson's dual enrollment between Ripon College and MIT earned him his bachelor's degree in management from the [MIT Sloan School of Management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MIT_Sloan_School_of_Management "MIT Sloan School of Management") in 1955. During his time in his undergraduate academic career, his studies in engineering sparked his initial interest in transaction costs. After graduating, he worked as a project engineer for [General Electric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Electric "General Electric"), as well as the Central Intelligence Agency.

Williamson received an [MBA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master_of_Business_Administration "Master of Business Administration") from [Stanford University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford_University "Stanford University") in 1960, and his [PhD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PhD "PhD") from [Carnegie Mellon University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carnegie_Mellon_University "Carnegie Mellon University"), formerly Carnegie Tech, in 1963. His dissertation was titled 'The Economics of Discretionary Behaviour: Managerial Objectives in a Theory of the Firm'. A student of [Ronald Coase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Coase "Ronald Coase"), [Herbert A. Simon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbert_A._Simon "Herbert A. Simon") and [Richard Cyert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Cyert "Richard Cyert"), he specialized in [transaction cost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transaction_cost "Transaction cost") economics.

From 1963 to 1965 he was an assistant professor of economics at the University of California, Berkeley. From 1965 to 1983 he was a professor at the [University of Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Pennsylvania "University of Pennsylvania") and from 1983 to 1988, a Gordon B. Tweedy Professor of [Economics of Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_and_economics "Law and economics") and Organization at [Yale University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yale_University "Yale University"). While at Yale, Williamson was a founder of *[The Journal of Law, Economics, & Organization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Journal_of_Law,_Economics,_&_Organization "The Journal of Law, Economics, & Organization")*. He held professorships in business administration, economics, and law at the [University of California, Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Berkeley "University of California, Berkeley") since 1988 and was the [Edgar F. Kaiser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edgar_Kaiser_Sr. "Edgar Kaiser Sr.") Professor Emeritus at the [Haas School of Business](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haas_School_of_Business "Haas School of Business"). As a [Fulbright Distinguished Chair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S.-Italy_Fulbright_Commission "U.S.-Italy Fulbright Commission"), in 1999 he taught economics at the [University of Siena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Siena "University of Siena").

Found to be one of the most cited authors in the social sciences, in 2009, he was awarded the [Nobel Memorial Prize in Economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Memorial_Prize_in_Economics "Nobel Memorial Prize in Economics") for "his analysis of economic governance, especially the boundaries of the firm", sharing it with [Elinor Ostrom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elinor_Ostrom "Elinor Ostrom"). Williamson died on May 21, 2020, in Berkeley, California.

## Theory

By drawing attention at a high theoretical level to equivalences and differences between market and non-market decision-making, management and service provision, Williamson was influential in the 1980s and 1990s debates on the boundaries between the [public](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_sector "Public sector") and [private sectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Private_sector "Private sector").

His focus on the costs of transactions led Williamson to distinguish between repeated case-by-case bargaining on the one hand and relationship-specific contracts on the other. For example, the repeated purchasing of coal from a [spot market](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spot_market "Spot market") to meet the daily or weekly needs of an [electric utility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_power "Electric power") would represent case-by-case bargaining. But over time, the utility is likely to form ongoing relationships with a specific supplier, and the economics of the relationship-specific dealings will be importantly different, he argued.

Other economists have tested Williamson's transaction-cost theories in empirical contexts. One important example is a paper by [Paul L. Joskow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Joskow "Paul Joskow"), "[Contract](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contract "Contract") Duration and Relationship-Specific Investments: Empirical Evidence from [Coal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal "Coal") [Markets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Market_(economics) "Market (economics)")", in *[American Economic Review](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Economic_Review "American Economic Review")*, March 1987. The [incomplete contracts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contract_theory#Incomplete_contracts "Contract theory") approach to the [theory of the firm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theory_of_the_firm "Theory of the firm") and [corporate finance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corporate_finance "Corporate finance") is partly based on the work of Williamson and Coase.

Williamson was credited with the development of the term "information impactedness", which applies in situations of unequal access to information. As he explained in *Markets and Hierarchies*, it exists "mainly because of uncertainty and [opportunism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_opportunism "Economic opportunism"), though [bounded rationality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bounded_rationality "Bounded rationality") is involved as well. It exists when true underlying circumstances relevant to the transaction, or related set of transactions, are known to one or more parties but cannot be costlessly discerned by or displayed for others". Thus, Williamson is to be counted among those who have taken issue with the view that the firm is another type of market, characterized by a nexus of contracts. In his own words: "But to regard the corporation only as a nexus of contracts misses much of what is truly distinctive about this mode of governance".

## Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/28/Oliver_Williamson%27s_pipe_holder_at_the_Nobel_Museum_%2851953%29.jpg/250px-Oliver_Williamson%27s_pipe_holder_at_the_Nobel_Museum_%2851953%29.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Oliver_Williamson's_pipe_holder_at_the_Nobel_Museum_(51953).jpg)

Williamson's pipe holder on display at the [Nobel Prize Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_Museum "Nobel Prize Museum")

In 2009, the [Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Swedish_Academy_of_Sciences "Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences") cited Williamson and [Elinor Ostrom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elinor_Ostrom "Elinor Ostrom") to share the 10-million [Swedish kronor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swedish_kronor "Swedish kronor") (£910,000; $1.44 million) prize "for his analysis of economic governance, especially the boundaries of the firm". Williamson, in the BBC's paraphrase of the academy's reasoning, "developed a theory where business firms served as structures for conflict resolution".

## Personal life

He met his wife Dolores Celini in 1957, while they both lived in Washington, D.C. They had five children.

## Legacy

For his dedication to the field and his service to the institution, the Haas School of Business at Berkeley established the Williamson Award in honor of Oliver Williamson. This prestigious award is presented to outstanding faculty members who embody Berkeley's four Defining Leadership Principles - Question the Status Quo, Confidence Without Attitude, Students Always, and Beyond Yourself. Beginning in fiscal year 2013, the Williamson Award has been presented to the following faculty members:

- 2013–2014: Professor [John Morgan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Morgan_(economist) "John Morgan (economist)")
- 2014–2015: Professor [Teck Ho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teck-Hua_Ho "Teck-Hua Ho")
- 2015–2016: Professor [Toby Stuart](https://haas.berkeley.edu/faculty/stuart-toby/)
- 2016–2017: Professor [Andy Rose](https://haas.berkeley.edu/faculty/rose-andrew/)
- 2019–2020: Professor [Nancy Wallace](https://haas.berkeley.edu/faculty/wallace-nancy/)
- 2022–2023: Associate Professor [Ned Augenblick](https://haas.berkeley.edu/faculty/augenblick-ned/)
