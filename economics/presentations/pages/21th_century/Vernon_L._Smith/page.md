**Vernon Lomax Smith** (born January 1, 1927) is an American [economist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economist "Economist") who is currently a professor of economics and law at [Chapman University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chapman_University "Chapman University"). He was formerly the McLellan/Regent's Professor of Economics at the [University of Arizona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Arizona "University of Arizona"), a professor of economics and law at [George Mason University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Mason_University "George Mason University"), and a board member of the [Mercatus Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercatus_Center "Mercatus Center"). Along with [Daniel Kahneman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Kahneman "Daniel Kahneman"), Smith won the 2002 [Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Memorial_Prize_in_Economic_Sciences "Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences") for his contributions to [behavioral economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Behavioral_economics "Behavioral economics") and his work in the field of [experimental economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Experimental_economics "Experimental economics"), which helped establish "laboratory experiments as a tool in empirical economic analysis, especially in the study of alternative market mechanisms."

Smith is the founder and president of the [International Foundation for Research in Experimental Economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Foundation_for_Research_in_Experimental_Economics?action=edit&redlink=1 "International Foundation for Research in Experimental Economics") (IFREEE), a member of the [Independent Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_Institute "Independent Institute")'s board of advisors, and a senior fellow at the [Cato Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cato_Institute "Cato Institute") in [Washington D.C.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington_D.C. "Washington D.C.") He was elected a [Fellow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_fellows_of_the_Econometric_Society "List of fellows of the Econometric Society") of the [Econometric Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Econometric_Society "Econometric Society") in 1987, and was elected to the [National Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Academy_of_Sciences "National Academy of Sciences") in 1995. In 2004, Smith was awarded an [honorary doctorate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honorary_degree "Honorary degree") by the [Universidad Francisco Marroquín](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universidad_Francisco_Marroquín "Universidad Francisco Marroquín"), where the Vernon Smith Center for Experimental Economics Research is named for him. He was also a founding board member of the Center for Growth and Opportunity at Utah State University. As of 2023, Smith also sits on the advisory board of the Madden Center for Value Creation at [Florida Atlantic University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_Atlantic_University "Florida Atlantic University").

## Early life and education

Smith was born in [Wichita, Kansas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wichita,_Kansas "Wichita, Kansas"), where he attended [Wichita North High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wichita_North_High_School "Wichita North High School") and [Friends University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friends_University "Friends University"). Grover Bougher, Vernon's mother's first husband, who worked as a fireman on the Santa Fe railroad, died in an accident. The life insurance money provided by the Santa Fe railroad was invested in a farm which became the sole means of survival for Vernon's family during the tough years of the [Great Depression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Depression "Great Depression"). His future interests were influenced by his childhood at the farm.

Smith received his bachelor's degree in [electrical engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_engineering "Electrical engineering") from [Caltech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caltech "Caltech") in 1949, an [M.A.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master_of_Arts "Master of Arts") [in economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master_of_Economics "Master of Economics") from the [University of Kansas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Kansas "University of Kansas") in 1952, and his [Ph.D.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctorate "Doctorate") in economics from [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University "Harvard University") in 1955. His Ph.D. thesis title was *A theoretical and empirical inquiry into the economic replacement of capital equipment*.

## Academic career

Smith's first teaching post was at the [Krannert School of Management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krannert_School_of_Management "Krannert School of Management"), [Purdue University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purdue_University "Purdue University"), which he held from 1955 until 1967, attaining the rank of full professor.

Smith also taught as a visiting associate professor at [Stanford University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford_University "Stanford University") (1961–1962) and there made contact with [Sidney Siegel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidney_Siegel "Sidney Siegel"), who was also doing work in [experimental economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Experimental_economics "Experimental economics"). Smith moved with his family to [Massachusetts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massachusetts "Massachusetts") and got a position first at [Brown University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brown_University "Brown University") (1967–1968) and then at the [University of Massachusetts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Massachusetts_Amherst "University of Massachusetts Amherst") (1968–1972). Smith also received appointments at the [Center for Advanced Study in the Behavioral Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_for_Advanced_Study_in_the_Behavioral_Sciences "Center for Advanced Study in the Behavioral Sciences") (1972–1973) and [Caltech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caltech "Caltech") (1973–1975).

Much of the research that earned Smith the [Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Memorial_Prize_in_Economic_Sciences "Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences") was conducted at the [University of Arizona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Arizona "University of Arizona") between 1976 and 2001. In 2001, Smith left Arizona for [George Mason University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Mason_University "George Mason University"). From 2003 to 2006, he held the Rasmuson Chair of Economics at the [University of Alaska Anchorage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Alaska_Anchorage "University of Alaska Anchorage"). In 2008, Smith founded the Economic Science Institute at [Chapman University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chapman_University "Chapman University") in [Orange, California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orange,_California "Orange, California").

Smith has served on the board of editors of the *[American Economic Review](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Economic_Review "American Economic Review")*, the *[Cato Journal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cato_Journal "Cato Journal")*, *[Journal of Economic Behavior and Organization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Journal_of_Economic_Behavior_and_Organization "Journal of Economic Behavior and Organization")*, *Science*, *[Economic Theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_Theory "Economic Theory")*, *Economic Design*, and the *[Journal of Economic Methodology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Journal_of_Economic_Methodology "Journal of Economic Methodology")*. He also served as an expert for the [Copenhagen Consensus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copenhagen_Consensus "Copenhagen Consensus").

### Academic work

Smith began his work in [experimental economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Experimental_economics "Experimental economics") at [Purdue University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purdue_University "Purdue University"). As Smith describes it:

> In the Autumn semester, 1955, I taught Principles of Economics, and found it a challenge to convey basic microeconomic theory to students. Why/how could any market approximate a competitive equilibrium? I resolved that on the first day of class the following semester, I would try running a market experiment that would give the students an opportunity to experience an actual market, and me the opportunity to observe one in which I knew, but they did not know what were the alleged driving conditions of supply and demand in that market.

In framing the experiment, Smith varied certain institutional parameters seen in the first classroom economics experiments as conducted by [Edward Chamberlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Chamberlin "Edward Chamberlin"): in particular, he ran the experiments for several trading periods, to give the student subjects time to train.

At Caltech, [Charles Plott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Plott "Charles Plott") encouraged Smith to formalize the methodology of experimental economics, which he did in two articles. In 1976, "Experimental Economics: Induced Value Theory" was published in the *American Economic Review* (AER). It was the first articulation of the principle behind economic experiments. Six years later, these principles were expanded in "Microeconomic Systems as an Experimental Science," also in the AER. This paper adapts the principles of [mechanism design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanism_design "Mechanism design"), a microeconomic system developed by [Leonid Hurwicz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonid_Hurwicz "Leonid Hurwicz"), to the development of economic experiments. In Hurwicz's formulation, a microeconomic system consists of an economic environment, an economic institution (or economic mechanism), and an economic outcome. The economic environment is simply the preferences of the people in the economy and the production capabilities of the firms in the economy. The key insight in this formulation is that the economic outcome can be affected by the economic institution. The mechanism design provides a formal means for tests of the performance of an economic institution, and [experimental economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Experimental_economics "Experimental economics"), as developed by Smith, provided a means for formal empirical assessment of the performance of economic institutions. The second main contribution of the paper is to the technique of induced values, the method used in controlled laboratory experiments in economics, political science, and psychology, which allows experimental economists to create a replica of a market in a laboratory. Subjects in an experiment are told that they can produce a "commodity" at a cost and then sell it to buyers. The seller earns the difference between the price received and its cost. Buyers are told that the commodity has a value to them when they consume it, and they earn the difference between the value of the commodity to them and its price. Using the technique, Smith and his coauthors have examined the performance of alternative trading mechanisms in resource allocation.

In February 2011, Smith participated in the "Visiting Scholars Series" at the Nicholas Academic Centers in [Santa Ana, California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santa_Ana,_California "Santa Ana, California"), conducted in collaboration with Chapman University. Smith and his colleague [Bart Wilson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bart_Wilson "Bart Wilson") conducted experiments designed to expose high school students from underserved neighborhoods to market dynamics and how concepts such as altruism influence economic behavior.

Smith has authored or coauthored articles and books on capital theory, [finance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finance "Finance"), [natural resource economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_resource_economics "Natural resource economics") and [experimental economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Experimental_economics "Experimental economics"). He was also one of the first to propose the [combinatorial auction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combinatorial_auction "Combinatorial auction") design, with Stephen J. Rassenti and Robert L. Bulfin in 1982.

In January 2009, Smith signed a public petition opposing the passage of the [American Recovery and Reinvestment Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Recovery_and_Reinvestment_Act "American Recovery and Reinvestment Act"). In a 2010 Econ Journal Watch study, Smith was found to be one of the most active petition-signers among US economists.

The Vernon Smith Prize for the Advancement of [Austrian Economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austrian_Economics "Austrian Economics") is named after him and is sponsored by the European Center of Austrian Economics.

Vernon Smith is renowned for his seminal contributions to the elucidation of [spontaneous order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spontaneous_order "Spontaneous order") within the field of economics. He posits spontaneous order as the organic emergence of structure and coherence from apparent disorder, a phenomenon ubiquitously observed in both social and economic contexts. His work on this has even extended out to using methods of [self-organization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-organization "Self-organization") from the physical sciences to model sociability. That framework shows how the same boundedly rational agents can exhibit either self-interested or reciprocal, socially responsive behavior as an emergent consequence of the interaction environment, rather than requiring economics to assume different kinds of people or rewrite the model for each setting. Smith has expounded upon the notion of spontaneous order in the context of his pedagogical endeavors as well. In his instructive course titled "Spontaneous Order and the Law" at the [Fowler School of Law, Chapman University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fowler_School_of_Law,_Chapman_University?action=edit&redlink=1 "Fowler School of Law, Chapman University"), he illuminates how social norms and legal frameworks emerge organically through human interaction, gradually coalescing from apparent chaos into structured societal systems.

In recent years, Vernon L. Smith's research has expanded to include the study of [Neuroeconomics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neuroeconomics "Neuroeconomics") where he integrates economic theory with neuroscience to understand decision-making processes. Vernon Smith's work in this area has led to new insights into how brain activity influences economic decisions. Additionally, he has been instrumental in promoting the use of experimental methods in economics education, advocating for a hands-on approach to learning economic concepts. Some of these contributions were used in [Neuroeconomics Lab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neuroeconomics_Lab?action=edit&redlink=1 "Neuroeconomics Lab") books published by [Academic Press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academic_Press "Academic Press") (2014).

## Personal life

In February 2005, Smith publicly attributed features of his personality to [Asperger syndrome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asperger_syndrome "Asperger syndrome") after a process of [self-diagnosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-diagnosis "Self-diagnosis").

Smith donated his [Nobel Prize medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_medal "Nobel Prize medal") to [Chapman University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chapman_University "Chapman University") in 2009.
