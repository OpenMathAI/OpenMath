**Peter Arthur Diamond** (born April 29, 1940) is an American [economist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economist "Economist") known for his analysis of [U.S. Social Security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Social_Security "U.S. Social Security") policy and his work as an advisor to the Advisory Council on Social Security in the late 1980s and 1990s. He was awarded the [Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Memorial_Prize_in_Economic_Sciences "Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences") in 2010, along with [Dale T. Mortensen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dale_T._Mortensen "Dale T. Mortensen") and [Christopher A. Pissarides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_A._Pissarides "Christopher A. Pissarides"). He is an [Institute Professor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Institute_Professors "List of Institute Professors") at the [Massachusetts Institute of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massachusetts_Institute_of_Technology "Massachusetts Institute of Technology"). On June 6, 2011, he withdrew his nomination to serve on the [Federal Reserve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Reserve "Federal Reserve")'s board of governors, citing intractable [Republican](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republican_Party_(United_States) "Republican Party (United States)") opposition for 14 months.

## Early life and education

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/ac/Nobel_Prize_2010-Press_Conference_KVA-DSC_8019.jpg/250px-Nobel_Prize_2010-Press_Conference_KVA-DSC_8019.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Nobel_Prize_2010-Press_Conference_KVA-DSC_8019.jpg)

Peter Diamond, [Dale T. Mortensen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dale_T._Mortensen "Dale T. Mortensen"), [Christopher A. Pissarides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_A._Pissarides "Christopher A. Pissarides"), [Konstantin Novoselov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Konstantin_Novoselov "Konstantin Novoselov"), [Andre Geim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andre_Geim "Andre Geim"), [Akira Suzuki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akira_Suzuki_(chemist) "Akira Suzuki (chemist)"), [Ei-ichi Negishi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ei-ichi_Negishi "Ei-ichi Negishi"), and [Richard Heck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Heck "Richard Heck"), Nobel Prize Laureates 2010, at a press conference at the [Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Swedish_Academy_of_Sciences "Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences") in Stockholm.

Diamond was born to a [Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Jews "American Jews") family in [New York City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_City "New York City"). His grandparents immigrated to the U.S. at the turn of the 20th century. His mother's parents and six older siblings came from Poland. His father's parents met in New York, she came from Russia and he came from Romania. His parents, both born in 1908, grew up in [New York City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_City "New York City") and never lived outside the metropolitan area. Both finished high school and went to work, his father studying at [Brooklyn Law School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brooklyn_Law_School "Brooklyn Law School") at night while selling shoes during the day. They married in 1929. He has one brother, Richard, born in 1934.

He started public school in the Bronx, and switched to suburban public schools in the second grade when the family moved to [Woodmere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woodmere,_New_York "Woodmere, New York"), on [Long Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_Island "Long Island"). He eventually graduated from [Lawrence High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawrence_High_School_(New_York) "Lawrence High School (New York)"). He earned a [bachelor's degree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor's_degree "Bachelor's degree") *summa cum laude* in [mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics "Mathematics") from [Yale University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yale_University "Yale University") (1960), and a [Ph.D.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ph.D. "Ph.D.") at the [Massachusetts Institute of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massachusetts_Institute_of_Technology "Massachusetts Institute of Technology") (1963).

## Career

He was an [assistant professor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assistant_professor "Assistant professor") at the [University of California, Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Berkeley "University of California, Berkeley"), from 1963 to 1965 and an acting [associate professor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associate_professor "Associate professor") there before joining the [MIT faculty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MIT_faculty "MIT faculty") as an associate professor in 1966. Diamond was promoted to [full professor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Full_professor "Full professor") in 1970, served as head of the Department of Economics in 1985–86 and was named an [Institute Professor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Institute_Professors "List of Institute Professors") in 1997.

In 1968, Diamond was elected a fellow and served as president of the [Econometric Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Econometric_Society "Econometric Society"). In 2003, he served as president of the [American Economic Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Economic_Association "American Economic Association"). He is a Fellow of the [American Academy of Arts and Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Academy_of_Arts_and_Sciences "American Academy of Arts and Sciences") (1978), a member of the [National Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_National_Academy_of_Sciences "United States National Academy of Sciences") (1984), and is a founding member of the National Academy of Social Insurance (1988). Diamond was the 2008 recipient of the Robert M. Ball Award for Outstanding Achievements in Social Insurance, awarded by NASI. As a [Fulbright Distinguished Chair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S.-Italy_Fulbright_Commission "U.S.-Italy Fulbright Commission"), in 2000 he taught economics at the [University of Siena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Siena "University of Siena").

Diamond wrote a book on Social Security with [Peter R. Orszag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_R._Orszag "Peter R. Orszag"), President Obama's former director of the [Office of Management and Budget](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Office_of_Management_and_Budget "Office of Management and Budget"), titled *Saving Social security: a balanced approach* (2004,-5, [Brookings Institution Press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brookings_Institution_Press "Brookings Institution Press")). An earlier paper from [Brookings Institution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brookings_Institution "Brookings Institution") introduced their ideas.

In April 2010, Diamond, along with [Janet Yellen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Janet_Yellen "Janet Yellen") and [Sarah Bloom Raskin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarah_Bloom_Raskin "Sarah Bloom Raskin"), was nominated by [President](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._President "U.S. President") [Barack Obama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barack_Obama "Barack Obama") to fill the vacancies on the [Federal Reserve Board](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Reserve_Board "Federal Reserve Board"). In August 2010, the Senate returned Diamond's nomination to the White House, effectively rejecting his nomination. President Obama renominated him in September. In June 2011, following a third round of consideration for the Fed seat, Diamond wrote in a *New York Times* [op-ed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Op-ed "Op-ed") column that he planned to withdraw his name. In the column, he strongly criticized the nomination process and "partisan polarization" in [Washington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington,_D.C. "Washington, D.C."), saying he was effectively blocked by Republicans on the [Senate Banking Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senate_Banking_Committee "Senate Banking Committee"). He also detailed the consideration process, saying that in the first and second rounds, three Republicans had favored his confirmation. In the third, when his name was resubmitted in January 2011, the Republicans all followed ranking minority member Shelby (R, [Alabama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alabama "Alabama")) in voting against it. Diamond continued, quoting Shelby:

> "Does Dr. Diamond have any experience in conducting monetary policy? No," [Shelby] said in March. "His academic work has been on pensions and labor market theory." But [Diamond began his reply, in the column] understanding the labor market—and the process by which workers and jobs come together and separate—is critical to devising an effective monetary policy.

Diamond went on to discuss how his expertise would, he felt, have benefited the central bank and his opinion that "[s]killed analytical thinking should not be drowned out by mistaken, ideologically driven views." In a statement, Shelby "wouldn't be drawn into a public spat with the nominee," saying simply "I have said many times that I commend Dr. Diamond's talent and career. I wish him the best in the future."

[Ben Bernanke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ben_Bernanke "Ben Bernanke") ([Nobel Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Memorial_Prize_in_Economic_Sciences "Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences") winner and former [chairman of the Fed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chairman_of_the_Federal_Reserve "Chairman of the Federal Reserve")) was once a student of Diamond.

In October 2010, Diamond was awarded the [Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Economic_Sciences "Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences"), along with [Dale T. Mortensen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dale_T._Mortensen "Dale T. Mortensen") from [Northwestern University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northwestern_University "Northwestern University") and [Christopher A. Pissarides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_A._Pissarides "Christopher A. Pissarides") from the [London School of Economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_School_of_Economics "London School of Economics") "for their analysis of markets with search frictions".

In 2011 he received The John R. Commons Award from [Omicron Delta Epsilon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omicron_Delta_Epsilon "Omicron Delta Epsilon"), the economics honor society.

[Andrei Shleifer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrei_Shleifer "Andrei Shleifer") and [Emmanuel Saez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emmanuel_Saez "Emmanuel Saez") are two of his doctoral supervisees who won the John Bates Clark Medal for the best American economist under the age of 40.

Diamond has been married to Kate (Priscilla Myrick) since 1966. They have two sons.

## Professional activity

Diamond has made fundamental contributions to a variety of areas, including [government debt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_debt "Government debt") and [capital accumulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capital_accumulation "Capital accumulation"), [capital markets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capital_markets "Capital markets") and risk sharing, [optimal taxation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optimal_taxation "Optimal taxation"), search and matching in [labor markets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labor_markets "Labor markets"), and [social insurance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_insurance "Social insurance").

Diamond (1965) extended the [Ramsey–Cass–Koopmans model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramsey–Cass–Koopmans_model "Ramsey–Cass–Koopmans model"), from a representative infinitely-lived [agent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agent_(economics) "Agent (economics)") to a setup where new individuals are continually being born and old individuals are continually dying. He built on a framework developed by [Paul Samuelson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Samuelson "Paul Samuelson"), who had termed it "an exact consumption-loan model."

Since individuals born at different times attain different utility levels, it is not clear how to evaluate [social welfare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_welfare "Social welfare"). One of the main results of this paper is that the decentralized equilibrium might not be dynamically [Pareto efficient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pareto_efficient "Pareto efficient"), even though markets are competitive and externalities are absent. In particular, depending on the preferences and technology, the economy might find itself saving too much, pushing the capital stock above what Edmund Phelps called the [Golden Rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_Rule_savings_rate "Golden Rule savings rate") level. In this situation, government debt can crowd out capital and, in doing so, increase welfare.

### Diamond–Mirrlees production efficiency result

The *Diamond–Mirrlees production efficiency result* follows from a set of assumptions which characterise what can be called a 'DM world'.

It is characterised by 7 assumptions:
i) perfect competition
ii) constant returns to scale to production
iii) lump sum taxation is not possible
iv) there is a revenue requirement i.e. the government has to raise revenue to fund its expenditures
v) full instrument set: the government has the flexibility to levy taxes on all commodities and all factors of production in the economy
vi) non-satiation in at least one good
vii) individualistic social welfare function.

Under these assumptions, it can be shown that the second best allocation requires [production efficiency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_efficiency#Allocative_and_productive_efficiency "Economic efficiency") to be preserved throughout the economy. This result arises from the assumptions that characterise the DM world:

- The production side of the economy is independent from the consumption side of the economy (assumption i and ii): perfect competition and constant returns to scale implies no profits (if not producers would increase production infinitely and thus profits). This means that the income of consumers do not depend on the producer prices. In addition, this also means that the incidence of the tax would fall 100% on consumers. Again, this is an application of the [theory of the second best](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theory_of_the_second_best "Theory of the second best"): Pareto efficiency should be restored in independent markets.
- The economy is not able to operate in a first-best world (assumption iii and iv): the government must raise revenue but is not able to raise this through lump sum taxation, thus a Pareto optimal allocation of resources is not possible. Note, this is the only irremovable distortion in the DM world.
- The government has a full instrument set that allows any configuration of prices to be achieved, which allows the government to bring about any configuration of relative consumer prices that is consistent with the revenue requirement.

The key idea is that when the government can control all consumer prices, the producer prices are independent from the consumer prices and the consumption decision part of the optimal taxation problem becomes independent of the production decision. The implication of the result is that there should be no taxes on intermediate goods and imports. Another implication is that public and private sector production should be based on the same relative prices. In practice, one needs to consider if the assumptions of the DM world are likely to apply; nevertheless, the efficiency result is a useful benchmark against which to judge whether any policy violation of production efficiency is justified.

### Labor market search and match

Diamond (1982) is one of the first papers which explicitly models the [search process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matching_theory_(macroeconomics) "Matching theory (macroeconomics)") involved in making trades and hiring workers, which results in equilibrium unemployment.

### Social Security policy

Diamond has focused much of his professional career on the analysis of U.S. [Social Security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_Security_(United_States) "Social Security (United States)") policy as well as its analogs in other countries, such as China. In numerous journal articles and books, he has presented analyses of social welfare programs in general and the American Social Security Administration in particular. He has frequently proposed policy adjustments, such as incremental but small increases in social security contributions using [actuarial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Actuary "Actuary") tables to adjust for changes in [life expectancy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life_expectancy "Life expectancy") and an increase in the proportion of earnings that are subject to taxation.
