**Guido Wilhelmus Imbens** (born 3 September 1963) is a Dutch-American [economist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economist "Economist") whose research concerns econometrics and statistics. He holds the Applied Econometrics Professorship in Economics (Emeritus) at the [Stanford Graduate School of Business](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford_Graduate_School_of_Business "Stanford Graduate School of Business") at [Stanford University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford_University "Stanford University"), where he has taught since 2012.

In 2021, Imbens was awarded half of the [Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Memorial_Prize_in_Economic_Sciences "Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences") jointly with [Joshua Angrist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joshua_Angrist "Joshua Angrist") "for their methodological contributions to the analysis of [causal relationships](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Causality "Causality")." Their work focused on [natural experiments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_experiment "Natural experiment"), which can offer empirical data in contexts where [controlled experimentation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_control "Scientific control") may be expensive, time-consuming, or unethical. In 1994 Imbens and Angrist introduced the [local average treatment effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_average_treatment_effect "Local average treatment effect") (LATE) framework, an influential mathematical methodology for reliably inferring causation from natural experiments that accounted for and defined the limitations of such inferences. Imbens' work with Angrist, together with the work of [Alan Krueger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_Krueger "Alan Krueger") and co-recipient of the prize [David Card](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Card "David Card") is credited with catalysing the "[credibility revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Credibility_revolution "Credibility revolution")" in empirical microeconomics.

## Early life and education

Guido Wilhelmus Imbens was born on 3 September 1963 in [Geldrop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geldrop "Geldrop"), the Netherlands. As a child, Imbens was an avid chess player. In a 2021 interview, Imbens connected his passion for [econometrics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Econometrics "Econometrics") to his childhood interest in the game.

In high school Imbens was introduced to the work of Dutch economist [Jan Tinbergen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jan_Tinbergen "Jan Tinbergen"). Influenced by Tinbergen's work, Imbens chose to study econometrics at [Erasmus University Rotterdam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erasmus_University_Rotterdam "Erasmus University Rotterdam"), where Tinbergen had taught and established a program in econometrics. Imbens graduated with a [Candidate's degree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Candidate_(degree)#The_Low_Countries "Candidate (degree)") in Econometrics from Erasmus University Rotterdam in 1983. He subsequently obtained an [M.Sc.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master_of_Science "Master of Science") degree *[with distinction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_honors "Latin honors")* in Economics and Econometrics from the [University of Hull](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Hull "University of Hull") in [Kingston upon Hull](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingston_upon_Hull "Kingston upon Hull"), UK in 1986.

In 1986, one of Imbens' mentors at the University of Hull, [Anthony Lancaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tony_Lancaster "Tony Lancaster"), moved to [Brown University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brown_University "Brown University") in [Providence, Rhode Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Providence,_Rhode_Island "Providence, Rhode Island"). Imbens followed Lancaster to Brown to pursue further graduate and doctoral studies. Imbens received an [A.M.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master_of_Arts "Master of Arts") and a [Ph.D.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Philosophy "Doctor of Philosophy") degree in economics from Brown in 1989 and 1991, respectively.

## Career

Imbens has taught at [Tilburg University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tilburg_University "Tilburg University") (1989–1990), [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University "Harvard University") (1990–97, 2007–12), the [University of California, Los Angeles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Los_Angeles "University of California, Los Angeles") (1997–2001), and the [University of California, Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Berkeley "University of California, Berkeley") (2001–07). He specializes in econometrics, which are particular methods for drawing [causal inference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Causal_inference "Causal inference"). He became the editor of *[Econometrica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Econometrica "Econometrica")* in 2019, with his term anticipated (as of 2022) to end in 2025. As of 2021, he is a professor of [applied econometrics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Econometrics "Econometrics") and [economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economics "Economics") at [Stanford Graduate School of Business](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford_Graduate_School_of_Business "Stanford Graduate School of Business"). He is also a senior fellow at the [Stanford Institute for Economic Policy Research (SIEPR)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford_Institute_for_Economic_Policy_Research "Stanford Institute for Economic Policy Research") and a professor of economics at the institute's School of Humanities and Sciences.

Imbens is a fellow of the [Econometric Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Econometric_Society "Econometric Society") (2001) and the [American Academy of Arts and Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Academy_of_Arts_and_Sciences "American Academy of Arts and Sciences") (2009). Imbens was elected to the [Royal Netherlands Academy of Arts and Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Netherlands_Academy_of_Arts_and_Sciences "Royal Netherlands Academy of Arts and Sciences") as a foreign member in 2017. He was elected as a [Fellow of the American Statistical Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow_of_the_American_Statistical_Association "Fellow of the American Statistical Association") in 2020.

### Econometrics and work on causal relationships

Working with fellow economists including [Joshua Angrist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joshua_Angrist "Joshua Angrist") and [Alan Krueger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_Krueger "Alan Krueger"), Imbens focused on developing methodologies and frameworks that help economists use a kind of real-life situations known as [natural experiments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_experiment "Natural experiment") to test hypotheses about [causal relationships](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Causal_relationships "Causal relationships"), such as the impact of additional years of school education on earnings. His frameworks for causal relationships study found use in multiple other fields including social and [biomedical sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biomedical_sciences "Biomedical sciences"). It provided researchers with tools to understand the limitations of real-world experiments, improving their ability to better understand the effects of field and [experimental data](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Experimental_data "Experimental data") based interventions.

In one of his earliest collaborations with Angrist, Imbens introduced a concept called [Local Average Treatment Effect (LATE)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_average_treatment_effect "Local average treatment effect") to draw causal inference from observational data. In a 1994 *[Econometrica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Econometrica "Econometrica")* paper titled "Identification and Estimation of Local Average Treatment Effects", the pair employed the idea of natural experiments, where one studies the effects of key changes by using chance and randomization that naturally occur in the real world, instead of [controlled conditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_control "Scientific control"), which can be expensive, time-consuming, or even unethical. The paper and the concept had significant impact on other research efforts across econometrics, statistics and other fields.

In a 2001 paper, Imbens partnered with statistician [Donald Rubin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Rubin "Donald Rubin") and economist [Bruce Sacerdote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bruce_Sacerdote "Bruce Sacerdote") to study the impact of [unearned earnings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unearned_income "Unearned income") on [labor supply](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labour_supply "Labour supply"), i.e. the implications of policy interventions such as [Universal Basic Income](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_basic_income "Universal basic income") or other federal and state wage assistance programs on citizens' willingness to participate in the [labor force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Workforce "Workforce") and the eventual impact on labor supply. To devise a [natural experiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_experiment "Natural experiment"), the group studied the winners of the [Massachusetts state lottery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massachusetts_Lottery "Massachusetts Lottery") where the winners were paid incrementally over many years as opposed to a [lump-sum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lump_sum "Lump sum") payment. In doing so, the group was able to study the causal effects of guaranteed income. They found that winning the lottery had only a small impact on how much people worked. Winners of $80,000 a year for 20 years reduced their working hours somewhat, but winners of $15,000 a year for 20 years did not. Among unemployed persons who played the lottery, winners worked more than non-winners in the six years after playing.

Some of Imbens' work was summarized in a 2015 book co-written with American statistician [Donald B. Rubin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Rubin "Donald Rubin"), *Causal Inference for Statistics, Social, and Biomedical Sciences.*

Around 2016, he (along with his wife [Susan Athey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susan_Athey "Susan Athey")) worked on using [machine learning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machine_learning "Machine learning") methods, particularly modifications to [random forests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_forest "Random forest") called causal forests, to estimate heterogeneous treatment effects in causal inference models.

### Nobel Memorial Prize in Economics

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/60/Guido_Imbens_Lemley_Lecture_2.jpg/250px-Guido_Imbens_Lemley_Lecture_2.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Guido_Imbens_Lemley_Lecture_2.jpg)

Imbens speaking at [Brown University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brown_University "Brown University") in March 2022

Imbens received the 2021 [Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Memorial_Prize_in_Economic_Sciences "Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences") along with fellow economists [David Card](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Card "David Card") and Joshua Angrist for their contributions toward methodologies for the analysis of [causal relationships](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Causal_relation "Causal relation"). In its press release, the [Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Swedish_Academy_of_Sciences "Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences") stated that they "have provided us with new insights about the labour market and shown what conclusions about cause and effect can be drawn from natural experiments. Their approach has spread to other fields and revolutionised [empirical research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empirical_research "Empirical research")."

## Personal life

Imbens has been married to fellow economist [Susan Athey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susan_Athey "Susan Athey") since 2002. Athey likewise teaches at the Stanford Graduate School of Business where she holds the Economics of Technology Professorship. The best man at Imbens and Athey's wedding was [Joshua Angrist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joshua_Angrist "Joshua Angrist"), with whom Imbens would share the Nobel prize 19 years later.

He holds [dual citizenship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiple_citizenship "Multiple citizenship") in the United States and the Netherlands.
