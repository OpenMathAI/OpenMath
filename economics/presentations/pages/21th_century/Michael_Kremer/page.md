**Michael Robert Kremer** (born November 12, 1964) is an American [development economist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Development_economics "Development economics") currently serving as university professor in economics at the [University of Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Chicago "University of Chicago") and director of the Development Innovation Lab at the [Becker Friedman Institute for Research in Economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Becker_Friedman_Institute_for_Research_in_Economics "Becker Friedman Institute for Research in Economics"). Kremer formerly served as the Gates Professor of Developing Societies at [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University "Harvard University"), a role he held from 2003 to 2020. In 2019, Kremer was jointly awarded the [Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Memorial_Prize_in_Economic_Sciences "Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences"), together with [Esther Duflo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Esther_Duflo "Esther Duflo") and [Abhijit Banerjee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abhijit_Banerjee "Abhijit Banerjee"), "for their experimental approach to [alleviating global poverty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poverty_reduction "Poverty reduction")."

In addition to his academic appointments, Kremer is the co-founder of the [Bureau for Research and Economic Analysis of Development (BREAD)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bureau_for_Research_and_Economic_Analysis_of_Development "Bureau for Research and Economic Analysis of Development"), a member of the [National Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Academy_of_Sciences "National Academy of Sciences"), and a Research Associate at the [National Bureau of Economic Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Bureau_of_Economic_Research "National Bureau of Economic Research"). In 2008, he was elected a Fellow of the [Econometric Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Econometric_Society "Econometric Society"). Kremer is also the Scientific Director of Development Innovation Ventures, a program of the [United States Agency for International Development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Agency_for_International_Development "United States Agency for International Development") aimed at maximizing the impact of development spending through rigorous impact evaluation.

## Early life and education

Michael Robert Kremer was born on November 12, 1964 to Eugene and Sara Lillian (née Kimmel) Kremer in [New York City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_City "New York City"). Both his mother and father were the children of Jewish immigrants, from [Poland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poland "Poland") and Austria-Poland, respectively. His father taught [architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architecture "Architecture") at [Kansas State University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kansas_State_University "Kansas State University"), and his mother was a professor of [English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_literature "English literature") at the same institution, where she specialized in American Jewish and Holocaust literature. Kremer attended [Manhattan High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manhattan_High_School "Manhattan High School") in [Manhattan, Kansas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manhattan,_Kansas "Manhattan, Kansas"), where his mother was formerly a teacher. Beginning in fifth grade, he took classes at [Kansas State University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kansas_State_University "Kansas State University"), taking enough credits to achieve sophomore standing by his junior year of high school.

He left high school a year early to attend [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University "Harvard University"), where he received his [AB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_Arts "Bachelor of Arts") in [Social Studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_studies "Social studies") in 1985, [magna cum laude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magna_cum_laude "Magna cum laude") and [Phi Beta Kappa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phi_Beta_Kappa "Phi Beta Kappa"). Kremer wrote his senior thesis comparing an employment guarantee program in [India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/India "India") with a food subsidy scheme in [Sri Lanka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sri_Lanka "Sri Lanka").

After graduating from [Harvard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University "Harvard University"), Kremer worked for a year as a teacher and administrator at Eshisiru Secondary School in [Kakamega District](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kakamega_District "Kakamega District") of [Kenya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenya "Kenya"). Inspired by the experience, he co-founded [WorldTeach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WorldTeach "WorldTeach"), a [Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge,_Massachusetts "Cambridge, Massachusetts")-based [nonprofit organization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonprofit_organization "Nonprofit organization") focused on international development and education, where he currently serves as President of the Board. After his time in [Kenya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenya "Kenya"), Kremer returned to [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University "Harvard University"), where he received a [PhD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PhD "PhD") in Economics in 1992. Kremer's dissertation research was supervised by [Robert Barro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Barro "Robert Barro"), [Eric Maskin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eric_Maskin "Eric Maskin"), and [Greg Mankiw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greg_Mankiw "Greg Mankiw"), and focused on the determinants of long-run economic growth. His PhD research was supported by a fellowship from the [National Science Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Science_Foundation "National Science Foundation"), and won the David A. Wells Prize, awarded annually to the best dissertation in the [Harvard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University "Harvard University") Department of Economics.

Kremer is the husband of [Rachel Glennerster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rachel_Glennerster "Rachel Glennerster"), president of the [Center for Global Development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_for_Global_Development "Center for Global Development"), an associate professor of economics at the [University of Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Chicago "University of Chicago"), and the former Chief Economist of the UK [Department for International Development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Department_for_International_Development "Department for International Development").

## Career

In September 2026, the [World Bank Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Bank_Group "World Bank Group") appointed Kremer as its chief economist and senior vice president for development economics, effective October 1, 2026. In that role, he is to lead the institution's research, data, and analytical agenda.

Kremer began his academic career at the [Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massachusetts_Institute_of_Technology "Massachusetts Institute of Technology"), where he served as a postdoctoral fellow from 1992 to 1993. He was a visiting assistant professor at the [University of Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Chicago "University of Chicago") in spring 1993, and joined [MIT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MIT "MIT") as an assistant professor the same year. He was promoted to associate professor in 1996, and again to professor of economics in 1998. In 1999, he joined [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University "Harvard University"), where he served as Gates Professor of Developing Societies from 2003 to 2020. After his time at [Harvard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University "Harvard University"), Kremer again joined the [University of Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Chicago "University of Chicago"), where he is a university professor in economics and director of the Development Innovation Lab at the [Becker Friedman Institute for Research in Economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Becker_Friedman_Institute_for_Research_in_Economics "Becker Friedman Institute for Research in Economics").

In addition to his academic appointments, Kremer is a fellow of the [Econometric Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Econometric_Society "Econometric Society"), a member of the [National Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Academy_of_Sciences "National Academy of Sciences"), and a fellow of the [American Academy of Arts and Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Academy_of_Arts_and_Sciences "American Academy of Arts and Sciences"). In 2002, he co-founded the [Bureau for Research and Economic Analysis of Development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bureau_for_Research_and_Economic_Analysis_of_Development "Bureau for Research and Economic Analysis of Development"), a non-profit learned society promoting research on the [microeconomics of development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Development_economics "Development economics"). Since 1999, Kremer has also been a research associate at the [National Bureau of Economic Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Bureau_of_Economic_Research "National Bureau of Economic Research").

[Abhijit Banerjee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abhijit_Banerjee "Abhijit Banerjee"), joint winner of the 2019 [Nobel Memorial Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Memorial_Prize_in_Economic_Sciences "Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences")

According to [Research Papers in Economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Research_Papers_in_Economics "Research Papers in Economics"), Kremer is among the most cited economists in the world, ranking in the top 130 authors by total research output as of November 2023. He has focused his research on [economic growth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_growth "Economic growth") and poverty alleviation, particularly as it relates to education and health. Alongside [Abhijit Banerjee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abhijit_Banerjee "Abhijit Banerjee") and [Esther Duflo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Esther_Duflo "Esther Duflo"), Kremer has helped establish the effectiveness of [randomized controlled trials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Randomized_controlled_trials "Randomized controlled trials") to test proposed antipoverty measures. Describing Kremer's early use of pioneering experimental methods, Duflo said that Kremer "was there from the very beginning, and took enormous risks. [...] He is a visionary."

Inspired by a [randomized evaluation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Randomized_controlled_trial "Randomized controlled trial") co-authored with [Edward Miguel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Miguel "Edward Miguel") showing substantial effects of school-based deworming programs on educational attainment and health, Kremer co-founded the [Deworm the World Initiative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deworm_the_World_Initiative "Deworm the World Initiative"), which has since 2014 delivered 1.8 billion treatments to children around the world. The scheme, run by non-profit [Evidence Action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evidence_Action "Evidence Action"), was ranked a [GiveWell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GiveWell "GiveWell") top charity from 2013 to 2022, considered among the best in the world for social impact per marginal dollar spent. Since 2009, Kremer has been a member of [Giving What We Can](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giving_What_We_Can "Giving What We Can"), an [effective altruism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effective_altruism "Effective altruism") organization whose members pledge to give 10% or more of their lifetime income to effective charities.

In 2010, Kremer co-founded Development Innovation Ventures, an evidence based innovation fund administered by [USAID](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USAID "USAID") aimed at testing and scaling new approaches to poverty alleviation. The program promotes a [venture capital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venture_capital "Venture capital") approach to development finance, prioritizing rapid distribution of grants and scale-up of policies successful in pilot stages. Since its founding, DIV has funded 277 grants in 49 countries, generating an estimated $17 in social benefit for each dollar invested in the program.

From 2011 to 2014, Kremer co-led an experimental evaluation of an [SMS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SMS "SMS")-based information service in which [Kenyan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenya "Kenya") smallholder farmers were texted agricultural advice. The program increased yields by 8%, inspiring Kremer to co-found [Precision Development (PxD)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precision_Development_(PxD)?action=edit&redlink=1 "Precision Development (PxD)"), a non-profit organization building digital information services for the world's poor. In 2020, PxD's programs had 5.7 million users, at an average cost per user of $1.61.

Kremer has also pursued research on vaccine development and financing. He developed an interest in the subject after contracting malaria while traveling in Kenya. In a 2004 book with his wife, [Rachel Glennerster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rachel_Glennerster "Rachel Glennerster"), Kremer advocates for the creation of [advance market commitments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advance_market_commitment "Advance market commitment"), in which governments enter into legally binding agreements with pharmaceutical or biotechnology companies, promising to purchase given quantities of vaccines or medications provided they meet certain benchmarks for safety and efficacy. In response to Kremer's research, a consortium of donors including the [Bill & Melinda Gates Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bill_&_Melinda_Gates_Foundation "Bill & Melinda Gates Foundation") and national governments such as Canada, Italy, and Norway pledged $1.5 billion as an [advance market commitment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advance_market_commitment "Advance market commitment") for [pneumococcal vaccines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pneumococcal_vaccine "Pneumococcal vaccine"). The [AMC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advance_market_commitment "Advance market commitment") has inspired the creation of three different vaccinations, which have collectively immunized 150 million children.

Kremer's research was also widely cited during the [COVID-19 pandemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic "COVID-19 pandemic") as a model for how to quickly develop vaccines through public-private partnership. In response to requests for consultation by several governments, Kremer founded the Accelerating Health Technologies Group, a consortium of academics including Glennerster, [Susan Athey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susan_Athey "Susan Athey"), and [Jonathan Levin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonathan_Levin_(economist) "Jonathan Levin (economist)") aimed at accelerating the wide-scale distribution of effective vaccines. The group advised the [US government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_states_government "United states government") on [Operation Warp Speed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Warp_Speed "Operation Warp Speed"), a public-private partnership announced in 2020 that funded successful COVID-19 vaccines such as those developed by [Pfizer–BioNTech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pfizer–BioNTech_COVID-19_vaccine "Pfizer–BioNTech COVID-19 vaccine") and [Moderna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moderna_COVID-19_vaccine "Moderna COVID-19 vaccine"). [Chris Blattman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chris_Blattman "Chris Blattman"), a professor at the [Harris School of Public Policy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harris_School_of_Public_Policy "Harris School of Public Policy"), has described Kremer as one of the world's "leading thinkers on incentives for creating new vaccine research."

## Research

Kremer's research focuses on [economic growth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_growth "Economic growth") and poverty alleviation, particularly as it relates to [health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_economics "Health economics") and [education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education_economics "Education economics"). Along with [Abhijit Banerjee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abhijit_Banerjee "Abhijit Banerjee") and [Esther Duflo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Esther_Duflo "Esther Duflo"), he is a leading proponent of the use of [randomized controlled trials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Randomized_controlled_trial "Randomized controlled trial") in [development economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Development_economics "Development economics"), a contribution recognized by the 2019 [Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Memorial_Prize_in_Economic_Sciences "Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences").

### O-ring theory of economic development

Kremer's dissertation research examined the determinants of long-run economic growth. Among his earliest contributions was the [O-ring theory of economic development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/O-ring_theory_of_economic_development "O-ring theory of economic development"), named for the [Space Shuttle Challenger disaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Shuttle_Challenger_disaster "Space Shuttle Challenger disaster"), in which a cascading failure was caused by the malfunctioning of a single small component. Kremer's insight was that complex products (like the space shuttle) often require completing many steps correctly for the final product to have any value. Kremer suggested this would make high-skilled workers [complements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complementary_goods "Complementary goods") to each other, meaning wages for high-skilled workers will be higher in an economic environment where other workers are also highly skilled. The [O-ring theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/O-ring_theory_of_economic_development "O-ring theory of economic development") has been recognized as a leading explanation for [human capital flight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_capital_flight "Human capital flight") and cross-country wage inequality. The framework has also been extended by other scholars, who have incorporated into the model "non O-ring" sectors without skill complementarities.

Kremer's work on the [O-ring theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/O-ring_theory_of_economic_development "O-ring theory of economic development") was inspired by his time in [Kenya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenya "Kenya"), when he organized a training session for [WorldTeach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WorldTeach "WorldTeach") volunteers, but forgot to purchase toilet paper for the event. The experience demonstrated how even small failures can derail a larger enterprise, such that production processes often require many complementary high-skilled workers to succeed.

### Population growth

During his graduate studies, Kremer also pursued research on the link between population growth and technological change. In a paper in [The Quarterly Journal of Economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Quarterly_Journal_of_Economics "The Quarterly Journal of Economics"), Kremer empirically tests the hypotheses of macroeconomic models predicting a positive relationship between population levels and technological innovation because of the [non-rivalry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonrivalrousness "Nonrivalrousness") of ideas. He shows that among societies with no technological contact and similar levels of productivity, those with higher populations saw faster improvements in technology.

### Species endangerment

In other theoretical work, Kremer studies the optimal policy responses to species endangerment. In a paper with Charles Morcom in the [American Economic Review](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Economic_Review "American Economic Review"), Kremer argues that because the price of ivory is positively associated with poachers’ incentives to kill endangered elephants, governments should accumulate stockpiles of ivory, releasing them onto the open market when elephant populations reach dangerous levels. He argues that doing so can counteract increased incentives to poach, thereby preventing species endangerment.

The policy value of Kremer and Morcom's work was criticized by Erwin H. Bulte and co-authors, who argue in a reply in the [American Economic Review](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Economic_Review "American Economic Review") that government ownership of ivory may incentivize the preemptive extermination of endangered species, thereby allowing stores to be legally sold under [CITES](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CITES "CITES"). They propose instead that stores of ivory are managed by intergovernmental conservation organizations who do not face the same incentives as governments to maximize revenues in the short-term.

### Deworming

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/38/USAID_Delivers_Deworming_Medication_to_Kindergartners_in_Nam_Dinh_Province_%288920938742%29.jpg/250px-USAID_Delivers_Deworming_Medication_to_Kindergartners_in_Nam_Dinh_Province_%288920938742%29.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:USAID_Delivers_Deworming_Medication_to_Kindergartners_in_Nam_Dinh_Province_(8920938742).jpg)

[USAID](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USAID "USAID") delivers deworming medication in [Vietnam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnam "Vietnam"), 2013

Among Kremer's most recognized work examines the effects of [deworming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deworming "Deworming") treatments on the health and educational outcomes of children. After completing his [PhD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PhD "PhD"), Kremer traveled with [Rachel Glennerster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rachel_Glennerster "Rachel Glennerster") to [Kenya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenya "Kenya") on vacation, where he met a friend from his time as a teacher and administrator. The friend was planning on rolling out a new scheme to help elementary school children that included the distribution of deworming medication. Kremer recommended that the scheme be implemented in a random order, such that the effects of treatment could be evaluated rigorously, as in a [randomized clinical trial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clinical_trial "Clinical trial"). Treatments were rolled out in 1998, and the results of the study were eventually published in [Econometrica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Econometrica "Econometrica") by Kremer and [Edward Miguel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Miguel "Edward Miguel"), his [PhD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PhD "PhD") student, in 2004. The experiment found large effects of [deworming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deworming "Deworming") on health and education outcomes, with a reduction in school absenteeism of 25%. The program's success sparked the [Deworm the World Initiative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deworm_the_World_Initiative "Deworm the World Initiative"), which has since 2014 delivered 1.8 billion [deworming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deworming "Deworming") treatments to children around the world.

In 2021, Kremer and Miguel published a long-term follow up of the initial [deworming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deworming "Deworming") program in the [Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proceedings_of_the_National_Academy_of_Sciences_of_the_United_States_of_America "Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences of the United States of America"), showing that those who received an extra two to three years of deworming treatment as a child have consumer spending that is 14% higher than their counterparts ten years later. They also find that [deworming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deworming "Deworming") increases the likelihood of living in an urban area, and decreases the likelihood of working in agriculture (a sector with generally lower wages and less capacity for growth).

### Public sector absenteeism

Another strand of Kremer's work concerns public sector absenteeism in the developing world, particularly in public education and health systems. In work with [Karthik Muralidharan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karthik_Muralidharan "Karthik Muralidharan"), [Jeffrey Hammer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeffrey_Hammer "Jeffrey Hammer"), Halsey Rogers, and Nazmul Chaudhury, Kremer shows via unannounced visits to Indian public schools that teacher absenteeism sits at 25%, varying from 15% in [Maharashtra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maharashtra "Maharashtra") to 42% in [Jharkhand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jharkhand "Jharkhand"). Subsequent research has estimated the salary cost of this absenteeism at $1.5 billion per year in India.

In wider work covering [Bangladesh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bangladesh "Bangladesh"), [Ecuador](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecuador "Ecuador"), [India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/India "India"), [Indonesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indonesia "Indonesia"), [Peru](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peru "Peru"), and [Uganda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uganda "Uganda"), Kremer and co-authors show that health worker absenteeism averages 35% across countries. Their work has inspired randomized evaluations of several novel policy solutions to public sector absenteeism, such as camera and mobile phone based monitoring schemes.

Logo of [Operation Warp Speed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Warp_Speed "Operation Warp Speed"), a public-private partnership inspired by Kremer's work on [AMCs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advance_market_commitment "Advance market commitment")

### Advance market commitments

Kremer has also pursued research on innovation in the pharmaceutical industry. He and Rachel Glennerster have been leading proponents of [advance market commitments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advance_market_commitment "Advance market commitment"), legally binding agreements in which governments agree to purchase a given quantity of a vaccine or other item at a profitable price, provided it meets certain standards for efficacy and safety. Kremer promoted the idea in a 2004 book, in addition to a widely cited 2003 report published by the [Center for Global Development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_for_Global_Development "Center for Global Development").

In a 2020 working paper, Kremer, [Jonathan Levin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonathan_Levin_(economist) "Jonathan Levin (economist)"), and Christopher Tucker review the economic case for [AMCs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advance_market_commitment "Advance market commitment"), showing theoretically that they can effectively prevent the [hold-up problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hold-up_problem "Hold-up problem") that emerges when pharmaceutical firms cannot bargain on prices until after vaccines or other drugs are developed. [Advance market commitments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advance_market_commitment "Advance market commitment") have been suggested as a means of spurring investment across a wide variety of industries, including pharmaceuticals, carbon removal, and renewable energy.

## Recognition

Kremer is among the most cited [development economists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Development_economics "Development economics"), ranking in the top 130 economists in the world by total research output according to [Research Papers in Economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Research_Papers_in_Economics "Research Papers in Economics"). The [Open Syllabus Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_Syllabus_Project "Open Syllabus Project") ranks him as the 28th most cited author on the syllabi of university economics courses. In 2006, he received a [Scientific American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_American "Scientific American") 50 Award, awarded to influencers from research, business, and politics with "an interest in leading technological innovation as a force for the public good."

### Nobel Memorial Prize in Economics

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/68/Nobel_9_Dec_2019_046_copy_%2849203837661%29.jpg/250px-Nobel_9_Dec_2019_046_copy_%2849203837661%29.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Nobel_9_Dec_2019_046_copy_(49203837661).jpg)

Kremer receiving the [Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Memorial_Prize_in_Economic_Sciences "Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences")

In 2019, Kremer was chosen as the co-winner of the [Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Memorial_Prize_in_Economic_Sciences "Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences"), along with [Esther Duflo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Esther_Duflo "Esther Duflo") and [Abhijit Banerjee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abhijit_Banerjee "Abhijit Banerjee") of [MIT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MIT "MIT") "for their experimental approach to alleviating global poverty." Speaking of Kremer, Duflo, and Banerjee's work popularizing the use of [randomized controlled trials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Randomized_controlled_trial "Randomized controlled trial") in development economics, the Nobel committee noted that the "[t]heir experimental research methods now entirely dominate development economics." In a tribute to Kremer, [Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Berkeley "University of California, Berkeley") professor [Edward Miguel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Miguel "Edward Miguel") notes that "Michael’s pioneering work also accelerated innovation in original data collection in development and made it normal and even downright necessary for development economists to spend extended time in the societies that they study." [Amartya Sen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amartya_Sen "Amartya Sen"), a [Harvard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University "Harvard University") based economist and winner of the 1998 [Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Memorial_Prize_in_Economic_Sciences "Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences"), stressed that Kremer "has made an outstanding contribution in combining economic theory and sophisticated empirical techniques and applying it to critical policy issues in development economics."

Kremer's [Nobel lecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_lecture "Nobel lecture"), entitled "Experimentation, Innovation, and Economics" provides an overview of the experimental revolution in [development economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Development_economics "Development economics"), arguing that [RCTs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Randomized_controlled_trial "Randomized controlled trial") can be a powerful tool for influencing public policy and promoting innovation. To do this, he draws on many examples of his own work, including that on [deworming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deworming "Deworming") and fertilizer usage.

Kremer, Duflo, and Banerjee donated the winnings associated with their prize to the Weiss Fund for Research in Development Economics, a [University of Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Chicago "University of Chicago") based grant-making body supporting frontier research in development economics. Writing of their decision to do so, Kremer noted that "Abhijit, Esther and I truly believe that our Nobel Prize is an award for the development economics community, and we wanted to invest it in a way that provides new opportunities for research."
