**Thomas John Sargent** (born July 19, 1943) is an American [economist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economist "Economist") and a Rowan Distinguished Professor of Finance at the [Wharton School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wharton_School "Wharton School") of the [University of Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Pennsylvania "University of Pennsylvania"). He specializes in the fields of [macroeconomics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macroeconomics "Macroeconomics"), [monetary economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monetary_economics "Monetary economics"), and [time series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_series "Time series") [econometrics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Econometrics "Econometrics"). He was awarded the [Nobel Memorial Prize in Economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Memorial_Prize_in_Economic_Sciences "Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences") in 2011 together with [Christopher A. Sims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_A._Sims "Christopher A. Sims") for their "empirical research on cause and effect in the macroeconomy".

Sargent previously held a joint appointment at [New York University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_University "New York University"), where he joined the university in 2002 as the first W. R. Berkley Professor of Economics and Business. He has also held teaching positions at the [University of Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Pennsylvania "University of Pennsylvania") (1970–71), [University of Minnesota](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Minnesota "University of Minnesota") (1971–87), [University of Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Chicago "University of Chicago") (1991–98), and [Stanford University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford_University "Stanford University") (1998–2002), as well as a visiting position at [Princeton University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University "Princeton University").

## Education

Sargent graduated from [Monrovia High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monrovia_High_School "Monrovia High School"). He earned his B.A. from the [University of California, Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Berkeley "University of California, Berkeley") in 1964, being the University Medalist as Most Distinguished Scholar in Class of 1964, and his PhD from [Harvard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University "Harvard University") in 1968, under supervision of [John R. Meyer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_R._Meyer "John R. Meyer"). Sargent's classmates at Harvard included [Christopher A. Sims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_A._Sims "Christopher A. Sims"). After serving in the U.S. Army as a first lieutenant and captain, he moved on to teaching. He held teaching positions at the [University of Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Pennsylvania "University of Pennsylvania") (1970–71), [University of Minnesota](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Minnesota "University of Minnesota") (1971–87), [University of Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Chicago "University of Chicago") (1991–98), [Stanford University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford_University "Stanford University") (1998–2002) and [Princeton University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University "Princeton University") (2009), and is currently a professor of economics at [New York University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_University "New York University") (since 2002). He previously held the position of President of the [American Economic Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Economic_Association "American Economic Association") and the [Econometric Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Econometric_Society "Econometric Society") where he has been a fellow since 1976. In 1983, Sargent was elected to the National Academy of Sciences and also the [American Academy of Arts and Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Academy_of_Arts_and_Sciences "American Academy of Arts and Sciences"). He has been a senior fellow of the [Hoover Institution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoover_Institution "Hoover Institution") at [Stanford University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford_University "Stanford University") since the year 1987.

## Professional contributions

Sargent is one of the leaders of the "[rational expectations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_expectations "Rational expectations") revolution," which argues that the people being modeled by economists can predict the future, or the probability of future outcomes, at least as well as the economist can with his model. Rational expectations was introduced into economics by [John Muth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Muth "John Muth"), then [Robert Lucas, Jr.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Lucas,_Jr. "Robert Lucas, Jr."), and [Edward C. Prescott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_C._Prescott "Edward C. Prescott") took it much farther. In work written in close collaboration with Lucas and [Neil Wallace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neil_Wallace "Neil Wallace"), Thomas J. Sargent contributed fundamentally to the evolution of [new classical macroeconomics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_classical_macroeconomics "New classical macroeconomics").

Sargent's main contributions to rational expectations were these:

- trace the implications of rational expectations, with Wallace, for alternative monetary-policy instruments and rules on output stability and price determinacy.
- help make the theory of rational expectations statistically operational.
- provide some early examples of rational expectations models of the [Phillips curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phillips_curve "Phillips curve"), the [term structure of interest rates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yield_curve "Yield curve"), and the [demand for money](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demand_for_money "Demand for money") during [hyperinflations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperinflation "Hyperinflation").
- analyze, along with Wallace, the dimensions along which [monetary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monetary_policy "Monetary policy") and [fiscal policy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiscal_policy "Fiscal policy") must be coordinated [intertemporally](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intertemporal_choice "Intertemporal choice").
- conduct several [historical studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_history "Economic history") that put rational expectations reasoning to work to explain consequences of dramatic changes in macroeconomic policy regimes.

In 1975 he and Wallace proposed the [policy-ineffectiveness proposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Policy-ineffectiveness_proposition "Policy-ineffectiveness proposition"), which challenged a basic assumption of Keynesian economics.

Sargent went on to refine or extend rational expectations reasoning by further:

- studying the conditions under which systems with [bounded rationality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bounded_rationality "Bounded rationality") of agents and [adaptive learners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adaptive_learning "Adaptive learning") converge to rational expectations.
- using the notion of a [self-confirming equilibrium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-confirming_equilibrium "Self-confirming equilibrium"), a weaker notion of rational expectations suggested by limits of learning models.
- studying contexts with [Lars Peter Hansen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lars_Peter_Hansen "Lars Peter Hansen") in which decision makers do not trust their probability model. In particular, Hansen and Sargent adapt and extend methods from [robust control theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robust_control "Robust control").

Sargent has also been a pioneer in introducing [recursive economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recursive_economics "Recursive economics") to academic study, especially for macroeconomic issues such as unemployment, fiscal and monetary policy, and growth. His series of textbooks, co-authored with [Lars Ljungqvist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lars_Ljungqvist "Lars Ljungqvist"), are seminal in the contemporary graduate economics curriculum.

Sargent has pursued a research program with Ljungqvist designed to understand determinants of differences in [unemployment outcomes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unemployment "Unemployment") in Europe and the United States during the last 30 years. The two key questions the program addresses are why, in the 1950s and 1960s, unemployment was systematically lower in Europe than in the United States and why, for two and a half decades after 1980,
unemployment has been systematically higher in Europe than in the United States. In "Two Questions about European Unemployment," the answer is that "Europe has stronger employment protection despite also having had more generous government supplied unemployment compensation"." While the institutional differences remained the same over this time period, the microeconomic environment for workers changed, with a higher risk of [human capital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_capital "Human capital") depreciation in the 1980s.

In 1997, he won the [Nemmers Prize in Economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nemmers_Prize_in_Economics "Nemmers Prize in Economics")

In 2011, he was awarded the [NAS Award for Scientific Reviewing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NAS_Award_for_Scientific_Reviewing "NAS Award for Scientific Reviewing") from the [National Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_National_Academy_of_Sciences "United States National Academy of Sciences") and, in September, he became the recipient of the 2011 [CME Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CME_Group "CME Group")-[MSRI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_Sciences_Research_Institute "Mathematical Sciences Research Institute") Prize in Innovative Quantitative Applications.

Sargent's reading group at Stanford and NYU is a known institution among graduate students in economics.

In 2016, Sargent helped found the non-profit [QuantEcon](https://quantecon.org/) project, which is dedicated to the development and documentation of modern open source computational tools for economics, econometrics, and decision making.

Currently he is director of the Sargent Institute of Quantitative Economics and Finance (SIQEF) at Peking University HSBC Business School in Shenzhen.

## Nobel Prize

[![](//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c0/Nobel_Thomas_J._Sargent_2011-12-08.ogv/250px--Nobel_Thomas_J._Sargent_2011-12-08.ogv.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser)](//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/c/c0/Nobel_Thomas_J._Sargent_2011-12-08.ogv?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=index&utm_content=original)

Interview with Thomas J. Sargent after his Nobel lecture

On October 10, 2011, Sargent, with [Christopher A. Sims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_A._Sims "Christopher A. Sims"), was awarded the [Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Memorial_Prize_in_Economic_Sciences "Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences"). The award cited their "empirical research on cause and effect in the macroeconomy". His Nobel lecture, "United States Then, Europe Now," was delivered on December 11, 2011.

## In popular culture

He is featured playing himself in a television commercial for [Ally Financial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ally_Financial "Ally Financial") in which he is asked if he can predict CD rates two years from now, to which he simply answers, "No."

Sargent is notable for making short speeches. For example, in 2007 his Berkeley graduation speech consumed 335 words.
