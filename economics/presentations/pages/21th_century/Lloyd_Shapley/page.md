**Lloyd Stowell Shapley** ([/ˈʃæpli/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English"); June 2, 1923 – March 12, 2016) was an American [mathematician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematician "Mathematician") and [Nobel Memorial Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Memorial_Prize "Nobel Memorial Prize")-winning [economist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economist "Economist"). He contributed to the fields of [mathematical economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_economics "Mathematical economics") and especially [game theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game_theory "Game theory"). Shapley is generally considered one of the most important contributors to the development of game theory since the work of [von Neumann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_von_Neumann "John von Neumann") and [Morgenstern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oskar_Morgenstern "Oskar Morgenstern"). With [Alvin E. Roth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alvin_E._Roth "Alvin E. Roth"), Shapley won the 2012 [Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Memorial_Prize_in_Economic_Sciences "Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences") "for the theory of stable allocations and the practice of [market design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Market_design "Market design")."

## Life and career

Lloyd Shapley was born on June 2, 1923, in [Cambridge, Massachusetts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge,_Massachusetts "Cambridge, Massachusetts"), one of the sons of astronomers [Harlow Shapley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harlow_Shapley "Harlow Shapley") and [Martha Betz Shapley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martha_Betz_Shapley "Martha Betz Shapley"), both from Missouri. He attended [Phillips Exeter Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phillips_Exeter_Academy "Phillips Exeter Academy") and was a student at Harvard when he was drafted in 1943. He served in the [United States Army Air Corps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Army_Air_Corps "United States Army Air Corps") in [Chengdu, China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chengdu,_China "Chengdu, China") and received the [Bronze Star](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronze_Star_Medal "Bronze Star Medal") decoration for breaking the Soviet weather code.

After the war, Shapley returned to Harvard and graduated with an [A.B.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_Arts "Bachelor of Arts") in mathematics in 1948. After working for one year at the [RAND Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RAND_Corporation "RAND Corporation"), he went to [Princeton University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University "Princeton University") where he received a Ph.D. in 1953 based on the thesis "Additive and non-additive set functions". His thesis and post-doctoral work introduced the [Shapley value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shapley_value "Shapley value") and the [core](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Core_(game_theory) "Core (game theory)") solution in [game theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game_theory "Game theory"). Shapley defined game theory as "a mathematical study of conflict and cooperation." After graduating, he remained at Princeton for a short time before going back to the RAND corporation from 1954 to 1981. In 1950, while a graduate student, Shapley invented the board game *[So Long Sucker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/So_Long_Sucker "So Long Sucker")*, along with [Mel Hausner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mel_Hausner?action=edit&redlink=1 "Mel Hausner"), [John Forbes Nash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Forbes_Nash "John Forbes Nash"), and [Martin Shubik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Shubik "Martin Shubik"). Israeli economist and Nobel Laureate [Robert Aumann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Aumann "Robert Aumann") considered Shapley to be "the greatest game theorist of all time."

From 1981 until his death, Shapley was a professor at the [University of California, Los Angeles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Los_Angeles "University of California, Los Angeles") (UCLA), serving at the time of his death as a professor emeritus there, affiliated with the Mathematics and Economics departments. He died on March 12, 2016, in [Tucson, Arizona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tucson,_Arizona "Tucson, Arizona"), after suffering from a [broken hip](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broken_hip "Broken hip"), at the age of 92.

Shapley was an expert [Kriegspiel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kriegspiel_(chess) "Kriegspiel (chess)") player, and an avid baseball fan.

## Contribution

Along with the [Shapley value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shapley_value "Shapley value"), [stochastic games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stochastic_games "Stochastic games"), the [Bondareva–Shapley theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bondareva–Shapley_theorem "Bondareva–Shapley theorem") (which implies that [convex games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convex_games "Convex games") have non-empty cores), the [Shapley–Shubik power index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shapley–Shubik_power_index "Shapley–Shubik power index") (for [weighted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weighted_voting "Weighted voting") or block voting power), the [Gale–Shapley algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gale–Shapley_algorithm "Gale–Shapley algorithm") for the [stable marriage problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stable_marriage_problem "Stable marriage problem"), the concept of a [potential game](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potential_game "Potential game") (with [Dov Monderer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dov_Monderer?action=edit&redlink=1 "Dov Monderer")), the [Aumann–Shapley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aumann–Shapley_value "Aumann–Shapley value") pricing, the [Harsanyi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Harsanyi "John Harsanyi")–Shapley solution, the [Snow–Shapley theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snow–Shapley_theorem?action=edit&redlink=1 "Snow–Shapley theorem") for matrix games, and the [Shapley–Folkman lemma & theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shapley–Folkman_lemma "Shapley–Folkman lemma") bear his name. According to *[The Economist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Economist "The Economist")*, Shapley "may have thought of himself as a mathematician, but he cannot avoid being remembered for his huge contributions to economics". The [American Economic Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Economic_Association "American Economic Association") noted that Shapley was "one of the giants of game theory and economic theory".

Besides, his early work with R. N. Snow and [Samuel Karlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Karlin "Samuel Karlin") on [matrix games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_game "Matrix game") was so complete that little has been added since. He has been instrumental in the development of [utility theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utility_theory "Utility theory"), and it was he who laid much of the groundwork for the solution of the problem of the existence of [Von Neumann–Morgenstern stable sets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Von_Neumann-Morgenstern_solution "Von Neumann-Morgenstern solution"). His work with [M. Maschler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Maschler "Michael Maschler") and B. Peleg on the kernel and the nucleolus, and his work with [Robert Aumann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Aumann "Robert Aumann") on non-atomic games and on long-term competition have all appeared in economic theory.

Shapley argued with his sons about whether he should accept the Nobel Prize at all. He opined that his father, the astronomer [Harlow Shapley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harlow_Shapley "Harlow Shapley"), deserved it more. His sons persuaded him to accept it and accompanied him to [Stockholm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stockholm,_Sweden "Stockholm, Sweden").
