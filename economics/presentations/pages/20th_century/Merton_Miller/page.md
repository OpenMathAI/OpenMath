**Merton Howard Miller** (May 16, 1923 – June 3, 2000) was an American economist, and the co-author of the [Modigliani–Miller theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modigliani–Miller_theorem "Modigliani–Miller theorem") (1958), which proposed the irrelevance of debt-equity structure. He shared the [Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Memorial_Prize_in_Economic_Sciences "Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences") in 1990, along with [Harry Markowitz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harry_Markowitz "Harry Markowitz") and [William F. Sharpe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_F._Sharpe "William F. Sharpe"). Miller spent most of his academic career at the [University of Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Chicago "University of Chicago")'s [Booth School of Business](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Booth_School_of_Business "Booth School of Business").

## Biography

### Early years

Miller was born in [Boston, Massachusetts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boston,_Massachusetts "Boston, Massachusetts") to Jewish parents Sylvia and Joel Miller, a housewife and attorney. He attended Harvard University as an undergraduate student. He worked during [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II") as an economist in the division of tax research of the Treasury Department, and received a [Ph.D.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Philosophy "Doctor of Philosophy") in economics from [Johns Hopkins University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johns_Hopkins_University "Johns Hopkins University"), 1952. His first academic appointment after receiving his doctorate was Visiting Assistant Lecturer at the [London School of Economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_School_of_Economics "London School of Economics").

### Career

In 1958, at [Carnegie Institute of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carnegie_Institute_of_Technology "Carnegie Institute of Technology") (now [Carnegie Mellon University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carnegie_Mellon_University "Carnegie Mellon University")), he collaborated with his colleague [Franco Modigliani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franco_Modigliani "Franco Modigliani") on the paper *The Cost of Capital, Corporate Finance and the Theory of Investment*. This paper urged a fundamental objection to the traditional view of [corporate finance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corporate_finance "Corporate finance"), according to which a corporation can reduce its [cost of capital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cost_of_capital "Cost of capital") by finding the right [debt-to-equity ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Debt-to-equity_ratio "Debt-to-equity ratio"). According to the [Modigliani–Miller theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modigliani–Miller_theorem "Modigliani–Miller theorem"), on the other hand, there is no right ratio, so corporate managers should seek to minimize tax liability and maximize corporate net wealth, letting the debt ratio chips fall where they will.

The way in which they arrived at this conclusion made use of the "no [arbitrage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arbitrage "Arbitrage")" argument, i.e. the [premise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Premise "Premise") that any state of affairs that will allow traders of any market instrument to create a riskless money machine will almost immediately disappear. They set the pattern for many arguments based on that premise in subsequent years.

Miller wrote or co-authored eight books. He became a fellow of the [Econometric Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Econometric_Society "Econometric Society") in 1975 and was president of the [American Finance Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Finance_Association "American Finance Association") in 1976. He was on the faculty of the [University of Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Chicago "University of Chicago")'s [Booth School of Business](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Booth_School_of_Business "Booth School of Business") from 1961 until his retirement in 1993, although he continued teaching at the school for several more years.

His works formed the basis of the "Modigliani-Miller Financial Theory".

He served as a public director on the [Chicago Board of Trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicago_Board_of_Trade "Chicago Board of Trade") 1983–85 and the [Chicago Mercantile Exchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicago_Mercantile_Exchange "Chicago Mercantile Exchange") from 1990 until his death in [Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicago "Chicago") on June 3, 2000. In 1993, Miller waded into the controversy surrounding $2 billion in trading losses by what was characterized as a rogue futures trader at a subsidiary of [Metallgesellschaft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metallgesellschaft "Metallgesellschaft"), arguing in the *Wall Street Journal* that management of the subsidiary was to blame for panicking and liquidating the position too early. In 1995, Miller was engaged by [Nasdaq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nasdaq "Nasdaq") to rebut allegations of price fixing.

### Personal life

Miller was married to Eleanor Miller, who died in 1969. He was survived by his second wife, Katherine Miller, and by three children from his first marriage: Pamela (1952), Margot (1955), and Louise (1958), and two grandsons.
