**Wassily Wassilyevich Leontief** ([Russian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_language "Russian language"): Васи́лий Васи́льевич Лео́нтьев; August 5, 1905 – February 5, 1999) was a Russian-American [economist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economist "Economist") known for his research on [input–output analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Input–output_model "Input–output model") and how changes in one [economic sector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_sector "Economic sector") may affect other sectors.

Leontief was awarded the [Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Memorial_Prize_in_Economic_Sciences "Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences") in 1973, and four of his doctoral students have also been awarded the prize ([Paul Samuelson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Samuelson "Paul Samuelson") 1970, [Robert Solow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Solow "Robert Solow") 1987, [Vernon L. Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vernon_L._Smith "Vernon L. Smith") 2002, [Thomas Schelling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Schelling "Thomas Schelling") 2005).

## Biography

### Early life

Wassily Leontief was born on August 5, 1905, in [Munich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Munich "Munich"), German Empire, the son of Wassily W. Leontief (professor of [Economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economics "Economics")) and Zlata (German spelling *Slata*; later Evgenia) Leontief (née Becker). Wassily Leontief Sr. belonged to a family of Russian [old-believer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Believers "Old Believers") merchants living in [St. Petersburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Petersburg "Saint Petersburg") since 1741. Evgenia (Genya) Becker belonged to a wealthy [Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish "Jewish") family from [Odessa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odessa "Odessa"). At 15 in 1921, Wassily Jr. entered [Petrograd State University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Petersburg_State_University "Saint Petersburg State University") in present-day [St. Petersburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Petersburg "Saint Petersburg"). He earned his Learned Economist degree (equivalent to [Master of Arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master_of_Arts "Master of Arts")) in 1925 at the age of 19.

### Opposition in USSR

Leontief sided with campaigners for academic autonomy, freedom of speech and in support of [Pitirim Sorokin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pitirim_Sorokin "Pitirim Sorokin"). As a consequence, he was detained several times by the [Cheka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cheka "Cheka"). In 1925, he was allowed to leave the USSR, mostly because the Cheka believed that he was mortally ill with a [sarcoma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarcoma "Sarcoma"), a diagnosis that later proved false. He continued his studies at the [Friedrich Wilhelm University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humboldt_University_of_Berlin "Humboldt University of Berlin") in Berlin and, in 1928, earned a Ph.D. degree in economics under the direction of [Werner Sombart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Werner_Sombart "Werner Sombart"), writing his dissertation on *The Economy as Circular Flow* (original German title: *Die Wirtschaft als Kreislauf*).

### Early professional life

From 1927 to 1930, he worked at the [Institute for the World Economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kiel_Institute_for_the_World_Economy "Kiel Institute for the World Economy") of the [University of Kiel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Kiel "University of Kiel"). There he researched the derivation of statistical demand and supply curves. In 1929, he traveled to China to assist its ministry of railroads as an advisor.

In 1931, he went to the United States and was employed by the [National Bureau of Economic Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Bureau_of_Economic_Research "National Bureau of Economic Research").

During World War II, Leontief served as consultant at the U. S. [Office of Strategic Services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Office_of_Strategic_Services "Office of Strategic Services").

### Harvard

Leontief joined [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University "Harvard University")'s department of economics in 1932 and in 1946 became [professor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Professor "Professor") of economics there.

In 1949, Leontief used an early computer at Harvard and data from the [U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Bureau_of_Labor_Statistics "U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics") to divide the U.S. economy into 500 sectors. Leontief modeled each sector with a linear equation based on the data and used the computer, the [Harvard Mark II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_Mark_II "Harvard Mark II"), to solve the system, one of the first significant uses of computers for mathematical modeling, along with [George W. Snedecor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_W._Snedecor "George W. Snedecor")'s usage of the [Atanasoff–Berry computer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atanasoff–Berry_computer "Atanasoff–Berry computer").

Leontief set up the Harvard Economic Research Project in 1948 and remained its director until 1973. Starting in 1965, he chaired the [Harvard Society of Fellows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_Society_of_Fellows "Harvard Society of Fellows").

### New York University

In 1975, Leontief joined [New York University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_University "New York University") and founded and directed the Institute for Economic Analysis. He taught graduate and undergraduate classes.

## Personal life

In 1932, Leontief married the poet Estelle Marks (1908–2005). Their only child, [Svetlana Leontief Alpers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Svetlana_Leontief_Alpers "Svetlana Leontief Alpers"), was born in 1936. Estelle wrote a memoir, *Genia and Wassily*, of their relations with his parents after they came to the US as émigrés.

As hobbies Leontief enjoyed [fly fishing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fly_fishing "Fly fishing"), ballet, and fine wines. He vacationed for years at his farm in [West Burke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Burke "West Burke"), Vermont, but after moving to New York in the 1970s moved his summer residence to [Lakeville, Connecticut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lakeville,_Connecticut "Lakeville, Connecticut").

Leontief died in New York City on Friday, February 5, 1999, at the age of 93.

## Major contributions

Leontief is credited with developing early contributions to [input–output analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Input–output_model "Input–output model") and was awarded the [Nobel Memorial Prize in Economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Memorial_Prize_in_Economic_Sciences "Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences") for his development of its associated theory. He has also made contributions in other areas of economics, such as [international trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heckscher-Ohlin_model "Heckscher-Ohlin model") where he documented the [Leontief paradox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leontief_paradox "Leontief paradox"). He was also one of the first to establish the composite commodity theorem.

Leontief was awarded the [Nobel Memorial Prize in economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Memorial_Prize_in_Economic_Sciences "Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences") for his work on input–output tables. Input–output tables analyze the process by which inputs from one industry produce outputs for consumption or for inputs for another industry. With the input–output table, one can estimate the change in demand for inputs resulting from a change in production of the final good. The analysis assumes that input proportions are fixed; thus the use of input–output analysis is limited to rough approximations rather than prediction. Input–output was novel and inspired large-scale empirical work; in 2010 its iterative method was recognized as an early intellectual precursor to [Google](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google "Google")'s [PageRank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PageRank "PageRank").

Leontief used input–output analysis to study the characteristics of trade flow between the U.S. and other countries, and found what has been named [Leontief's paradox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leontief_paradox "Leontief paradox"); "this country resorts to foreign trade in order to economize its [capital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capital_(economics) "Capital (economics)") and dispose of its [surplus labor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surplus_labor "Surplus labor"), rather than vice versa", i.e., U.S. exports were relatively labor-intensive when compared to U.S. imports. This is the opposite of what one would expect, considering the fact that the U.S.'s comparative advantage was in capital-intensive goods. According to some economists, this paradox has since been explained as due to the fact that when a country produces "more than two goods, the abundance of capital relative to labor does not imply that the capital intensity of its exports should exceed that of imports."

Leontief was also a very strong proponent of the use of quantitative data in the study of economics. Throughout his life Leontief campaigned against "theoretical assumptions and non-observed facts". According to Leontief, too many economists were reluctant to "get their hands dirty" by working with raw empirical facts. To that end, Wassily Leontief did much to make quantitative data more accessible, and more indispensable, to the study of economics.

## Quotes

> Much of current academic teaching and research has been criticized for its lack of relevance, that is, of immediate practical impact. ... The trouble is caused, however, not by an inadequate selection of targets, but rather by our inability to hit squarely on them, ... by the palpable inadequacy of the scientific means with which they try to solve them. ... The weak and all too slowly growing empirical foundations clearly cannot support the proliferating superstructure of pure, or should I say, speculative economic theory.... By the time it comes to interpretations of the substantive conclusions, the assumptions on which the model has been based are easily forgotten. But it is precisely the empirical validity of these assumptions on which the usefulness of the entire exercise depends. ... A natural Darwinian feedback operating through selection of academic personnel contributes greatly to the perpetuation of this state of affairs.

> The role of humans as the most important factor of production is bound to diminish in the same way that the role of horses in agricultural production was first diminished and then eliminated by the introduction of tractors.
