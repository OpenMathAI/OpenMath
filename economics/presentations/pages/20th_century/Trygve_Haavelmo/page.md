**Trygve Magnus Haavelmo** (13 December 1911 – 28 July 1999), born in [Skedsmo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skedsmo "Skedsmo"), [Norway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norway "Norway"), was an [economist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economist "Economist") whose research interests centered on [econometrics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Econometrics "Econometrics"). He received the [Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Memorial_Prize_in_Economic_Sciences "Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences") in 1989.

## Biography

After attending [Oslo Cathedral School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oslo_Cathedral_School "Oslo Cathedral School"), Haavelmo received a degree in [economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economics "Economics") from the University of Oslo in 1930 and eventually joined the Institute of Economics with the recommendation of [Ragnar Frisch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ragnar_Frisch "Ragnar Frisch"). Haavelmo was Frisch's assistant for a period of time until he was appointed as head of computations for the institute. In 1936, Haavelmo studied statistics at [University College London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_College_London "University College London") while he subsequently traveled to Berlin, Geneva, and Oxford for additional studies. Haavelmo assumed a lecturing position at the University of Aarhus in 1938 for one year and then in the subsequent year was offered an academic scholarship to travel abroad and study in the United States. During World War II he worked with [Nortraship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nortraship "Nortraship") in the Statistical Department in New York City. He received his PhD in 1946 from Oslo University for his work on *The Probability Approach in Econometrics* which he had completed while at Harvard in 1941.
He was a professor of economics and statistics at the [University of Oslo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Oslo "University of Oslo") between 1948–79 and was the trade department head of division from 1947 to 1948.

In 1989, Haavelmo was awarded the [Nobel Prize in Economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Economics "Nobel Prize in Economics") "for his clarification of the [probability theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability_theory "Probability theory") foundations of econometrics and his analyses of simultaneous economic structures."

Haavelmo resided at Østerås in [Bærum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bærum "Bærum"). He died on 28 July 1999 in Oslo.

## Legacy

[Judea Pearl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judea_Pearl "Judea Pearl") wrote "Haavelmo was the first to recognize the capacity of economic models to guide policies" and "presented a mathematical procedure that takes an arbitrary model and produces quantitative answers to policy questions". According to Pearl, "Haavelmo's paper, 'The Statistical Implications of a System of Simultaneous Equations', marks a pivotal turning point, not in the statistical implications of econometric models, as historians typically presume, but in their causal counterparts." Haavelmo's idea that an economic model depicts a series of hypothetical experiments and that policies can be simulated by modifying equations in the model became the basis of all currently used formalisms of econometric causal inference." (The biostatistics and epidemiology literature on causal inference draws from different sources.) It was first operationalized by [Robert H. Strotz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_H._Strotz "Robert H. Strotz") and [Herman Wold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herman_Wold "Herman Wold") (1960) who advocated "wiping out" selected equations, and then translated into graphical models as "wiping out" incoming arrows. This operation has subsequently led to Pearl's "do"-calculus and to a mathematical theory of [counterfactuals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counterfactual "Counterfactual") in econometric models. Pearl further speculates that the reason economists do not generally appreciate these revolutionary contributions of Haavelmo is because economists themselves have still not reached
consensus of what an economic model stands for, as attested by profound disagreements among econometric textbooks.
