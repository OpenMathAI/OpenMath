**Sir John Richard Hicks** (8 April 1904 – 20 May 1989) was a British [economist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economist "Economist"). He is considered one of the most important and influential economists of the twentieth century. The most familiar of his many contributions in the field of [economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economics "Economics") were his statement of [consumer demand theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consumer_theory "Consumer theory") in [microeconomics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microeconomics "Microeconomics"), and the [IS–LM model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IS–LM_model "IS–LM model") (1937), which summarised a [Keynesian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keynesian "Keynesian") view of [macroeconomics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macroeconomics "Macroeconomics"). His book *[Value and Capital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Value_and_Capital "Value and Capital")* (1939) significantly extended [general-equilibrium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_equilibrium_theory "General equilibrium theory") and value theory. The [compensated demand function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compensated_demand_function "Compensated demand function") is named the Hicksian demand function in memory of him.

In 1972 he received the [Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Memorial_Prize_in_Economic_Sciences "Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences") (jointly) for his pioneering contributions to [general equilibrium theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_equilibrium_theory "General equilibrium theory") and [welfare theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welfare_economics "Welfare economics").

## Early life

Hicks was born in 1904 in [Warwick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warwick "Warwick"), England, and was the son of Edward Hicks, editor and part proprietor of the Warwick and Leamington Spa Courier newspaper, and Dorothy Catherine, née Stephens, daughter of a non-conformist minister.

He was educated at [Clifton College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clifton_College "Clifton College") (1917–1922) and at [Balliol College, Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balliol_College,_Oxford "Balliol College, Oxford") (1922–1926), and was financed by mathematical scholarships. During his school days and in his first year at Oxford, he specialised in mathematics but also had interests in literature and history. In 1923, he moved to [Philosophy, Politics and Economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy,_Politics_and_Economics "Philosophy, Politics and Economics"), the "new school" that was just being started at Oxford. He graduated with [second-class honours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_undergraduate_degree_classification "British undergraduate degree classification") and, as he stated, "no adequate qualification in any of the subjects" that he had studied.

## Career

From 1926 to 1935, Hicks lectured at the [London School of Economics and Political Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_School_of_Economics "London School of Economics"). He started as a labour economist and did descriptive work on industrial relations but gradually, he moved over to the analytical side, where his mathematics background returned to the fore. Hicks's influences included [Lionel Robbins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lionel_Robbins "Lionel Robbins") and such associates as [Friedrich von Hayek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_von_Hayek "Friedrich von Hayek"), [R.G.D. Allen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R.G.D._Allen "R.G.D. Allen"), [Nicholas Kaldor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicholas_Kaldor "Nicholas Kaldor"), [Abba Lerner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abba_Lerner "Abba Lerner") and [Ursula Webb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lady_Ursula_Hicks "Lady Ursula Hicks"), the last of whom, in 1935, became his wife.

From 1935 to 1938, he lectured at [Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_University "Cambridge University") where he was also a fellow of [Gonville & Caius College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gonville_and_Caius_College,_Cambridge "Gonville and Caius College, Cambridge"). He was occupied mainly in writing *Value and Capital*, which was based on his earlier work in London. From 1938 to 1946, he was Professor at the [University of Manchester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Manchester "University of Manchester"). There, he did his main work on welfare economics, with its application to social accounting.

In 1946, he returned to [Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford_University "Oxford University"), first as a research fellow of [Nuffield College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuffield_College,_Oxford "Nuffield College, Oxford") (1946–1952) then as [Drummond Professor of Political Economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drummond_Professor_of_Political_Economy "Drummond Professor of Political Economy") (1952–1965) and finally as a research fellow of [All Souls College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All_Souls_College "All Souls College") (1965–1971), where he continued writing after his retirement.

## Later life

Hicks was knighted in 1964 and became an honorary fellow of [Linacre College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linacre_College "Linacre College"). He was co-recipient of the Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences (with [Kenneth J. Arrow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenneth_J._Arrow "Kenneth J. Arrow")) in 1972. He donated the Nobel Prize to the [London School of Economics and Political Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_School_of_Economics "London School of Economics")'s Library Appeal in 1973. He died on 20 May 1989 at his home in the [Cotswold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cotswold "Cotswold") village of [Blockley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blockley "Blockley").

Hicks's early work as a [labour economist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labour_economics "Labour economics") culminated in *[The Theory of Wages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Theory_of_Wages "The Theory of Wages")* (1932, 2nd ed. 1963), still considered standard in the field. He collaborated with R.G.D. Allen in two seminal papers on [value theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Value_theory "Value theory") published in 1934.

His [magnum opus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masterpiece "Masterpiece") is *[Value and Capital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Value_and_Capital "Value and Capital")* published in 1939. The book built on [ordinal utility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordinal_utility "Ordinal utility") and mainstreamed the now-standard distinction between the [substitution effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Substitution_effect "Substitution effect") and the [income effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Income_effect "Income effect") for an individual in [demand theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consumer_theory "Consumer theory") for the 2-good case. It generalised the analysis to the case of one good and a [composite good](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Composite_good "Composite good"), that is, all other goods. It aggregated individuals and businesses through demand and supply across the economy. It anticipated the [aggregation problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aggregation_problem "Aggregation problem"), most acutely for the stock of capital goods. It introduced [general equilibrium theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_equilibrium_theory "General equilibrium theory") to an English-speaking audience, refined the theory for dynamic analysis, and for the first time attempted a rigorous statement of stability conditions for general equilibrium. In the course of analysis Hicks formalised [comparative statics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_statics "Comparative statics"). In the same year, he also developed the famous "compensation" criterion called [Kaldor–Hicks efficiency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaldor–Hicks_efficiency "Kaldor–Hicks efficiency") for welfare comparisons of alternative public policies or economic states.

Hicks's most familiar contribution in [macroeconomics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macroeconomics "Macroeconomics") was the [Hicks–Hansen IS–LM model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IS–LM_model "IS–LM model"), published in his paper "[Mr. Keynes and the "Classics"; a suggested interpretation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mr_Keynes_and_the_Classics "Mr Keynes and the Classics")". This model formalised an interpretation of the theory of [John Maynard Keynes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Maynard_Keynes "John Maynard Keynes") (see [Keynesian economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keynesian_economics "Keynesian economics")), and describes the economy as a balance between three commodities: money, consumption and investment. Hicks himself wavered in his acceptance of his IS–LM formulation; in a paper published in 1980 he dismissed it as a 'classroom gadget'.

## Contributions to interpretation of income for accounting purposes

Hicks's influential discourse on [income](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Profit_(accounting) "Profit (accounting)") sets the basis for its subjectivity but relevancy for accounting purposes. He aptly summarized it as follows. "The purpose of income calculations in practical affairs is to give people an indication of the amount they can consume without impoverishing themselves".

Formally, he defined income precisely in three measures:

**Hicks's number 1 measure of income**: "the maximum amount, which can be spent during a period if there is to be an expectation of maintaining intact the capital value of prospective receipts (in money terms)" (Hicks, 1946, p. 173)

**Hicks's number 2 measure of income** (market price-neutral): "the maximum amount the individual can spend during a week, and still expect to be able to spend the same amount in each ensuing week" (Hicks, 1946, p. 174).

**Hicks's number 3 measure of income** (takes into account market prices): "the maximum amount of money which an individual can spend this week, and still expect to be able to spend the same amount in real terms in each ensuing week" (Hicks, 1946, p. 174)
