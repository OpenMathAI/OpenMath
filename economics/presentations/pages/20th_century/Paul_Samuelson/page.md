**Paul Anthony Samuelson** (May 15, 1915 – December 13, 2009) was an American economist who was the first American to win the [Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Memorial_Prize_in_Economic_Sciences "Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences"). When awarding the prize in 1970, the [Swedish Royal Academies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swedish_Royal_Academies "Swedish Royal Academies") stated that he "has done more than any other contemporary economist to raise the level of scientific analysis in economic theory".

Samuelson was one of the most influential economists of the latter half of the 20th century. In 1996, he was awarded the [National Medal of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Medal_of_Science "National Medal of Science"). Samuelson considered [mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics "Mathematics") to be the "natural language" for economists and contributed significantly to the mathematical foundations of economics with his book *[Foundations of Economic Analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foundations_of_Economic_Analysis "Foundations of Economic Analysis")*. He was author of the best-selling economics textbook of all time: *[Economics: An Introductory Analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economics:_An_Introductory_Analysis "Economics: An Introductory Analysis")*, first published in 1948. It was the second American textbook that attempted to explain the principles of [Keynesian economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keynesian_economics "Keynesian economics").

Samuelson served as an advisor to President [John F. Kennedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_F._Kennedy "John F. Kennedy") and President [Lyndon B. Johnson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyndon_B._Johnson "Lyndon B. Johnson"), and was a consultant to the [United States Treasury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Treasury "United States Treasury"), the Bureau of the Budget and the President's [Council of Economic Advisers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_of_Economic_Advisers "Council of Economic Advisers"). Samuelson wrote a weekly column for *[Newsweek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newsweek "Newsweek")* magazine along with [Chicago School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicago_school_(economics) "Chicago school (economics)") economist [Milton Friedman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milton_Friedman "Milton Friedman"), where they represented opposing sides: Samuelson, as a self described "Cafeteria Keynesian", claimed taking the Keynesian perspective but only accepting what he felt was good in it. By contrast, Friedman represented the [monetarist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monetarism "Monetarism") perspective. Together with [Henry Wallich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Wallich "Henry Wallich"), their 1967 columns earned the magazine a [Gerald Loeb Special Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerald_Loeb_Special_Award "Gerald Loeb Special Award") in 1968.

## Biography

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/86/Paul_Samuelson.jpg/250px-Paul_Samuelson.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Paul_Samuelson.jpg)

Samuelson in 1997

Samuelson was born in [Gary, Indiana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gary,_Indiana "Gary, Indiana"), on May 15, 1915, to Frank Samuelson, a [pharmacist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pharmacy "Pharmacy"), and Ella [née](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_name#Maiden_and_married_names "Birth name") Lipton. His family, he later said, was "made up of upwardly mobile [Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish "Jewish") immigrants from [Poland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poland "Poland") who had prospered considerably in [World War I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I "World War I"), because Gary was a brand new steel-town when my family went there". In 1923, Samuelson moved to Chicago where he graduated from Hyde Park High School (now [Hyde Park Career Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyde_Park_Career_Academy "Hyde Park Career Academy")).

Samuelson attended the [University of Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Chicago "University of Chicago") as an undergraduate, earning a [Bachelor of Arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_Arts "Bachelor of Arts") degree in 1935. He said he was born as an economist at 8:00 am on January 2, 1932, in the University of Chicago classroom. The lecture mentioned as the cause was on the British economist [Thomas Malthus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Robert_Malthus "Thomas Robert Malthus"), who most famously studied population growth and its effects. Samuelson felt there was a dissonance between [neoclassical economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoclassical_economics "Neoclassical economics") and the way the system seemed to behave; he said [Henry Simons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Calvert_Simons "Henry Calvert Simons") and [Frank Knight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Knight "Frank Knight") were a big influence on him. He next completed his [Master of Arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master_of_Arts "Master of Arts") degree in 1936, and his [Doctor of Philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Philosophy "Doctor of Philosophy") in 1941 at [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University "Harvard University"). He won the David A. Wells Prize in 1941 for writing the best doctoral dissertation at Harvard University in economics, for a thesis titled "Foundations of Analytical Economics", which later turned into *[Foundations of Economic Analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foundations_of_Economic_Analysis "Foundations of Economic Analysis")*. As a graduate student at Harvard, Samuelson studied economics under [Joseph Schumpeter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Schumpeter "Joseph Schumpeter"), [Wassily Leontief](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wassily_Leontief "Wassily Leontief"), [Gottfried Haberler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gottfried_Haberler "Gottfried Haberler"), and the "American Keynes" [Alvin Hansen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alvin_Hansen "Alvin Hansen").

Samuelson moved to MIT as an assistant professor in 1940 and remained there until his death. Samuelson's biographer argues that a central reason for Samuelson's move from Harvard to MIT was the anti-Semitism that was famously widespread at Harvard at the time. In a 1989 letter to his friend [Henry Rosovsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Rosovsky "Henry Rosovsky"), Samuelson blamed anti-Semitism in Harvard economics above all on chair Harold Burbank, as well as on [Edward Chamberlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Chamberlin "Edward Chamberlin"), [John H. Williams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Henry_Williams_(economist) "John Henry Williams (economist)"), [John D. Black](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_D._Black "John D. Black"), and Leonard Crum.

Samuelson's family included many well-known economists, including brother [Robert Summers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Summers_(economist) "Robert Summers (economist)"), sister-in-law [Anita Summers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anita_Summers "Anita Summers"), brother-in-law [Kenneth Arrow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenneth_Arrow "Kenneth Arrow") and nephew [Larry Summers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Larry_Summers "Larry Summers").

During his seven decades as an economist, Samuelson's professional positions included:

- Assistant professor of economics at MIT, 1940; associate professor, 1944.
- Member of the [Radiation Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MIT_Radiation_Laboratory "MIT Radiation Laboratory") 1944–45.
- Professor of international economic relations (part-time) at the [Fletcher School of Law and Diplomacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fletcher_School_of_Law_and_Diplomacy "Fletcher School of Law and Diplomacy") in 1945.
- Guggenheim Fellowship from 1948 to 1949
- Professor of economics at MIT beginning in 1947 and [Institute Professor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Institute_Professors "List of Institute Professors") beginning in 1962.
- Vernon F. Taylor Visiting Distinguished Professor at [Trinity University (Texas)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_University_(Texas) "Trinity University (Texas)") in spring 1989.

### Death

Samuelson died after a brief illness on December 13, 2009, at the age of 94. His death was announced by the [Massachusetts Institute of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massachusetts_Institute_of_Technology "Massachusetts Institute of Technology"). [James M. Poterba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_M._Poterba "James M. Poterba"), an economics professor at MIT and the president of the [National Bureau of Economic Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Bureau_of_Economic_Research "National Bureau of Economic Research"), commented that Samuelson "leaves an immense legacy, as a researcher and a teacher, as one of the giants on whose shoulders every contemporary economist stands". [Susan Hockfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susan_Hockfield "Susan Hockfield"), the president of MIT, said that Samuelson "transformed everything he touched: the theoretical foundations of his field, the way economics was taught around the world, the ethos and stature of his department, the investment practices of MIT, and the lives of his colleagues and students". His second wife died in 2019.

## Fields of interest

As professor of economics at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, Samuelson worked in many fields, including:

- [Consumer theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consumer_theory "Consumer theory"), where he pioneered the [revealed preference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revealed_preference "Revealed preference") approach, which is a method by which one can discern a consumer's [utility function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utility_function "Utility function"), by observing their behavior. Rather than postulate a [utility function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utility_function "Utility function") or a preference ordering, Samuelson imposed conditions directly on the choices made by individuals – their preferences as revealed by their choices.
- [Welfare economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welfare_economics "Welfare economics") and [public finance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_finance "Public finance") theory, in which he popularised the [Lindahl–Bowen–Samuelson conditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lindahl–Bowen–Samuelson_conditions "Lindahl–Bowen–Samuelson conditions") (criteria for deciding whether an action will improve welfare) and demonstrated in 1950 the insufficiency of a national-income index to reveal which of two social options was uniformly outside the other's (feasible) [possibility function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_welfare_function#Bergson–Samuelson_social_welfare_function "Social welfare function"), and he is particularly known for his work on determining the [optimal allocation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allocative_efficiency "Allocative efficiency") of resources in the presence of both [public goods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_good_(economics) "Public good (economics)") and [private goods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Private_good "Private good").
- [Capital theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capital_(economics) "Capital (economics)"), where he is known for 1958 consumption loans model and a variety of [turnpike theorems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turnpike_theory "Turnpike theory") and involved in [Cambridge capital controversy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_capital_controversy "Cambridge capital controversy").
- [Finance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finance "Finance") theory, in which he is known for the [random walk hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_walk_hypothesis "Random walk hypothesis") and [efficient-market hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Efficient-market_hypothesis "Efficient-market hypothesis").
- [International economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_economics "International economics"), where he influenced the development of two important international trade models: the [Balassa–Samuelson effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balassa–Samuelson_effect "Balassa–Samuelson effect"), and the [Heckscher–Ohlin model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heckscher–Ohlin_model "Heckscher–Ohlin model") (with the [Stolper–Samuelson theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stolper–Samuelson_theorem "Stolper–Samuelson theorem")).
- [Macroeconomics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macroeconomics "Macroeconomics"), where he popularized the [overlapping generations model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overlapping_generations_model "Overlapping generations model") as a way to analyze economic agents' behavior across multiple periods of time, developed [multiplier-accelerator model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplier-accelerator_model "Multiplier-accelerator model"), analyzed [Phillips curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phillips_curve "Phillips curve"), and contributed to formation of the [neoclassical synthesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoclassical_synthesis "Neoclassical synthesis").

## Impact

Samuelson is considered one of the founders of [neo-Keynesian economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-Keynesian_economics "Neo-Keynesian economics") and a seminal figure in the development of [neoclassical economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoclassical_economics "Neoclassical economics"). In awarding him the [Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Memorial_Prize_in_Economic_Sciences "Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences"), the committee stated:

> More than any other contemporary economist, Samuelson has helped to raise the general analytical and methodological level in economic science. He has simply rewritten considerable parts of economic theory. He has also shown the fundamental unity of both the problems and analytical techniques in economics, partly by a systematic application of the methodology of maximization for a broad set of problems. This means that Samuelson's contributions range over a large number of different fields.

He was also essential in creating the [neoclassical synthesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoclassical_synthesis "Neoclassical synthesis"), which ostensibly incorporated [Keynesian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keynesian_economics "Keynesian economics") and [neoclassical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoclassical_economics "Neoclassical economics") principles and still dominates current [mainstream economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mainstream_economics "Mainstream economics"). In 2003, Samuelson was one of the ten Nobel Prize–winning economists signing the [Economists' statement opposing the Bush tax cuts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economists'_statement_opposing_the_Bush_tax_cuts "Economists' statement opposing the Bush tax cuts").

Samuelson believed [unregulated markets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unregulated_markets "Unregulated markets") have drawbacks, he stated, "free markets do not stabilise themselves. Zero regulating is vastly suboptimal to rational regulating. Libertarianism is its own worst enemy!" Samuelson strongly criticised Friedman and [Friedrich Hayek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Hayek "Friedrich Hayek"), arguing their opposition to state intervention "tells us something about them rather than something about [Genghis Khan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genghis_Khan "Genghis Khan") or [Franklin Roosevelt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franklin_Roosevelt "Franklin Roosevelt"). It is paranoid to warn against inevitable slippery slopes ... once individual commercial freedoms are in any way infringed upon."

## Aphorisms and quotations

[Stanislaw Ulam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanislaw_Ulam "Stanislaw Ulam") once challenged Samuelson to name one theory in all of the social sciences that is both true and nontrivial. Several years later, Samuelson responded with [David Ricardo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Ricardo "David Ricardo")'s theory of [comparative advantage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_advantage "Comparative advantage"): "That it is logically true need not be argued before a mathematician; that it is not trivial is attested by the thousands of important and intelligent men who have never been able to grasp the [doctrine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctrine "Doctrine") for themselves or to believe it after it was explained to them."

For many years, Samuelson wrote a column for *[Newsweek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newsweek "Newsweek")*. One article included Samuelson's most quoted remark and a favorite economics joke:

> To prove that Wall Street is an early omen of movements still to come in GNP, commentators quote economic studies alleging that market downturns predicted four out of the last five recessions. That is an understatement. Wall Street indexes predicted nine out of the last five recessions! And its mistakes were beauties.

In the early editions of his famous, bestselling economics textbook Paul Samuelson joked that GDP falls when a man "marries his maid".

### *Foundations of Economic Analysis*

Paul Samuelson's book *[Foundations of Economic Analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foundations_of_Economic_Analysis "Foundations of Economic Analysis")* (1946) is considered his [magnum opus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masterpiece "Masterpiece"). It is derived from his [doctoral dissertation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctoral_dissertation "Doctoral dissertation"), and was inspired by the [classical thermodynamic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermodynamics#Classical_thermodynamics "Thermodynamics") methods. The book proposes to:

- Examine underlying analogies between central features in theoretical and applied economics and
- Study how *[operationally meaningful](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operationalism "Operationalism") theorems* can be derived with a small number of *analogous methods* (p. 3),

in order to derive "a general theory of economic theories" (Samuelson, 1983, p. xxvi). The book showed how these goals could be parsimoniously and fruitfully achieved, using the language of the mathematics applied to diverse subfields of economics. The book proposes two general hypotheses as sufficient for its purposes:

- *Maximizing behavior* of *agents* (including *consumers* as to utility and *business firms* as to profit) and
- Economic *systems* (including a market and an economy) in *stable equilibrium*.

The first tenet suggests that all actors, whether firms or consumers, are striving to maximize something. They could be attempting to maximize profits, utility, or wealth, but it did not matter because their efforts to improve their well-being would provide a basic model for all actors in an economic system. His second tenet focuses on providing insight on the workings of equilibrium in an economy. Generally in a market, supply would equal demand. However, he noted that this isn't always the case and that the important thing to look at was a system's natural resting point. *Foundations* presents the question of how an equilibrium would react when it is moved from its optimal point. Samuelson was also influential in providing explanations on how the changes in certain factors can affect an economic system. For example, he could explain the economic effect of changes in taxes or new technologies.

In the course of analysis, *[comparative statics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_statics "Comparative statics")*, (the analysis of changes in equilibrium of the system that result from a parameter change of the system) is formalized and clearly stated.

The chapter on [welfare economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welfare_economics "Welfare economics") "attempt(s) to give a brief but fairly complete survey of the whole field of welfare economics" (Samuelson, 1947, p. 252). It also exposits on and develops what became commonly called the [Bergson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abram_Bergson "Abram Bergson")–Samuelson [social welfare function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_welfare_function#Bergson–Samuelson_social_welfare_function "Social welfare function"). It shows how to represent (in the maximization calculus) all real-valued economic measures of any belief system that is required to rank consistently different feasible social configurations in an ethical sense as "better than", "worse than", or "indifferent to" each other (p. 221).

### *Economics*

Samuelson is also author (and from 1985 co-author) of an influential principles textbook, *[Economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economics:_An_Introductory_Analysis "Economics: An Introductory Analysis")*, first published in 1948 (19th ed. as of 2010; multiple reprints). The book sold more than 300,000 copies of each edition from 1961 through 1976 and was translated into forty-one languages. As of 2018, it had sold over four million copies. [William Nordhaus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Nordhaus "William Nordhaus") joined as co-author on the 12th edition (1985). Sometime before 1988, it had become the best-selling economics textbook of all time.

Samuelson was once quoted as saying, "Let those who will write the nation's laws if I can write its textbooks." Written in the shadow of the [Great Depression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Depression "Great Depression") and the [Second World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_World_War "Second World War"), it helped to popularize the insights of [John Maynard Keynes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Maynard_Keynes "John Maynard Keynes"). A main focus was how to avoid, or at least mitigate, the recurring slumps in economic activity.

Samuelson wrote: "It is not too much to say that the widespread creation of dictatorships and the resulting World War II stemmed in no small measure from the world's failure to meet this basic economic problem [the Great Depression] adequately." This reflected the concern of Keynes himself with the economic causes of war and the importance of economic policy in promoting peace.

Samuelson's book was the second to introduce Keynesian economics to a wide audience, and was by far the most successful. Canadian economist [Lorie Tarshis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorie_Tarshis "Lorie Tarshis"), who had been a student attending Keynes's lectures at Harvard in the 1930s, published in 1947 an introductory textbook that incorporated his lecture notes, titled *[Elements of Economics](https://archive.org/details/elementsofeconom030865mbp/page/n5/mode/2up)*.

Samuelson's textbook was a watershed in introducing the serious study of business cycles to the economics curriculum. It was particularly timely because it followed the Great Depression. The study of business cycles along with the introduction of the Keynesian approach of aggregate demand set the stage for the macroeconomic revolution in America, which then diffused throughout the world through translations into every major language. Generations of students, who then became teachers, learned their first and most influential lessons from Samuelson's *Economics.* It attracted many imitators, who became successful in different niches of the college market.

The text was not without criticism. While it praised the "mixed economy" of market and government, some found that too radical and attacked it as socialist. As a precursor to criticisms of Samuelson's *Economics* textbook, [Lorie Tarshis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorie_Tarshis "Lorie Tarshis")'s textbook was attacked by trustees of, and donors to, American colleges and universities as preaching a "[socialist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialist "Socialist") [heresy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heresy "Heresy")". Piling on, [William F. Buckley, Jr.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_F._Buckley,_Jr. "William F. Buckley, Jr."), in his 1951 book, *[God and Man at Yale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/God_and_Man_at_Yale "God and Man at Yale"),* devoted an entire chapter, attacking both Samuelson's and Tarshis' textbooks. For Samuelson's book, Buckley drew from the *Educational Examiner* and credited it as an "excellent review of Samuelson's text." ("Note to Chapter Two." p. 234) For Tarshis' book, Buckley drew from [Merwin K. Hart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merwin_K._Hart "Merwin K. Hart")'s organization *[to wit](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/to%20wit "wikt:to wit")*: "I am also grateful to the [National Economic Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Economic_Council,_Inc. "National Economic Council, Inc.") for its telling analysis of the Tarshis." ("Note to Chapter Two." p. 234) Buckley essentially characterized both as – in the words of [Paul Davidson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Davidson_(economist) "Paul Davidson (economist)") – "communist inspired". Buckley, for the rest of his life, defended the criticisms set forth in his book.

### Explanatory annotations

[↑](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Samuelson#cite_ref-Educational-Reviewer-info_54-0) The *Educational Reviewer* was founded in 1949 by Lucille Cardin Crain (*née* Marie Lucille Gabrielle Cardin; 1901–1983), a conservative activist whose primary interest was in – as she stated in 1951 – "rooting out radical influences in American education." In each issue, arch-conservative academicians and writers offered their views of high school and college textbooks as evidence of [collectivist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collectivism_and_individualism "Collectivism and individualism") content and the like. The publication, for the first three years, was chiefly financed by [William F. Buckley, Jr.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_F._Buckley,_Jr. "William F. Buckley, Jr.") Crain's husband, Kenneth Cardwell Crain (1883–1969), was a brother of [Gustavus Demetrious Crain, Jr.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gustavus_Demetrious_Crain,_Jr. "Gustavus Demetrious Crain, Jr.") (1885–1973), founder of [Crain Communications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crain_Communications "Crain Communications").
