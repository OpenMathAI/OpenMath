**Bertil Gotthard Ohlin** (Swedish: [[ˈbæ̌ʈːɪl ʊˈliːn]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Swedish "Help:IPA/Swedish")) (23 April 1899 – 3 August 1979) was a Swedish economist and politician. He was a professor of [economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economics "Economics") at the [Stockholm School of Economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stockholm_School_of_Economics "Stockholm School of Economics") from 1929 to 1965. He was also leader of the [People's Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberal_People's_Party_(Sweden) "Liberal People's Party (Sweden)"), a [social-liberal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_liberalism "Social liberalism") party which at the time was the largest party in opposition to the governing [Social Democratic Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swedish_Social_Democratic_Party "Swedish Social Democratic Party"), from 1944 to 1967. He served briefly as [Minister of Commerce and Industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_of_Commerce_and_Industry_(Sweden) "Minister of Commerce and Industry (Sweden)") from 1944 to 1945 in the Swedish [coalition government during World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_cabinet_of_Per_Albin_Hansson "Third cabinet of Per Albin Hansson"). He was President of the [Nordic Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nordic_Council "Nordic Council") in 1959 and 1964.

Ohlin's name lives on in one of the standard [mathematical models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_model "Mathematical model") of international [free trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_trade "Free trade"), the [Heckscher–Ohlin model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heckscher–Ohlin_model "Heckscher–Ohlin model"), which he developed together with [Eli Heckscher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eli_Heckscher "Eli Heckscher"). He was jointly awarded the [Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Memorial_Prize_in_Economic_Sciences "Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences") in 1977 together with the British economist [James Meade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Meade "James Meade") "for their pathbreaking contribution to the theory of [international trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_trade "International trade") and international [capital movements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capital_movement "Capital movement")".

## Biography

Bertil Ohlin was raised in [Klippan, Scania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klippan,_Scania "Klippan, Scania") with seven siblings, where his father Elis was a civil servant and bailiff. His mother Ingeborg influenced him with her left-liberal views on the society, with Nordic partnership and [Karl Staaff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Staaff "Karl Staaff") as her role model. He received his B.A. from [Lund University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lund_University "Lund University") 1917 at the age of 18 and his MSc. from [Stockholm School of Economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stockholm_School_of_Economics "Stockholm School of Economics") in 1919.

He obtained an M.A. from [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University "Harvard University") in 1923 and his doctorate from [Stockholm University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stockholm_University "Stockholm University") a year after in 1924 at the age of 25. In 1925, he became a professor at the [University of Copenhagen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Copenhagen "University of Copenhagen"). In 1929, he debated with [John Maynard Keynes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Maynard_Keynes "John Maynard Keynes") and contradicted the latter's view on the consequences of the heavy war [reparations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I_reparations "World War I reparations") payments imposed on [Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germany "Germany"). (Keynes predicted a war caused by the burden of [debt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Debt "Debt"), but Ohlin thought that [Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germany "Germany") could afford the reparations.) The debate was important in the modern theory of [unilateral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unilateral "Unilateral") [international payments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Payment_and_Exchange "International Payment and Exchange"). In 1930, Ohlin succeeded [Eli Heckscher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eli_Heckscher "Eli Heckscher"), his teacher, as a professor of [economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economics "Economics"), at the [Stockholm School of Economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stockholm_School_of_Economics "Stockholm School of Economics").

In 1937, Ohlin spent half a year at the [University of California, Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Berkeley "University of California, Berkeley"), as a visiting professor. He also worked as an outside expert for the [Economic and Financial Organization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_and_Financial_Organization_of_the_League_of_Nations "Economic and Financial Organization of the League of Nations") of the [League of Nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/League_of_Nations "League of Nations"), together with [Oskar Morgenstern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oskar_Morgenstern "Oskar Morgenstern") and [Jacques Rueff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques_Rueff "Jacques Rueff"), supporting the EFO's work on economic depressions in the late 1930s.

Ohlin was party leader of the [liberal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberalism "Liberalism") [Liberal People's Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberal_People's_Party_(Sweden) "Liberal People's Party (Sweden)") from 1944 to 1967, the main opposition party to the [Social Democrat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialdemokratiska_arbetarpartiet "Socialdemokratiska arbetarpartiet") Governments of the era, and from 1944 to 1945 was [Minister of Commerce and Industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_of_Commerce_and_Industry_(Sweden) "Minister of Commerce and Industry (Sweden)") in the wartime government. His daughter [Anne Wibble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anne_Wibble "Anne Wibble"), representing the same party, served as [Minister for Finance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_for_Finance_(Sweden) "Minister for Finance (Sweden)") from 1991 to 1994.

## Heckscher–Ohlin theorem

In 1933, Ohlin published *Interregional and International Trade*. Ohlin built in it an economic theory of international trade from earlier work by Heckscher and his own doctoral thesis. It is now known as the [Heckscher–Ohlin model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heckscher–Ohlin_model "Heckscher–Ohlin model"), one of the standard model economists use to debate [trade theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_trade "Free trade").

The model was a breakthrough because it showed how [comparative advantage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_advantage "Comparative advantage") might relate to general features of a country's [capital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capital_(economics) "Capital (economics)") and labor, and how those features might change through time. The model provided a basis for later work on the effects of protection on [real](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inflation "Inflation") wages, and has been fruitful in producing predictions and analysis; Ohlin himself used the model to derive the [Heckscher–Ohlin theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heckscher–Ohlin_theorem "Heckscher–Ohlin theorem"), which predicts that capital-abundant countries export capital-intensive goods, while labor-abundant countries export the labor-intensive goods.

The Heckscher–Ohlin Theorem, which is concluded from the [Heckscher–Ohlin model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heckscher–Ohlin_model "Heckscher–Ohlin model") of international trade, states: trade between countries is in proportion to their relative amounts of capital and labor. In countries with an abundance of capital, wage rates tend to be high; therefore, labor-intensive products, e.g. textiles, simple electronics, etc., are more costly to produce internally. In contrast, capital-intensive products, e.g. automobiles, chemicals, etc., are less costly to produce internally. Countries with large amounts of capital will export capital-intensive products and import labor-intensive products with the proceeds. Countries with high amounts of labor will do the reverse.

The following conditions must be true:

- The major factors of production, namely labor and capital, are not available in the same proportion in both countries.
- The two goods produced either require more capital or more labor.
- Labor and capital do not move between the two countries.
- There are no costs associated with transporting the goods between countries.
- The citizens of the two trading countries have the same needs.

The theory does not depend on total amounts of capital or labor, but on the amounts per worker. This allows small countries to trade with large countries by specializing in production of products that use the factors which are more available than its trading partner. The key assumption is that capital and labor are not available in the same proportions in the two countries. That leads to specialization, which in turn benefits the country's economic welfare. The greater the difference between the two countries, the greater the gain from specialization.

[Wassily Leontief](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wassily_Leontief "Wassily Leontief") made a study of the theory that seemed to invalidate it. He noted that the United States had a lot of capital; therefore, it should export capital-intensive products and import labor-intensive products. Instead, he found that it exported products that used more labor than the products it imported. This finding is known as the [Leontief paradox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leontief_paradox "Leontief paradox").
