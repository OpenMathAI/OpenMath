**Brook Taylor** [FRS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow_of_the_Royal_Society "Fellow of the Royal Society") (18 August 1685 – 29 December 1731) was an English mathematician and barrister best known for several results in [mathematical analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_analysis "Mathematical analysis"). Taylor's most famous developments are [Taylor's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taylor's_theorem "Taylor's theorem") and the [Taylor series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taylor_series "Taylor series"), essential in the infinitesimal approach of functions in specific points.

## Life and work

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/56/Taylor_-_Methodus_incrementorum_directa_et_inversa%2C_1715_-_811460.tif/lossy-page1-250px-Taylor_-_Methodus_incrementorum_directa_et_inversa%2C_1715_-_811460.tif.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Taylor_-_Methodus_incrementorum_directa_et_inversa,_1715_-_811460.tif)

*Methodus incrementorum directa et inversa*, 1715

Brook Taylor was born in [Edmonton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmonton,_London "Edmonton, London") (former [Middlesex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middlesex "Middlesex")). Taylor was the son of John Taylor, MP of [Patrixbourne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patrixbourne "Patrixbourne"), Kent and Olivia Tempest, the daughter of John Tempest of Edmonton, and not [Sir Nicholas Tempest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_Nicolas_Tempest,_1st_Baronet "Sir Nicolas Tempest, 1st Baronet"), Baronet of Durham.

He entered [St John's College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_John's_College,_Cambridge "St John's College, Cambridge"), as a [fellow-commoner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow_commoner "Fellow commoner") in 1701, and took degrees in [LL.B.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_Law "Bachelor of Law") in 1709 and [LL.D.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LL.D. "LL.D.") in 1714. Taylor studied [mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics "Mathematics") under [John Machin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Machin "John Machin") and [John Keill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Keill "John Keill"), leading to Taylor obtaining a solution to the problem of "[center of oscillation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_of_oscillation "Center of oscillation")". Taylor's solution remained unpublished until May 1714, when his claim to [priority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_priority "Scientific priority") was disputed by [Johann Bernoulli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Bernoulli "Johann Bernoulli").

Taylor's [*Methodus Incrementorum Directa et Inversa* (1715)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brook_Taylor#CITEREFTaylor1715a) ("Direct and Indirect Methods of Incrementation") added a new branch to higher mathematics, called "[calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calculus "Calculus") of [finite differences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_difference "Finite difference")". Taylor used this development to determine the form of movement in vibrating strings. Taylor also wrote the first satisfactory investigation of [astronomical refraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_refraction "Astronomical refraction"). The same work contains the well-known [Taylor's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taylor's_theorem "Taylor's theorem"), the importance of which remained unrecognized until 1772, when [Joseph-Louis Lagrange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph-Louis_Lagrange "Joseph-Louis Lagrange") realized its usefulness and termed it "the main foundation of differential calculus".

In Taylor's 1715 essay [*Linear Perspective*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brook_Taylor#CITEREFTaylor1715b), Taylor set forth the principles of perspective in a more understandable form, but the work suffered from brevity and obscurity problems which plagued most of his writings, meaning the essay required further explanation in the treatises of [Joshua Kirby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joshua_Kirby "Joshua Kirby") (1754) and Daniel Fournier (1761).

Taylor was elected as a fellow in the [Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society "Royal Society") in 1712. In the same year, Taylor sat on the committee for adjudicating the claims of Sir [Isaac Newton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Newton "Isaac Newton") and [Gottfried Leibniz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gottfried_Leibniz "Gottfried Leibniz"). He acted as secretary to the society from 13 January 1714 to 21 October 1718.

From 1715 onward, Taylor's studies took a philosophical and religious bent. He corresponded with the [Comte de Montmort](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comte_de_Montmorency "Comte de Montmorency") on the subject of [Nicolas Malebranche](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolas_Malebranche "Nicolas Malebranche")'s tenets. Unfinished treatises written on his return from [Aix-la-Chapelle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aix-la-Chapelle "Aix-la-Chapelle") in 1719, *On the Jewish Sacrifices* and *On the Lawfulness of Eating Blood*, were afterwards found among his papers.

Taylor was one of few English mathematicians, along with Isaac Newton and [Roger Cotes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roger_Cotes "Roger Cotes"), who was capable of holding his own with the [Bernoullis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernoulli_family "Bernoulli family"), but a lack of clarity affected a great part of his demonstrations and Taylor lost brevity through his failure to express his ideas fully and clearly.

His health began to fail in 1717 after years of intense work.

Taylor married Miss Brydges of [Wallington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wallington,_London "Wallington, London"), Surrey in 1721 without his father's approval. The marriage led to an estrangement with his father, which improved in 1723 after Taylor's wife died in childbirth while giving birth to a son. Taylor's son did not survive.

He spent the next two years with his family at Bifrons, and in 1725 he married with his father's approval, Sabetta Sawbridge of [Olantigh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olantigh "Olantigh"), [Kent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kent "Kent"). She died in childbirth in 1730, though his only daughter, Elizabeth, survived.

Taylor's father died in 1729, leaving Taylor to inherit the Bifrons estate.

Taylor died at the age of 46, on 29 December 1731, at [Somerset House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somerset_House "Somerset House"), London.

## Selected writings

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/53/Taylor_Brook_Goupy_NPG.jpg/250px-Taylor_Brook_Goupy_NPG.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Taylor_Brook_Goupy_NPG.jpg)

Brook Taylor

Taylor's grandson, Sir William Young, printed a [posthumous work](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Posthumous_work "Posthumous work") entitled *Contemplatio Philosophica* for private circulation in 1793, (2nd Bart., 10 January 1815). The work was prefaced by a biography, and had an appendix containing letters addressed to him by [Bolingbroke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_St_John,_1st_Viscount_Bolingbroke "Henry St John, 1st Viscount Bolingbroke"), [Bossuet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques-Bénigne_Bossuet "Jacques-Bénigne Bossuet"), and others.

Several short papers by Taylor were published in *Phil. Trans.,* vols. xxvii to xxxii, which including accounts of experiments in [magnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetism "Magnetism") and [capillary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capillary "Capillary") attraction. In 1719, Brook issued an improved version of his work on perspective, *New Principles of Linear Perspective*, which was revised by [John Colson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Colson "John Colson") in 1749. A French translation was published in 1757. It was reprinted, with a portrait and short biography, in 1811.

## Tribute

[Taylor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taylor_(crater) "Taylor (crater)") is an [impact crater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impact_crater "Impact crater") located on the [Moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moon "Moon"), named in honor of Brook Taylor in 1935.
