**Colin Maclaurin** ([/məˈklɔːrən/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English"); [Scottish Gaelic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scottish_Gaelic_language "Scottish Gaelic language"): *Cailean MacLabhruinn*; February 1698 – 14 June 1746) was a Scottish [mathematician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematician "Mathematician") who made important contributions to [geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometry "Geometry") and [algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebra "Algebra"). He is also known for being a [child prodigy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Child_prodigy "Child prodigy") and holding the record for being the youngest professor. The Maclaurin series, a special case of the [Taylor series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taylor_series "Taylor series"), is named after him.

Owing to changes in [orthography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthography "Orthography") since that time (his name was originally rendered as **M'Laurine**), his surname is alternatively written **MacLaurin.**

## Early life

Maclaurin was born in [Kilmodan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilmodan "Kilmodan"), [Scotland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scotland "Scotland") and was the youngest of three sons. His father, John Maclaurin, a minister, died when Maclaurin was in infancy, and his mother died when he was nine years of age. He was then raised by his uncle, Daniel MacLaurin.

## Academic career

At eleven, Maclaurin, a [child prodigy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Child_prodigy "Child prodigy") at the time, entered the [University of Glasgow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Glasgow "University of Glasgow"). He graduated [Master of Arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master_of_Arts "Master of Arts") three years later by defending a thesis on *the Power of Gravity,* and remained at Glasgow to study [divinity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divinity_(academic_discipline) "Divinity (academic discipline)") until he was 19, when he was elected professor of [mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics "Mathematics") in a ten-day competition at [Marischal College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marischal_College "Marischal College") and University in Aberdeen. This record as the world's youngest professor endured until February 2008, when [Alia Sabur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alia_Sabur "Alia Sabur") was appointed as a professor in South Korea for one year.

In the vacations of 1719 and 1721, Maclaurin went to London, where he became acquainted with [Isaac Newton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Newton "Isaac Newton"), [Benjamin Hoadly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benjamin_Hoadly_(physician) "Benjamin Hoadly (physician)"), [Samuel Clarke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Clarke "Samuel Clarke"), [Martin Folkes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Folkes "Martin Folkes"), and other philosophers. He was admitted as a member of the [Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society "Royal Society").

In 1722, having provided a temporary replacement teacher for his class at Aberdeen, he travelled on the Continent as tutor to George Hume, the son of [Alexander Hume, 2nd Earl of Marchmont](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Hume,_2nd_Earl_of_Marchmont "Alexander Hume, 2nd Earl of Marchmont"). During their time in [Lorraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duchy_of_Lorraine "Duchy of Lorraine"), he wrote his essay on the percussion of bodies (*Demonstration des loix du choc des corps*), which gained the prize of the [Royal Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Academy_of_Sciences "French Academy of Sciences") in 1724. Upon the death of his pupil at [Montpellier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montpellier "Montpellier"), Maclaurin returned to Aberdeen.

In 1725, Maclaurin was appointed deputy to the mathematical professor at the [University of Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Edinburgh "University of Edinburgh"), James Gregory (brother of [David Gregory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Gregory_(mathematician) "David Gregory (mathematician)") and nephew of the esteemed [James Gregory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Gregory_(astronomer_and_mathematician) "James Gregory (astronomer and mathematician)")), upon the recommendation of [Isaac Newton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Newton "Isaac Newton"). On 3 November of that year Maclaurin succeeded Gregory, and went on to raise the character of that university as a school of science. Newton was so impressed with Maclaurin that he had offered to pay his salary himself.

## Contributions to mathematics

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/37/Acta_Eruditorum_-_I_monete_geometria%2C_1747_%E2%80%93_BEIC_13417751_%28cropped%29.jpg/250px-Acta_Eruditorum_-_I_monete_geometria%2C_1747_%E2%80%93_BEIC_13417751_%28cropped%29.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Acta_Eruditorum_-_I_monete_geometria,_1747_–_BEIC_13417751_(cropped).jpg)

Illustration of critique of *De fluxionibus libri duo* published in [Acta Eruditorum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acta_Eruditorum "Acta Eruditorum"), 1747

Maclaurin used [Taylor series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taylor_series "Taylor series") to characterize maxima, minima, and points of inflection for infinitely differentiable functions in his *Treatise of Fluxions*. Maclaurin attributed the series to [Brook Taylor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brook_Taylor "Brook Taylor"), though the series was known before to [Newton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Newton "Isaac Newton") and [Gregory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Gregory_(mathematician) "James Gregory (mathematician)").
Nevertheless, Maclaurin received credit for his use of the series, and the Taylor series expanded around 0 is sometimes known as the *Maclaurin series*.

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c8/Colin_Maclaurin_color.jpg/250px-Colin_Maclaurin_color.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Colin_Maclaurin_color.jpg)

Colin Maclaurin (1698–1746)

Maclaurin also made significant contributions to the gravitation attraction of ellipsoids, a subject that furthermore attracted the attention of d'Alembert, A.-C. Clairaut, Euler, Laplace, Legendre, Poisson and Gauss. Maclaurin showed that an oblate spheroid was a possible equilibrium in Newton's theory of gravity. The subject continues to be of scientific interest, and Nobel Laureate [Subramanyan Chandrasekhar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subramanyan_Chandrasekhar "Subramanyan Chandrasekhar") dedicated a chapter of his book *Ellipsoidal Figures of Equilibrium* to [Maclaurin spheroids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maclaurin_spheroid "Maclaurin spheroid"). Maclaurin corresponded extensively with [Clairaut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexis_Clairaut "Alexis Clairaut"), [Maupertuis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Louis_Maupertuis "Pierre Louis Maupertuis"), and [d'Ortous de Mairan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Jacques_d'Ortous_de_Mairan "Jean-Jacques d'Ortous de Mairan").

Independently from [Euler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler "Euler") and using the same methods, Maclaurin discovered the [Euler–Maclaurin formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler–Maclaurin_formula "Euler–Maclaurin formula"). He used it to sum powers of [arithmetic progressions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arithmetic_progression "Arithmetic progression"), derive [Stirling's formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stirling's_formula "Stirling's formula"), and to derive the Newton–Cotes numerical integration formulas which includes [Simpson's rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simpson's_rule "Simpson's rule") as a special case.

Maclaurin contributed to the study of [elliptic integrals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptic_integral "Elliptic integral"), reducing many intractable integrals to problems of finding arcs for hyperbolas. His work was continued by d'Alembert and Euler, who gave a more concise approach.

In his *Treatise of Algebra* (Ch. XII, Sect 86), published in 1748 two years after his death, Maclaurin proved a rule for solving square linear systems in the cases of 2 and 3 unknowns, and discussed the case of 4 unknowns. This publication preceded by two years [Cramer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gabriel_Cramer "Gabriel Cramer")'s publication of a generalization of the rule to *n* unknowns, now commonly known as [Cramer's rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cramer's_rule "Cramer's rule").

## Personal life

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e2/The_grave_of_Colin_Maclaurin%2C_Greyfriars_Kirkyard.jpg/330px-The_grave_of_Colin_Maclaurin%2C_Greyfriars_Kirkyard.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:The_grave_of_Colin_Maclaurin,_Greyfriars_Kirkyard.jpg)

The grave of Colin Maclaurin, Greyfriars Kirkyard

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/2e/Colin_MacLaurin_Road%2C_Edinburgh.JPG/250px-Colin_MacLaurin_Road%2C_Edinburgh.JPG?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Colin_MacLaurin_Road,_Edinburgh.JPG)

Colin Maclaurin Road, Edinburgh

In 1733, Maclaurin married Anne Stewart, the daughter of Walter Stewart, the [Solicitor General for Scotland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solicitor_General_for_Scotland "Solicitor General for Scotland"), by whom he had seven children. His eldest son [John Maclaurin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Maclaurin,_Lord_Dreghorn "John Maclaurin, Lord Dreghorn") studied law, was a [Senator of the College of Justice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senator_of_the_College_of_Justice "Senator of the College of Justice"), and became Lord Dreghorn; he was also joint founder of the [Royal Society of Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society_of_Edinburgh "Royal Society of Edinburgh").

Maclaurin actively opposed the [Jacobite rising of 1745](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobite_rising_of_1745 "Jacobite rising of 1745") and superintended the operations necessary for the defence of Edinburgh against the Highland army. Maclaurin compiled a diary of his exertions against the Jacobites, both within and without the city. When the Highland army entered the city, however, he fled to [York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/York "York"), where he was invited to stay by the [Archbishop of York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archbishop_of_York "Archbishop of York").

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d3/Maclaurin_grave.jpg/250px-Maclaurin_grave.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Maclaurin_grave.jpg)

Memorial, Greyfriars Kirkyard, Edinburgh

On his journey south, Maclaurin fell from his horse, and the fatigue, anxiety, and cold to which he was exposed on that occasion laid the foundations of [dropsy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dropsy "Dropsy"). He returned to Edinburgh after the [Jacobite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobitism "Jacobitism") army marched south, but died soon after his return.

He is buried at [Greyfriars Kirkyard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greyfriars_Kirkyard "Greyfriars Kirkyard"), [Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edinburgh "Edinburgh"). The simple table stone is inscribed simply "C. M. Nat MDCXCVIII Ob MDCCXLVI" and stands close to the south-west corner of the church but is supplemented by a more wordy memorial on the outer wall of the church.

The mathematician and former [MIT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MIT "MIT") President [Richard Cockburn Maclaurin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Cockburn_Maclaurin "Richard Cockburn Maclaurin") was from the same family.

The Maclaurin Society (MacSoc), the Mathematics and Statistics Society at Glasgow University, is named in his honour.

Colin Maclaurin Road within Edinburgh University's [King's Buildings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King's_Buildings "King's Buildings") complex is named in his honour.

## Notable works

Some of his important works are:

- *Geometria Organica* – 1720
- *De Linearum Geometricarum Proprietatibus* – 1720
- *Treatise on Fluxions* – 1742 (763 pages in two volumes. The first systematic exposition of Newton's methods.)
- *Treatise of Algebra* – 1748 (two years after his death.)
- *Account of Newton's Discoveries* – Incomplete upon his death and published in 1748
- [*Account of Sir Isaac Newton's philosophical discoveries*](https://gutenberg.beic.it/webclient/DeliveryManager?pid=743185) (in French). Paris: Laurent Durand. 1749.

Colin Maclaurin was the name used for the new Mathematics and Actuarial Mathematics and Statistics Building at [Heriot-Watt University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heriot-Watt_University "Heriot-Watt University"), Edinburgh.
