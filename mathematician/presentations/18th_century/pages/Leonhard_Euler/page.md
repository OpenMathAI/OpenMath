**Leonhard Euler** ([/ˈɔɪlər/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English") [*OY-lər*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:Pronunciation_respelling_key "Help:Pronunciation respelling key"); 15 April 1707 – 18 September 1783) was a Swiss [polymath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polymath "Polymath") who was active as a mathematician, physicist, astronomer, logician, geographer, music theorist and engineer. He founded the studies of [graph theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graph_theory "Graph theory") and [topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topology "Topology") and made influential discoveries in many other branches of mathematics, such as [analytic number theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytic_number_theory "Analytic number theory"), [complex analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_analysis "Complex analysis"), and [infinitesimal calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinitesimal_calculus "Infinitesimal calculus"). He also introduced much of modern [mathematical terminology and notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_notation "Mathematical notation"), including the notion of a [mathematical function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_function "Mathematical function"). He is known for his work in [mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanics "Mechanics"), [fluid dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluid_dynamics "Fluid dynamics"), optics, astronomy, and music theory. Euler has been called a "universal genius" who "was fully equipped with almost unlimited powers of imagination, intellectual gifts and extraordinary memory". He spent most of his adult life in [Saint Petersburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Petersburg "Saint Petersburg"), Russia, and in [Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berlin "Berlin"), then the capital of [Prussia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Prussia "Kingdom of Prussia").

Euler made contributions to [applied mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Applied_mathematics "Applied mathematics") and [engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineering "Engineering"), such as his study of ships helping navigation, his three volumes on optics, which contributed to the design of [microscopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microscope "Microscope") and [telescopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telescope "Telescope"), and his studies of beam bending and column [critical loads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Load_(unit) "Load (unit)").

Euler is credited with being the first to develop graph theory (partly as a solution for the problem of the [Seven Bridges of Königsberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seven_Bridges_of_Königsberg "Seven Bridges of Königsberg"), which is also considered the first practical application of topology). He also became famous for, among many other accomplishments, solving several unsolved problems in number theory and analysis, including the famous [Basel problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basel_problem "Basel problem"). Euler has also been credited for discovering that the sum of the numbers of vertices and faces minus the number of edges of a [polyhedron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyhedron "Polyhedron") that has no holes equals 2, a number now commonly known as the [Euler characteristic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler_characteristic "Euler characteristic"). In physics, Euler reformulated [Isaac Newton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Newton "Isaac Newton")'s [laws of motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton's_laws_of_motion "Newton's laws of motion") into [new laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler's_laws_of_motion "Euler's laws of motion") in his two-volume work *[Mechanica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanica "Mechanica")* to better explain the motion of [rigid bodies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rigid_bodies "Rigid bodies"). He contributed to the study of [elastic deformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler–Bernoulli_beam_theory "Euler–Bernoulli beam theory") of solid objects. Euler formulated the [partial differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler_equations_(fluid_dynamics) "Euler equations (fluid dynamics)") for the motion of [inviscid fluid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inviscid_flow "Inviscid flow"), and laid the mathematical foundations of [potential theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potential_theory "Potential theory").

Euler is regarded as arguably the most prolific contributor in the history of mathematics and science, and the greatest mathematician of the 18th century. His 866 publications and his correspondence were collected in the *[Opera Omnia Leonhard Euler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opera_Omnia_Leonhard_Euler "Opera Omnia Leonhard Euler")*. Several great mathematicians who worked after Euler's death have recognised his importance in the field: [Pierre-Simon Laplace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre-Simon_Laplace "Pierre-Simon Laplace") said, "Read Euler, read Euler, he is the master of us all"; [Carl Friedrich Gauss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Friedrich_Gauss "Carl Friedrich Gauss") wrote: "The study of Euler's works will remain the best school for the different fields of mathematics, and nothing else can replace it."

## Early life

Leonhard Euler was born in [Basel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basel "Basel") on 15 April 1707 to Paul III Euler, a pastor of the [Reformed Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reformed_Church "Reformed Church"), and Marguerite ([née](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_name#Maiden_and_married_names "Birth name") Brucker), whose ancestors include a number of well-known scholars in the [classics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classics "Classics"). He was the oldest of four children, with two younger sisters, Anna Maria and Maria Magdalena, and a younger brother, Johann Heinrich. Soon after Leonhard's birth, the Eulers moved from Basel to [Riehen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riehen "Riehen"), Switzerland, where his father became pastor in the local church and Leonhard spent most of his childhood.

From a young age, Euler received schooling in mathematics from his father, who had taken courses from [Jacob Bernoulli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacob_Bernoulli "Jacob Bernoulli") some years earlier at the [University of Basel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Basel "University of Basel"). Around the age of eight, Euler was sent to live at his maternal grandmother's house and enrolled in the Latin school in Basel. In addition, he received private tutoring from Johannes Burckhardt, a young theologian with a keen interest in mathematics.

In 1720, at age 13, Euler enrolled at the [University of Basel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Basel "University of Basel"). Attending university at such a young age was not unusual at the time. The course on elementary mathematics was given by [Johann Bernoulli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Bernoulli "Johann Bernoulli"), the younger brother of the deceased Jacob Bernoulli, who had taught Euler's father. Johann Bernoulli and Euler soon got to know each other better. Euler described Bernoulli in his autobiography:

> The famous professor Johann Bernoulli [...] made it a special pleasure for himself to help me along in the mathematical sciences. Private lessons, however, he refused because of his busy schedule. However, he gave me a far more salutary advice, which consisted in myself getting a hold of some of the more difficult mathematical books and working through them with great diligence, and should I encounter some objections or difficulties, he offered me free access to him every Saturday afternoon, and he was gracious enough to comment on the collected difficulties, which was done with such a desired advantage that, when he resolved one of my objections, ten others at once disappeared, which certainly is the best method of making happy progress in the mathematical sciences.

During this time, Euler, backed by Bernoulli, obtained his father's consent to become a mathematician instead of a pastor.

In 1723, Euler received a [Master of Philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master_of_Philosophy "Master of Philosophy") with a dissertation that compared the philosophies of [René Descartes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/René_Descartes "René Descartes") and [Isaac Newton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Newton "Isaac Newton"). Afterwards, he enrolled in the theological faculty of the University of Basel.

In 1726, Euler completed a dissertation on the [propagation of sound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_sound "Speed of sound") titled *De Sono*, with which he unsuccessfully attempted to obtain a position at the University of Basel. In 1727, he entered the [Paris Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Academy_of_Sciences "French Academy of Sciences") prize competition (offered annually and later biennially by the academy beginning in 1720) for the first time. The problem posed that year was to find the best way to place the [masts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mast_(sailing) "Mast (sailing)") on a ship. [Pierre Bouguer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Bouguer "Pierre Bouguer"), who became known as "the father of naval architecture", won and Euler took second place. Over the years, Euler entered this competition 15 times, winning 12 of them.

## Career

### First Saint Petersburg period (1727–1741)

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/db/The_Soviet_Union_1957_CPA_2000_stamp%2C_Portrait_of_Leonhard_Euler_%281707-1783%29.jpg/250px-The_Soviet_Union_1957_CPA_2000_stamp%2C_Portrait_of_Leonhard_Euler_%281707-1783%29.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Euler-USSR-1957-stamp.jpg)

1957 [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union "Soviet Union") stamp commemorating the 250th birthday of Euler. The text says: 250 years from the birth of the great mathematician, academician Leonhard Euler.

Johann Bernoulli's two sons, [Daniel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Bernoulli "Daniel Bernoulli") and [Nicolaus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolaus_II_Bernoulli "Nicolaus II Bernoulli"), entered into service at the [Imperial Russian Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Academy_of_Sciences "Russian Academy of Sciences") in [Saint Petersburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Petersburg "Saint Petersburg") in 1725, leaving Euler with the assurance they would recommend him to a post when one was available. On 31 July 1726, Nicolaus died of appendicitis after spending less than a year in Russia. When Daniel assumed his brother's position in the mathematics/physics division, he recommended that the post in physiology that he had vacated be filled by his friend Euler. In November 1726, Euler eagerly accepted the offer, but delayed making the trip to Saint Petersburg while he unsuccessfully applied for a physics professorship at the University of Basel.

Euler arrived in Saint Petersburg in May 1727. He was promoted from his junior post in the medical department of the academy to a position in the mathematics department. He lodged with Daniel Bernoulli with whom he worked in close collaboration. Euler mastered Russian, settled into life in Saint Petersburg and took on an additional job as a medic in the [Russian Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_Russian_Navy "Imperial Russian Navy").

The academy at Saint Petersburg, established by [Peter the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_the_Great "Peter the Great"), was intended to improve education in Russia and to close the scientific gap with Western Europe. As a result, it was made especially attractive to foreign scholars like Euler. The academy's benefactress, [Catherine I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catherine_I_of_Russia "Catherine I of Russia"), who had continued the progressive policies of her late husband, died before Euler's arrival to Saint Petersburg. The Russian conservative nobility then gained power upon the ascension of the twelve-year-old [Peter II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_II_of_Russia "Peter II of Russia"). The nobility, suspicious of the academy's foreign scientists, cut funding for Euler and his colleagues and prevented the entrance of foreign and non-aristocratic students into the Gymnasium and universities.

Conditions improved slightly after the death of Peter II in 1730 and the German-influenced [Anna of Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anna_of_Russia "Anna of Russia") assumed power. Euler swiftly rose through the ranks in the academy and was made a professor of physics in 1731. In 1730, he began a lengthy correspondence with the secretary of the academy [Johann Daniel Schumacher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Daniel_Schumacher "Johann Daniel Schumacher") which continued until 1757, long after Euler had left for Berlin, comprising more than 300 letters in total. They shared scientific news and Euler advised Schumacher on suitable candidates for vacancies at the academy (such as the mathematician and astronomer [Tobias Mayer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tobias_Mayer "Tobias Mayer"), the natural philosopher [Johann Peter Eberhard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Peter_Eberhard?action=edit&redlink=1 "Johann Peter Eberhard"), and the mathematician and philosopher [Johann Konrad Spangenberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Konrad_Spangenberg?action=edit&redlink=1 "Johann Konrad Spangenberg")), as well as sending scientific equipment and his own latest publications. From the 1750s, he corresponded similarly with [Gerhard Friedrich Müller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerhard_Friedrich_Müller "Gerhard Friedrich Müller"), to whom Schumacher delegated many of his responsibilities.

In 1731 Euler also left the Russian Navy, refusing a promotion to [lieutenant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lieutenant_(navy) "Lieutenant (navy)"). Two years later, Daniel Bernoulli, fed up with the censorship and hostility he faced at Saint Petersburg, left for Basel. Euler succeeded him as the head of the mathematics department. In January 1734, he married Katharina Gsell (1707–1773), a daughter of [Georg Gsell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georg_Gsell "Georg Gsell"). [Frederick II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_the_Great "Frederick the Great") had made an attempt to recruit the services of Euler for his newly established [Berlin Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prussian_Academy_of_Sciences "Prussian Academy of Sciences") in 1740, but Euler initially preferred to stay in St Petersburg. But after Empress Anna died and Frederick II agreed to pay 1600 [ecus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Écu "Écu") (the same as Euler earned in Russia) he agreed to move to Berlin. In 1741, he requested permission to leave for Berlin, arguing he was in need of a milder climate for his eyesight. The Russian academy gave its consent and would pay him 200 rubles per year as one of its active members.

### Berlin period (1741–1766)

Concerned about the continuing turmoil in Russia, Euler left St. Petersburg in June 1741 to take up a post at the [Berlin Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prussian_Academy_of_Sciences "Prussian Academy of Sciences"), which he had been offered by [Frederick the Great of Prussia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_the_Great_of_Prussia "Frederick the Great of Prussia"). He lived for 25 years in [Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berlin "Berlin"), where he wrote several hundred articles. In 1748 his text on functions called the *[Introductio in analysin infinitorum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Introductio_in_analysin_infinitorum "Introductio in analysin infinitorum")* was published and in 1755 a text on [differential calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_calculus "Differential calculus") called the *[Institutiones calculi differentialis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institutiones_calculi_differentialis "Institutiones calculi differentialis")* was published. In 1755, he was elected a foreign member of the [Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Swedish_Academy_of_Sciences "Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences") and of the [French Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Academy_of_Sciences "French Academy of Sciences"). Notable students of Euler in Berlin included [Stepan Rumovsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stepan_Rumovsky "Stepan Rumovsky"), later considered as the first Russian astronomer. In 1748 he declined an offer from the University of Basel to succeed the recently deceased Johann Bernoulli. In 1753 he bought a house in [Charlottenburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlottenburg "Charlottenburg"), in which he lived with his family and widowed mother.

Euler became the tutor for [Friederike Charlotte of Brandenburg-Schwedt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friederike_Charlotte_of_Brandenburg-Schwedt "Friederike Charlotte of Brandenburg-Schwedt"), the Princess of [Anhalt-Dessau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anhalt-Dessau "Anhalt-Dessau") and Frederick's niece. He wrote over 200 letters to her in the early 1760s, which were later compiled into a volume entitled *[Letters of Euler on different Subjects in Natural Philosophy Addressed to a German Princess](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Letters_to_a_German_Princess "Letters to a German Princess")*. This work contained Euler's exposition on various subjects pertaining to physics and mathematics and offered valuable insights into Euler's personality and religious beliefs. It was translated into multiple languages, published across Europe and in the United States, and became more widely read than any of his mathematical works. The popularity of the *Letters* testifies to Euler's ability to communicate scientific matters effectively to a lay audience, a rare ability for a dedicated research scientist.

Despite Euler's immense contribution to the academy's prestige and having been put forward as a candidate for its presidency by [Jean le Rond d'Alembert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_le_Rond_d'Alembert "Jean le Rond d'Alembert"), [Frederick II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_II_of_Prussia "Frederick II of Prussia") named himself as its president. The Prussian king had a large circle of intellectuals in his court, and he found the mathematician unsophisticated and ill-informed on matters beyond numbers and figures. Euler was a simple, devoutly religious man who never questioned the existing social order or conventional beliefs. He was, in many ways, the polar opposite of [Voltaire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voltaire "Voltaire"), who enjoyed a high place of prestige at Frederick's court. Euler was not a skilled debater and often made it a point to argue subjects that he knew little about, making him the frequent target of Voltaire's wit. Frederick also expressed disappointment with Euler's practical engineering abilities, stating:

> I wanted to have a water jet in my garden: Euler calculated the force of the wheels necessary to raise the water to a reservoir, from where it should fall back through channels, finally spurting out in [Sanssouci](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanssouci "Sanssouci"). My mill was carried out geometrically and could not raise a mouthful of water closer than fifty paces to the reservoir. Vanity of vanities! Vanity of geometry!

However, the disappointment was almost surely unwarranted from a technical perspective. Euler's calculations look likely to be correct, even if Euler's interactions with Frederick and those constructing his fountain may have been dysfunctional.

Throughout his stay in Berlin, Euler maintained a strong connection to the academy in St. Petersburg and also published 109 papers in Russia. He also assisted students from the St. Petersburg academy and at times accommodated Russian students in his house in Berlin. In 1760, with the [Seven Years' War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seven_Years'_War "Seven Years' War") raging, Euler's farm in Charlottenburg was sacked by advancing Russian troops. Upon learning of this event, [General Ivan Petrovich Saltykov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivan_Saltykov "Ivan Saltykov") paid compensation for the damage caused to Euler's estate, with [Empress Elizabeth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empress_Elizabeth "Empress Elizabeth") of Russia later adding a further payment of 4000 rubles—an exorbitant amount at the time. Euler decided to leave Berlin in 1766 and return to Russia.

During his Berlin years (1741–1766), Euler was at the peak of his productivity. He wrote 380 works, 275 of which were published. This included 125 memoirs in the Berlin Academy and over 100 memoirs sent to the [St. Petersburg Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Academy_of_Sciences "Russian Academy of Sciences"), which had retained him as a member and paid him an annual stipend. Euler's *Introductio in Analysin Infinitorum* was published in two parts in 1748. In addition to his own research, Euler supervised the library, the observatory, the botanical garden, and the publication of calendars and maps from which the academy derived income. He was even involved in the design of the water fountains at [Sanssouci](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanssouci "Sanssouci"), the King's summer palace.

### Second Saint Petersburg period (1766–1783)

The political situation in Russia stabilized after [Catherine the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catherine_the_Great "Catherine the Great")'s accession to the throne, so in 1766 Euler accepted an invitation to return to the St. Petersburg Academy. His conditions were quite exorbitant—a 3000 ruble annual salary, a pension for his wife, and the promise of high-ranking appointments for his sons. At the university he was assisted by his student [Anders Johan Lexell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anders_Johan_Lexell "Anders Johan Lexell"). While living in St. Petersburg, a fire in 1771 destroyed his home.

## Personal life

On 7 January 1734, Euler married Katharina Gsell, daughter of [Georg Gsell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georg_Gsell "Georg Gsell"), a painter at the Academy Gymnasium in Saint Petersburg. The couple bought a house by the [Neva River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neva_River "Neva River"). Of their 13 children, five survived childhood, three sons and two daughters. Their first son was [Johann Albrecht Euler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Euler "Johann Euler"), whose godfather was [Christian Goldbach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_Goldbach "Christian Goldbach"). His brother Johann Heinrich settled in St. Petersburg in 1735 and was employed as a painter at the academy. Three years after his wife's death in 1773, Euler married her half-sister, Salome Abigail Gsell. This marriage lasted until his death in 1783.

Early in his life, Euler memorized [Virgil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virgil "Virgil")'s *[Aeneid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeneid "Aeneid")*, and by old age, he could recite the poem and give the first and last sentence on each page of the edition from which he had learnt it. Euler knew the first hundred prime numbers and could give each of their powers up to the sixth degree. Euler was known as a generous and kind person, not neurotic as seen in some geniuses, keeping his good-natured disposition even after becoming entirely blind.

### Eyesight deterioration

Euler's eyesight worsened throughout his mathematical career. In 1738, three years after nearly dying of fever, he became almost blind in his right eye. Euler blamed the [cartography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartography "Cartography") he performed for the St. Petersburg Academy for his condition, but the cause of his blindness remains the subject of speculation. Euler's vision in that eye worsened throughout his stay in Germany, to the extent that Frederick called him "[Cyclops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclopes "Cyclopes")". Euler said of his loss of vision, "Now I will have fewer distractions." In 1766 a [cataract](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cataract "Cataract") in his left eye was discovered. Though [couching of the cataract](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Couching_(ophthalmology) "Couching (ophthalmology)") temporarily improved his vision, complications rendered him almost totally blind in the left eye as well. His condition appeared to have little effect on his productivity. With the aid of his scribes, Euler's productivity in many areas of study increased; in 1775, he produced, on average, one mathematical paper per week.

### Death

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c5/Euler_Grave_at_Alexander_Nevsky_Monastry.jpg/250px-Euler_Grave_at_Alexander_Nevsky_Monastry.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Euler_Grave_at_Alexander_Nevsky_Monastry.jpg)

Euler's grave at the [Alexander Nevsky Monastery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Nevsky_Lavra "Alexander Nevsky Lavra")

In St. Petersburg on 18 September 1783, after a lunch with his family, Euler was discussing the newly discovered planet [Uranus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranus "Uranus") and its orbit with [Anders Johan Lexell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anders_Johan_Lexell "Anders Johan Lexell") when he collapsed and died of a [brain hemorrhage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brain_hemorrhage "Brain hemorrhage"). [Jacob von Staehlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacob_von_Staehlin?action=edit&redlink=1 "Jacob von Staehlin") wrote a short obituary for the [Russian Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Academy_of_Sciences "Russian Academy of Sciences") and Russian mathematician [Nicolas Fuss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolas_Fuss "Nicolas Fuss"), one of Euler's disciples, wrote a more detailed eulogy, which he delivered at a memorial meeting. In his eulogy for the [French Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Academy_of_Sciences "French Academy of Sciences"), French mathematician and philosopher [Marquis de Condorcet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marquis_de_Condorcet "Marquis de Condorcet") wrote:

> *... il cessa de calculer et de vivre.*
>
> ... he ceased to calculate and to live.

Euler was buried next to Katharina at the [Smolensk Lutheran Cemetery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smolensk_Lutheran_Cemetery "Smolensk Lutheran Cemetery") on [Vasilievsky Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vasilievsky_Island "Vasilievsky Island"). In 1837, the [Russian Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Academy_of_Sciences "Russian Academy of Sciences") installed a new monument, replacing his overgrown grave plaque. In 1957, to commemorate the 250th anniversary of his birth, his tomb was moved to the [Lazarevskoe Cemetery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lazarevskoe_Cemetery "Lazarevskoe Cemetery") at the [Alexander Nevsky Monastery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Nevsky_Monastery "Alexander Nevsky Monastery").

Euler worked in almost all areas of mathematics, including [geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometry "Geometry"), [infinitesimal calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinitesimal_calculus "Infinitesimal calculus"), [trigonometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigonometry "Trigonometry"), [algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebra "Algebra"), and [number theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_theory "Number theory"), as well as [continuum physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuum_physics "Continuum physics"), [lunar theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_theory "Lunar theory"), and other areas of [physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics "Physics"). He is a seminal figure in the history of mathematics; if printed, his works, many of which are of fundamental interest, would occupy between 60 and 80 [quarto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quarto_(text) "Quarto (text)") volumes. Euler's name is associated with a [large number of topics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_topics_named_after_Leonhard_Euler "List of topics named after Leonhard Euler"). Euler's work averages 800 pages a year from 1725 to 1783. He also wrote over 4500 letters and hundreds of manuscripts. It has been estimated that Leonhard Euler was the author of a quarter of the combined output in mathematics, physics, mechanics, astronomy, and navigation in the 18th century, while other researchers credit Euler for a third of the output in mathematics in that century.

### Mathematical notation

Euler introduced and popularized several notational conventions through his numerous and widely circulated textbooks. Most notably, he introduced the concept of a [function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Function_(mathematics) "Function (mathematics)") and was the first to write *f*(*x*) to denote the function *f* applied to the argument *x*. He also introduced the modern notation for the [trigonometric functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigonometric_functions "Trigonometric functions"), the letter *e* for the base of the [natural logarithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_logarithm "Natural logarithm") (now also known as [Euler's number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler's_number "Euler's number")), the Greek letter [Σ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigma "Sigma") for summations and the letter *i* to denote the [imaginary unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imaginary_unit "Imaginary unit"). The use of the Greek letter *[π](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pi_(letter) "Pi (letter)")* to denote the [ratio of a circle's circumference to its diameter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pi "Pi") was also popularized by Euler, although it originated with [Welsh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh_people "Welsh people") mathematician [William Jones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Jones_(mathematician) "William Jones (mathematician)").

### Analysis

The development of [infinitesimal calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinitesimal_calculus "Infinitesimal calculus") was at the forefront of 18th-century mathematical research, and the [Bernoullis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernoulli_family "Bernoulli family")—family friends of Euler—were responsible for much of the early progress in the field. Thanks to their influence, studying calculus became the major focus of Euler's work. While some of Euler's proofs are not acceptable by modern standards of [mathematical rigour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_rigor "Mathematical rigor") (in particular his reliance on the principle of the [generality of algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generality_of_algebra "Generality of algebra")), his ideas led to many great advances.

Euler pioneered the use of analytic methods to solve number theory problems. For example, he proved the [infinitude of primes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinitude_of_primes "Infinitude of primes") using the divergence of the [harmonic series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harmonic_series_(mathematics) "Harmonic series (mathematics)"), and he used analytic methods to gain some understanding of the way [prime numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_numbers "Prime numbers") are distributed. Euler's work in this area led to the development of the [prime number theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_number_theorem "Prime number theorem").

### Number theory

Euler's interest in number theory can be traced to the influence of [Christian Goldbach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_Goldbach "Christian Goldbach"), his friend in the St. Petersburg Academy. Much of Euler's early work on number theory was based on the work of [Pierre de Fermat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_de_Fermat "Pierre de Fermat"). Euler developed some of Fermat's ideas and disproved some of his conjectures, such as his conjecture that all numbers of the form

Euler linked the nature of prime distribution with ideas in analysis. He proved that [the sum of the reciprocals of the primes diverges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proof_that_the_sum_of_the_reciprocals_of_the_primes_diverges "Proof that the sum of the reciprocals of the primes diverges"). In doing so, he discovered the connection between the [Riemann zeta function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann_zeta_function "Riemann zeta function") and prime numbers; this is known as the [Euler product formula for the Riemann zeta function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proof_of_the_Euler_product_formula_for_the_Riemann_zeta_function "Proof of the Euler product formula for the Riemann zeta function").

Euler invented the [totient function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Totient_function "Totient function") φ(*n*), the number of positive integers less than or equal to the integer *n* that are [coprime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coprime "Coprime") to *n*. Using properties of this function, he generalized [Fermat's little theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermat's_little_theorem "Fermat's little theorem") to what is now known as [Euler's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler's_theorem "Euler's theorem"). He contributed significantly to the theory of [perfect numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perfect_number "Perfect number"), which had fascinated mathematicians since [Euclid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclid "Euclid"). He proved that the relationship shown between even perfect numbers and [Mersenne primes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_prime "Mersenne prime") (which he had earlier proved) was one-to-one, a result otherwise known as the [Euclid–Euler theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclid–Euler_theorem "Euclid–Euler theorem"). Euler also conjectured the law of [quadratic reciprocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_reciprocity "Quadratic reciprocity"). The concept is regarded as a fundamental theorem within number theory, and his ideas paved the way for the work of [Carl Friedrich Gauss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Friedrich_Gauss "Carl Friedrich Gauss"), particularly *[Disquisitiones Arithmeticae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disquisitiones_Arithmeticae "Disquisitiones Arithmeticae")*. By 1772 Euler had proved that 231 − 1 = [2,147,483,647](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2147483647 "2147483647") is a Mersenne prime. It may have remained the [largest known prime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Largest_known_prime "Largest known prime") until 1867.

Euler also contributed major developments to the theory of [partitions of an integer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partitions_of_an_integer "Partitions of an integer").

### Graph theory

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/5/5d/Konigsberg_bridges.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail_unscaled)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Konigsberg_bridges.png)

Map of [Königsberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Königsberg "Königsberg") in Euler's time showing the actual layout of the [seven bridges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seven_Bridges_of_Königsberg "Seven Bridges of Königsberg"), highlighting the river [Pregel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pregolya "Pregolya") and the bridges

In 1735, Euler presented a solution to the problem known as the [Seven Bridges of Königsberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seven_Bridges_of_Königsberg "Seven Bridges of Königsberg"). The city of [Königsberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Königsberg "Königsberg"), [Prussia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Prussia "Kingdom of Prussia") was set on the [Pregel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pregolya "Pregolya") River, and included two large islands that were connected to each other and the mainland by seven bridges. The problem is to decide whether it is possible to follow a path that crosses each bridge exactly once. Euler showed that it is not possible: there is no [Eulerian path](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eulerian_path "Eulerian path"). This solution is considered to be the first theorem of [graph theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graph_theory "Graph theory").

Some of Euler's greatest successes were in solving real-world problems analytically, and in describing numerous applications of the [Bernoulli numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernoulli_numbers "Bernoulli numbers"), [Fourier series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourier_series "Fourier series"), [Euler numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler_number "Euler number"), the constants [e](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E_(mathematical_constant) "E (mathematical constant)") and [π](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pi "Pi"), continued fractions, and integrals. He integrated [Leibniz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gottfried_Leibniz "Gottfried Leibniz")'s [differential calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_calculus "Differential calculus") with Newton's [Method of Fluxions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Method_of_Fluxions "Method of Fluxions"), and developed tools that made it easier to apply calculus to physical problems. He made great strides in improving the [numerical approximation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numerical_approximation "Numerical approximation") of integrals, inventing what are now known as the [Euler approximations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler_approximations "Euler approximations"). The most notable of these approximations are [Euler's method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler's_method "Euler's method") and the [Euler–Maclaurin formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler–Maclaurin_formula "Euler–Maclaurin formula").

Euler helped develop the [Euler–Bernoulli beam equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler–Bernoulli_beam_equation "Euler–Bernoulli beam equation"), which became a cornerstone of engineering. Besides successfully applying his analytic tools to problems in [classical mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_mechanics "Classical mechanics"), Euler applied these techniques to celestial problems. His work in astronomy was recognized by multiple [Paris Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Academy_of_Sciences "French Academy of Sciences") Prizes over the course of his career. His accomplishments include determining with great accuracy the [orbits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit "Orbit") of [comets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comet "Comet") and other celestial bodies, understanding the nature of comets, and calculating the [parallax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_parallax "Solar parallax") of the Sun. His calculations contributed to the development of accurate [longitude tables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_longitude "History of longitude").

Euler made important contributions in [optics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optics "Optics"). He disagreed with Newton's [corpuscular theory of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corpuscular_theory_of_light "Corpuscular theory of light"), which was the prevailing theory of the time. His 1740s papers on optics helped ensure that the [wave theory of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_theory_of_light "Wave theory of light") proposed by [Christiaan Huygens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christiaan_Huygens "Christiaan Huygens") would become the dominant mode of thought, at least until the development of the [quantum theory of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave-particle_duality "Wave-particle duality").

In [fluid dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluid_dynamics "Fluid dynamics"), Euler was the first to predict the phenomenon of [cavitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cavitation "Cavitation"), in 1754, long before its first observation in the late 19th century, and the [Euler number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler_number_(physics) "Euler number (physics)") used in fluid flow calculations comes from his related work on the efficiency of [turbines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turbine "Turbine"). In 1757 he published an important set of equations for [inviscid flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inviscid_flow "Inviscid flow") in [fluid dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluid_dynamics "Fluid dynamics"), that are now known as the [Euler equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler_equations_(fluid_dynamics) "Euler equations (fluid dynamics)").

Euler is well known in [structural engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structural_engineering "Structural engineering") for his formula giving [Euler's critical load](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler's_critical_load "Euler's critical load"), the critical [buckling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buckling "Buckling") load of an ideal strut, which depends only on its length and [flexural stiffness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flexural_rigidity "Flexural rigidity").

### Logic

Euler is credited with using [closed curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_curve "Closed curve") to illustrate [syllogistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syllogism "Syllogism") reasoning (1768). These diagrams have become known as [Euler diagrams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler_diagram "Euler diagram").

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/07/Euler_Diagram.svg/250px-Euler_Diagram.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Euler_Diagram.svg)

An Euler diagram

An Euler diagram is a [diagrammatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diagram "Diagram") means of representing [sets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Set_(mathematics) "Set (mathematics)") and their relationships. Euler diagrams consist of simple closed curves (usually circles) in the plane that depict [sets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Set_(mathematics) "Set (mathematics)"). Each Euler curve divides the plane into two regions or "zones": the interior, which symbolically represents the [elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Element_(mathematics) "Element (mathematics)") of the set, and the exterior, which represents all elements that are not members of the set. The sizes or shapes of the curves are not important; the significance of the diagram is in how they overlap. The spatial relationships between the regions bounded by each curve (overlap, containment or neither) corresponds to set-theoretic relationships ([intersection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intersection_(set_theory) "Intersection (set theory)"), [subset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subset "Subset"), and [disjointness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disjoint_sets "Disjoint sets")). Curves whose interior zones do not intersect represent [disjoint sets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disjoint_sets "Disjoint sets"). Two curves whose interior zones intersect represent sets that have common elements; the zone inside both curves represents the set of elements common to both sets (the [intersection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intersection_(set_theory) "Intersection (set theory)") of the sets). A curve that is contained completely within the interior zone of another represents a [subset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subset "Subset") of it.

Euler diagrams (and their refinement to [Venn diagrams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venn_diagram "Venn diagram")) were incorporated as part of instruction in [set theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Set_theory "Set theory") as part of the [new math](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_math "New math") movement in the 1960s. Since then, they have come into wide use as a way of visualizing combinations of characteristics.

### Demography

In his 1760 paper *A General Investigation into the Mortality and Multiplication of the Human Species* Euler produced a model which showed how a population with constant fertility and mortality might grow geometrically using a difference equation. Under this geometric growth Euler also examined relationships among various demographic indices showing how they might be used to produce estimates when observations were missing. Three papers published around 150 years later by [Alfred J. Lotka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_J._Lotka "Alfred J. Lotka") (1907, 1911 (with F.R. Sharpe) and 1922) adopted a similar approach to Euler's and produced their Stable Population Model. These marked the start of 20th century formal demographic modelling.

### Music

One of Euler's more unusual interests was the application of [mathematical ideas in music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_and_mathematics "Music and mathematics"). In 1739 he wrote the *Tentamen novae theoriae musicae* (*Attempt at a New Theory of Music*), hoping to eventually incorporate [music theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_theory "Music theory") as part of mathematics. This part of his work, however, did not receive wide attention and was once described as too mathematical for musicians and too musical for mathematicians. Even when dealing with music, Euler's approach is mainly mathematical, for instance, his introduction of [binary logarithms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_logarithm "Binary logarithm") as a way of numerically describing the subdivision of [octaves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octave "Octave") into fractional parts. His writings on music are not particularly numerous (a few hundred pages, in his total production of about thirty thousand pages), but they reflect an early preoccupation and one that remained with him throughout his life.

A first point of Euler's musical theory is the definition of "genres", i.e. of possible divisions of the octave using the prime numbers 3 and 5. Euler describes 18 such genres, with the general definition 2mA, where A is the "exponent" of the genre (i.e. the sum of the exponents of 3 and 5) and 2m (where "m is an indefinite number, small or large, so long as the sounds are perceptible"), expresses that the relation holds independently of the number of octaves concerned. The first genre, with A = 1, is the octave itself (or its duplicates); the second genre, 2m.3, is the octave divided by the fifth (fifth + fourth, C–G–C); the third genre is 2m.5, major third + minor sixth (C–E–C); the fourth is 2m.32, two-fourths and a tone (C–F–B♭–C); the fifth is 2m.3.5 (C–E–G–B–C); etc. Genres 12 (2m.33.5), 13 (2m.32.52) and 14 (2m.3.53) are corrected versions of the [diatonic, chromatic and enharmonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genus_(music) "Genus (music)"), respectively, of the Ancients. Genre 18 (2m.33.52) is the "diatonico-chromatic", "used generally in all compositions", and which turns out to be identical with the system described by [Johann Mattheson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Mattheson "Johann Mattheson"). Euler later envisaged the possibility of describing genres including the prime number 7.

Euler devised a specific graph, the *Speculum musicum*, to illustrate the diatonico-chromatic genre, and discussed paths in this graph for specific intervals, recalling his interest in the Seven Bridges of Königsberg (see [above](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonhard_Euler#Graph_theory)). The device drew renewed interest as the [Tonnetz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tonnetz "Tonnetz") in [Neo-Riemannian theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-Riemannian_theory "Neo-Riemannian theory") (see also [Lattice (music)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lattice_(music) "Lattice (music)")).

Euler further used the principle of the "exponent" to propose a derivation of the *gradus suavitatis* (degree of suavity, of agreeableness) of intervals and chords from their prime factors – one must keep in mind that he considered [just intonation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Just_intonation "Just intonation"), i.e. 1 and only the prime numbers 3 and 5. ## Personal philosophy and religious beliefs

Euler was religious throughout his life. Much of what is known of his religious beliefs can be deduced from his *[Letters to a German Princess](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Letters_to_a_German_Princess "Letters to a German Princess")* and an earlier work, *Rettung der Göttlichen Offenbahrung gegen die Einwürfe der Freygeister* (*Defense of the Divine Revelation against the Objections of the Freethinkers*). These show that Euler was a devout Christian who believed the Bible to be inspired; the *Rettung* was primarily an argument for the [divine inspiration of scripture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biblical_inspiration "Biblical inspiration").

Euler opposed the concepts of [Leibniz's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gottfried_Leibniz "Gottfried Leibniz") [monadism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monadism "Monadism") and the philosophy of [Christian Wolff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_Wolff_(philosopher) "Christian Wolff (philosopher)"). He insisted that knowledge is founded in part on the basis of precise quantitative laws, something that monadism and Wolffian science were unable to provide. Euler called Wolff's ideas "heathen and atheistic".

There is a legend inspired by Euler's arguments with secular philosophers over religion, which is set during Euler's second stint at the St. Petersburg Academy. The French philosopher [Denis Diderot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denis_Diderot "Denis Diderot") was visiting Russia on Catherine the Great's invitation. The Empress was alarmed that Diderot's arguments for atheism were influencing members of her court, and so Euler was asked to confront him. Diderot was informed that a learned mathematician had produced a proof of the [existence of God](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Existence_of_God "Existence of God"): he agreed to view the proof as it was presented in court. ## Legacy

### Recognition

Euler is widely recognized as one of the greatest mathematicians of all time, and more likely than not the most prolific contributor to mathematics and science. Mathematician and physicist [John von Neumann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_von_Neumann "John von Neumann") called Euler "the greatest [virtuoso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virtuoso "Virtuoso") of the period". Mathematician [François Arago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/François_Arago "François Arago") said, "Euler calculated without any apparent effort, just as men breathe and as eagles sustain themselves in air". He is generally ranked right below [Carl Friedrich Gauss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Friedrich_Gauss "Carl Friedrich Gauss"), [Isaac Newton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Newton "Isaac Newton"), and [Archimedes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archimedes "Archimedes") among the greatest mathematicians of all time, while some rank him as equal with them. Physicist and mathematician [Henri Poincaré](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_Poincaré "Henri Poincaré") called Euler the "god of mathematics".

French mathematician [André Weil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/André_Weil "André Weil") noted that Euler stood above his contemporaries and more than anyone else was able to cement himself as the leading force of his era's mathematics:

> No mathematician ever attained such a position of undisputed leadership in all branches of mathematics, pure and applied, as Euler did for the best part of the eighteenth century.

Swiss mathematician [Nicolas Fuss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolas_Fuss "Nicolas Fuss") noted Euler's extraordinary memory and breadth of knowledge, saying:

> Knowledge that we call erudition was not inimical to him. He had read all the best Roman writers, knew perfectly the ancient history of mathematics, held in his memory the historical events of all times and peoples, and could without hesitation adduce by way of examples the most trifling of historical events. He knew more about medicine, botany, and chemistry than might be expected of someone who had not worked especially in those sciences.

Euler was featured on both the sixth and seventh series of the Swiss 10-[franc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swiss_franc "Swiss franc") banknote and on numerous Swiss, German, and Russian postage stamps. In 1782 he was elected a Foreign Honorary Member of the [American Academy of Arts and Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Academy_of_Arts_and_Sciences "American Academy of Arts and Sciences"). The [asteroid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asteroid "Asteroid") [2002 Euler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2002_Euler "2002 Euler") was named in his honour.

## Selected bibliography

Euler has [an extensive bibliography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contributions_of_Leonhard_Euler_to_mathematics#Works "Contributions of Leonhard Euler to mathematics"). His books include:

- *[Mechanica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanica "Mechanica")* (1736)
- [*Methodus inveniendi lineas curvas maximi minimive proprietate gaudentes, sive solutio problematis isoperimetrici latissimo sensu accepti* (1744)](https://www.loc.gov/item/04028085/) (*A method for finding curved lines enjoying properties of maximum or minimum, or solution of isoperimetric problems in the broadest accepted sense*)
- *[Introductio in analysin infinitorum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Introductio_in_analysin_infinitorum "Introductio in analysin infinitorum")* (1748) (*Introduction to Analysis of the Infinite*)
- *[Institutiones calculi differentialis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institutiones_calculi_differentialis "Institutiones calculi differentialis")* (1755) (*Foundations of differential calculus*)
- *[Vollständige Anleitung zur Algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elements_of_Algebra "Elements of Algebra")* (1765) (*Elements of Algebra*)
- *[Institutiones calculi integralis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institutiones_calculi_integralis "Institutiones calculi integralis")* (1768–1770) (*Foundations of integral calculus*)
- *[Letters to a German Princess](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Letters_to_a_German_Princess "Letters to a German Princess")* (1768–1772)
- *Dioptrica*, published in three volumes beginning in 1769

It took until 1830 for the bulk of Euler's posthumous works to be individually published, with an additional batch of 61 unpublished works discovered by [Paul Heinrich von Fuss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Heinrich_von_Fuss "Paul Heinrich von Fuss") (Euler's great-grandson and [Nicolas Fuss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolas_Fuss "Nicolas Fuss")'s son) and published as a collection in 1862. A chronological catalog of Euler's works was compiled by Swedish mathematician [Gustaf Eneström](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gustaf_Eneström "Gustaf Eneström") and published from 1910 to 1913. The catalog, known as the **Eneström index**, numbers Euler's works from E1 to E866. The Euler Archive was started at [Dartmouth College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dartmouth_College "Dartmouth College") before moving to the [Mathematical Association of America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_Association_of_America "Mathematical Association of America") and, most recently, to [University of the Pacific](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_the_Pacific_(United_States) "University of the Pacific (United States)") in 2017.

In 1907, the [Swiss Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swiss_Academies_of_Arts_and_Sciences "Swiss Academies of Arts and Sciences") created the [Euler Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler_Commission "Euler Commission") and charged it with the publication of Euler's complete works. After several delays in the 19th century, the first volume of the *[Opera Omnia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opera_Omnia_Leonhard_Euler "Opera Omnia Leonhard Euler")*, was published in 1911. However, the discovery of new manuscripts continued to increase the magnitude of this project. Fortunately, the publication of Euler's Opera Omnia has made steady progress, with over 70 volumes (averaging 426 pages each) published by 2006 and 80 volumes published by 2022. These volumes are organized into four series. The first series compiles the works on analysis, algebra, and number theory; it consists of 29 volumes and numbers over 14,000 pages. The 31 volumes of Series II, amounting to 10,660 pages, contain the works on mechanics, astronomy, and engineering. Series III contains 12 volumes on physics. Series IV, which contains the massive amount of Euler's correspondence, unpublished manuscripts, and notes only began compilation in 1967. After publishing 8 print volumes in Series IV, the project decided in 2022 to publish its remaining projected volumes in Series IV in online format only.
