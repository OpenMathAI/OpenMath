**Richard of Wallingford** (1292–1336) was an English mathematician, astronomer, horologist, and cleric who made major contributions to [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy "Astronomy") and [horology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horology "Horology") while serving as [abbot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbot "Abbot") of [St Albans Abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Albans_Abbey "St Albans Abbey") in Hertfordshire.

## Biography

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/64/Clock_of_St._Alban%27s_Abbey.jpg/250px-Clock_of_St._Alban%27s_Abbey.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Clock_of_St._Alban's_Abbey.jpg)

Reconstructed clock of St. Alban's Abbey, originally built circa 1356.

Richard was born, the son of a [blacksmith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blacksmith "Blacksmith"), at [Wallingford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wallingford,_Oxfordshire "Wallingford, Oxfordshire") in Berkshire (now [Oxfordshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxfordshire "Oxfordshire")) in England, in 1292. When he was [orphaned](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orphan "Orphan") he was taken to [William de Kirkeby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_de_Kirkeby "William de Kirkeby") the [Prior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prior_(ecclesiastical) "Prior (ecclesiastical)") of Wallingford Priory and dedicated to the Holy Trinity. Wallingford was a dependent priory to St Albans Abbey. Richard subsequently spent six years studying at [Oxford University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford_University "Oxford University") before becoming a monk at [St Albans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Albans "St Albans"). He later studied for nine more years at Oxford. In 1327 he became [abbot of St Albans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbot_of_St_Albans "Abbot of St Albans").

Richard is best known for the [astronomical clock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_clock "Astronomical clock") he designed, while he was abbot, which is described in the *Tractatus Horologii Astronomici* (1327). The clock was completed about 20 years after Richard's death by William of Walsham, but was apparently destroyed during [Henry VIII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_VIII_of_England "Henry VIII of England")'s [reformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Reformation "English Reformation") and the [dissolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissolution_of_the_monasteries "Dissolution of the monasteries") of [St Albans Abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Albans_Abbey "St Albans Abbey") in 1539. His clock almost certainly was the most complex [clock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clock "Clock") mechanism in existence at the time in the [British Isles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Isles "British Isles"), and one of the most sophisticated ones anywhere. The only other clocklike mechanism of comparable complexity that is documented in the 14th century is the [astrarium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrarium "Astrarium") by [Giovanni de Dondi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giovanni_Dondi_dell'Orologio "Giovanni Dondi dell'Orologio"). Richard’s clock gave the mean time in equal and unequal hours, as well as the true solar time. It also displayed the phases of the moon and showed the positions of the lunar nodes and the height of the tide at [London Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Bridge "London Bridge").

Based on the 14th-century literary evidence still surviving in the 20th century, scholars of [horological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horological "Horological") history have tried to build recreations of Richard of Wallingford's clock. The best known of these was built by Haward Horological and for many years was displayed at the Time Museum (now defunct) in [Rockford, Illinois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rockford,_Illinois "Rockford, Illinois"); it is currently on display at the Halim Time and Glass Museum in [Evanston, Illinois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evanston,_Illinois "Evanston, Illinois"). One was built by Eric Watson and is now in the [Wallingford Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wallingford_Museum "Wallingford Museum"); one built in 1988 is located at St Albans Cathedral; and one was built by Don Unwin for the [Whipple Museum of the History of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whipple_Museum_of_the_History_of_Science "Whipple Museum of the History of Science") in Cambridge.

Richard suffered from what was then thought to be [leprosy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leprosy "Leprosy") (though it might have been [scrofula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scrofula "Scrofula") or [tuberculosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuberculosis "Tuberculosis")) which he apparently contracted when he went to have his position, as abbot of St Albans Abbey, confirmed by the Pope at [Avignon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avignon "Avignon"). He died at St Albans in 1336.

## Studies in astronomy and mathematics

Richard also designed and constructed calculation devices: a [torquetum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torquetum "Torquetum"), the *[Rectangulus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rectangulus "Rectangulus")*, and an [equatorium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equatorium "Equatorium"), which he called *Albion*. The Albion could be used for astronomical calculations such as [lunar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moon "Moon"), [solar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun "Sun") and [planetary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planet "Planet") [longitudes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longitude "Longitude") and could predict [eclipses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eclipse "Eclipse"), and was capable of doing this without relying on a set of tables that had to be copied out. This is described in the *Tractatus Albionis*. He published other works on [trigonometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigonometry "Trigonometry"), celestial coordinates, astrology, and various religious works.
