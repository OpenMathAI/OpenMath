**Jean Buridan** ([/ˈbjʊərɪdən/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English"); French: [[byʁidɑ̃]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/French "Help:IPA/French"); [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin "Latin"): *Johannes Buridanus*; c. 1301 – c. 1359/62) was an influential 14th‑century French [scholastic philosopher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scholastic_philosopher "Scholastic philosopher").

Buridan taught in the [faculty of arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faculty_(division)#Faculty_of_Arts "Faculty (division)") at the [University of Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Paris "University of Paris") for his entire career and focused in particular on [logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logic "Logic") and on the works of [Aristotle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotle "Aristotle"). Buridan sowed the seeds of the [Copernican Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copernican_Revolution "Copernican Revolution") in Europe. He developed the concept of  [impetus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theory_of_impetus "Theory of impetus"), the first step toward the modern concept of [inertia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inertia "Inertia") and an important development in the [history of medieval science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_science_in_the_Middle_Ages "History of science in the Middle Ages"). His name is most familiar through the [thought experiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thought_experiment "Thought experiment") known as [Buridan's ass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buridan's_ass "Buridan's ass"), but the thought experiment does not appear in his extant writings.

## Life

### Education and career

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/96/Aristotelis_De_anima.jpg/250px-Aristotelis_De_anima.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Aristotelis_De_anima.jpg)

14th-century manuscript of Buridan's *Questions on Aristotle's* *[De anima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_anima "De anima")*.

Buridan was born sometime before 1301, perhaps at or near the town of [Béthune](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Béthune "Béthune") in [Picardy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Picardy "Picardy"), [France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/France "France"), or perhaps elsewhere in the diocese of Arras. He received his education in Paris, first at the [Collège du Cardinal Lemoine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collège_du_Cardinal_Lemoine "Collège du Cardinal Lemoine") and then at the [University of Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Paris "University of Paris"), receiving his [Master of Arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master_of_Arts "Master of Arts") degree and formal license to teach at the latter by the mid-1320s.

Unusually, he spent his entire academic life in the faculty of arts, rather than obtaining the doctorate in [law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law "Law"), [medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medicine "Medicine") or [theology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theology "Theology") that typically prepared the way for a career in [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy "Philosophy"). Also unusual for a philosopher of his time, Buridan further maintained his intellectual independence by remaining a [secular cleric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secular_clergy "Secular clergy"), rather than joining a [religious order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_order "Religious order"). A papal letter of 1330 refers to him as simply, "*clericus Atrebatensis diocoesis, magister in artibus* [a cleric from the Diocese of Arras and Master of Arts]." As university statutes permitted only those educated in theology to teach or write on the subject, there are no writings from Buridan on either theological matters or commentary of [Peter Lombard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Lombard "Peter Lombard")'s *[Sentences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sentences "Sentences")*.

Speculation on his reasons for avoiding religious matters have remained uncertain. Most scholars think it is unlikely that he went unnoticed, given his philosophical talents. As well, it is unlikely that he could not afford to study theology, given that he received several bursaries and stipends. Indeed, he is listed in a document from 1350 as being among the teachers capable of supporting themselves without the need for financial assistance from the University. John Alexander Zupko has speculated that Buridan "deliberately chose to remain among the 'artists [*artistae*]'," possibly envisioning philosophy as a secular enterprise based on what is evident to both the senses and the intellect, rather than the non-evident truths of theology revealed through scripture and doctrine.

The last appearance of Buridan in historical documents came in 1359, where he was mentioned as the adjudicator in a territorial dispute between the [English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/England "England") and Picard nations. It is supposed that he died sometime after then, since one of his benefices was awarded to another person in 1362.

The [bishop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bishop "Bishop") [Albert of Saxony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_of_Saxony_(philosopher) "Albert of Saxony (philosopher)"), himself renowned as a [logician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logician "Logician"), was among the most notable of his students.

An ordinance of [Louis XI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_XI "Louis XI") in 1473, directed against the [nominalists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nominalism "Nominalism"), prohibited the reading of his works.

Apocryphal stories abound about his reputed amorous affairs and adventures which are enough to show that he enjoyed a reputation as a glamorous and mysterious figure in Paris life. None of the stories can be confirmed, and most contradict known historical information.

Some rumors hold that he died when the King of France had him put in a sack and thrown into the River [Seine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seine "Seine") after his affair with the Queen came to light. [François Villon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/François_Villon "François Villon") alludes to this in his famous poem *[Ballade des Dames du Temps Jadis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ballade_des_Dames_du_Temps_Jadis "Ballade des Dames du Temps Jadis")*. Others suggest that he was expelled from Paris due to his nominalist teachings and moved to [Vienna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vienna "Vienna") to found the [University of Vienna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Vienna "University of Vienna"). Another story talks of him hitting [Pope Clement VI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Clement_VI "Pope Clement VI") with a shoe.

## Impetus theory

The concept of *inertia* was alien to the physics of [Aristotle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotle "Aristotle"). Aristotle, and his [peripatetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peripatetic_school "Peripatetic school") followers held that a body was maintained in motion only by the action of a continuous external [force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force "Force"). Thus, in the Aristotelian view, a projectile moving through the air would owe its continuing motion to *eddies* or *vibrations* in the surrounding medium, a phenomenon known as *[antiperistasis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antiperistasis "Antiperistasis")*. In the absence of a proximate force, the body would come to rest almost immediately.

Buridan developed a [theory of impetus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theory_of_impetus "Theory of impetus") by which the mover imparts to the moved a power, proportional to the speed and mass, which keeps it moving. In addition, he correctly theorized that resistance of the air progressively reduces the impetus and that weight can add or detract from speed. Buridan was the first to name this motion-maintaining property *impetus* but the theory itself probably did not originate with him. A less sophisticated notion of impressed forced can be found in the commentary of [John Philoponus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Philoponus "John Philoponus") on Aristoteilan physics. In this he was possibly influenced by [John Philoponus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Philoponus "John Philoponus") who was developing the Stoic notion of *hormé* (impulse). The major difference between Buridan's theory and that of his predecessor is that he rejected the view that the impetus dissipated spontaneously, instead asserting that a body would be arrested by the forces of air resistance and [gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity "Gravity") which might be opposing its impetus. Buridan further held that the impetus of a body increased with the speed with which it was set in motion, and with its quantity of matter. This is closely related to the modern concept of [momentum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Momentum "Momentum"). Buridan saw impetus as *causing* the motion of the object:

:   *...after leaving the arm of the thrower, the projectile would be moved by an impetus given to it by the thrower and would continue to be moved as long as the impetus remained stronger than the resistance, and would be of infinite duration were it not diminished and corrupted by a contrary force resisting it or by something inclining it to a contrary motion* (*Questions on Aristotle's [Metaphysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphysics_(Aristotle) "Metaphysics (Aristotle)")* XII.9: 73ra).

Buridan also contended that impetus is a variable quality whose force is determined by the speed and quantity of the matter in the subject. In this way, the acceleration of a falling body could be understood in terms of its gradual accumulation of units of impetus.

### Legacy

Because of his developments, historians of science [Pierre Duhem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Duhem "Pierre Duhem") and [Anneliese Maier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anneliese_Maier "Anneliese Maier") both saw Buridan as playing an important role in the demise of [Aristotelian cosmology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_the_Heavens "On the Heavens"). Duhem even called Buridan the forerunner of [Galileo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galileo_Galilei "Galileo Galilei"). Zupko has disagreed, pointing out that Buridan did not use his theory to transform the science of mechanics, but instead remained a committed Aristotelian in thinking that motion and rest are contrary states and that the universe is finite in extent.
