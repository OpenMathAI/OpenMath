**Thomas Bradwardine** (c. 1300 – 26 August 1349) was an English [cleric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cleric "Cleric"), scholar, [mathematician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematician "Mathematician"), [physicist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physicist "Physicist"), courtier and, very briefly, [Archbishop of Canterbury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archbishop_of_Canterbury "Archbishop of Canterbury"). As a celebrated [scholastic philosopher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scholastic_philosopher "Scholastic philosopher") and [doctor of theology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_theology "Doctor of theology"), he is often called ***Doctor Profundus*** ([medieval epithet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval_epithet "Medieval epithet"), meaning "the Profound Doctor" or "the Profound Teacher").

## Life

Sources vary about Bradwardine's early life before receiving his degree in 1321. His exact date of birth is unknown but sources point to a date between 1290 and 1300. His place of birth is also unknown but some sources point to it being near [Chichester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chichester "Chichester"), Sussex, or [Harfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harfield?action=edit&redlink=1 "Harfield"). The first concrete sources of his do not appear until he received his degree in 1321 from [Balliol College, Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balliol_College,_Oxford "Balliol College, Oxford"). Thomas Bradwardine became a [Fellow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow "Fellow") of [Merton College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merton_College,_Oxford "Merton College, Oxford") in Oxford, and was awarded his B.A. in August 1321. Bradwardine stayed at Merton College until 1333, when he was appointed [Canon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canon_regular "Canon regular") of Lincoln, and in 1337 he was appointed the chaplain of [St Paul's Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Paul's_Cathedral "St Paul's Cathedral"). His involvement with the ecclesiastical began in September 1333, when he was made the Canon of Lincoln. It is less corroborated by sources but it is stated that Bradwardine may have been the Bishop of Durham between 1335 and 1337. It is rumoured that this move to Durham helped put him into contact with [King Edward III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_III_of_England "Edward III of England"), which would lead to his eventual appointment of Chaplain of [Old St Paul's Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_St_Paul's_Cathedral "Old St Paul's Cathedral") in London.

He acquired several degrees from Oxford; it is presumed he acquired them on these dates: B.A. by August 1321, an M.A. by 1323, a B.Th. by 1330, and a D.Th. by 1348.

Bradwardine was a precocious student, educated at Balliol College, Oxford, where he was a fellow by 1321; he took the degree of doctor of divinity, and acquired the reputation of a profound scholar, a skilful [mathematician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematician "Mathematician") and an able [theologian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theology "Theology"). He was also a gifted [logician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logician "Logician") with theories on the *[insolubles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insolubilia "Insolubilia")* and in particular the [liar paradox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liar_paradox "Liar paradox").

Bradwardine subsequently moved to [Merton College, Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merton_College,_Oxford "Merton College, Oxford") on a fellowship. He was afterwards raised to the high offices of chancellor of the university and professor of divinity. Bradwardine (like his contemporary [William of Ockham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_of_Ockham "William of Ockham")) was a culminating figure of the great intellectual movement at Oxford that had begun in the 1240s.

Bradwardine was an ordinary secular cleric, which gave him intellectual freedom but deprived him of the security and wherewithal that the Preaching Orders would have afforded; instead he turned to royal patronage. From being chancellor of the [diocese of London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diocese_of_London "Diocese of London") as [Dean of St Paul's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dean_of_St_Paul's "Dean of St Paul's"), he became chaplain and confessor to [Edward III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_III_of_England "Edward III of England"), whom he attended during his wars in France at the [Battle of Crécy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Crécy "Battle of Crécy"), where he preached at the victory Mass, and at the subsequent [siege of Calais](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Calais_(1346) "Siege of Calais (1346)"). Edward repeatedly entrusted him with diplomatic missions. On his return to England, he was successively appointed [prebendary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prebendary "Prebendary") of Lincoln and [dean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dean_of_Lincoln "Dean of Lincoln") (1348). In 1349 the canons of the chapter at Canterbury elected him Archbishop following the death of Archbishop John Stratford, but [Edward III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_III_of_England "Edward III of England") withheld his consent, preferring his chancellor [John de Ufford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_de_Ufford "John de Ufford"), perhaps loth to lose his trusted confessor. After Ufford died of the [Black Death](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Death "Black Death"), 2 May, Bradwardine went to receive confirmation from Pope [Clement VI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Clement_VI "Pope Clement VI") at Avignon, but on his return he died of the plague at [Rochester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rochester,_Kent "Rochester, Kent") on 26 August 1349, 38 days after his consecration. He was buried at Canterbury.

[Chaucer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chaucer "Chaucer") in "[The Nun's Priest's Tale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Nun's_Priest's_Tale "The Nun's Priest's Tale")" (line 476) ranks Bradwardine with [Augustine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustine_of_Hippo "Augustine of Hippo") and [Boethius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anicius_Manlius_Severinus_Boethius "Anicius Manlius Severinus Boethius"). His great theological work, to modern eyes, is a treatise against the [Pelagians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pelagians "Pelagians"), entitled *De causa Dei contra Pelagium et de virtute causarum*. Bradwardine's major treatise argued that space was an infinite void in which God could have created other worlds, which he would rule as he ruled this one. The "causes of virtue" include the [influences of the planets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrology "Astrology"), not as predestining a human career, but influencing a subject's essential nature. This astrophysical treatise was not published until it was edited by [Sir Henry Savile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_Henry_Savile "Sir Henry Savile") and printed in London, 1618; its circulation in manuscript was very limited. The implications of the infinite void were revolutionary; to have pursued them would have threatened the singular relationship of man and this natural world to God (Cantor 2001); in it he treated theology mathematically. He wrote also *De Geometria speculativa* (printed at Paris, 1530); *De Arithmetica practica* (printed at Paris, 1502); *De proportionibus velocitatum in motibus (1328)* (printed at Paris, 1495; Venice, 1505); *De Quadratura Circuli* (Paris, 1495); and an *Ars Memorative* ([Sloane MS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Sloane "Hans Sloane") no. 3974 in the [British Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Library "British Library")) – earning from the Pope the title of the "Profound Doctor". Another text, *De Continuo* is more tenuously credited to him and thought to be written sometime between 1328 and 1325.

## Theology

Bradwardine helped revive [Augustinian Theology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustinianism "Augustinianism") during his time in the fourteenth century. He wrote extensively on various subjects, including speculative arithmetic, geometry, and the workings of the human mind. As a theologian and scholar of natural philosophy, Bradwardine rejected [William of Ockham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_of_Ockham "William of Ockham")'s belief that God could know future events and [contingencies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contingency_(philosophy) "Contingency (philosophy)") in a limited sense, insisting instead that God's knowledge is absolute.

Bradwardine was influenced by the [Augustinian soteriology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustinian_soteriology "Augustinian soteriology"), which centered on a divine [monergism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monergism "Monergism") and implied a [double predestination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_predestination "Double predestination"). He accepted the idea of [predestination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Predestination "Predestination") and suggested that all evil acts of Human will were due to God. He argued that [providence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divine_providence "Divine providence") is inseparable from [predestination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Predestination "Predestination"), and rejected the notion that humans could do good of their own volition. Instead, he claimed they act solely according to God's will. Bradwardine would go on to write that free will and predestination through [predeterminism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Predeterminism "Predeterminism") are compatible, a theory known as [compatibilism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compatibilism "Compatibilism").

## Science

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/9f/Bradwardine_-_Geometria_speculativa%2C_1495_-_66918.jpg/330px-Bradwardine_-_Geometria_speculativa%2C_1495_-_66918.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Bradwardine_-_Geometria_speculativa,_1495_-_66918.jpg)

*Geometria speculativa*, 1495

Merton College sheltered a group of dons devoted to natural science, mainly physics, astronomy and mathematics, rivals of the intellectuals at the [University of Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Paris "University of Paris"). Bradwardine was one of these [Oxford Calculators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford_Calculators "Oxford Calculators"), studying mechanics with [William Heytesbury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Heytesbury "William Heytesbury"), [Richard Swineshead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Swineshead "Richard Swineshead"), and [John Dumbleton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Dumbleton "John Dumbleton"). The Oxford Calculators distinguished [kinematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinematics "Kinematics") from [dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamics_(mechanics) "Dynamics (mechanics)"), emphasising kinematics, and investigating instantaneous velocity. They first formulated the [mean speed theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mean_speed_theorem "Mean speed theorem"): a body moving with constant velocity travels the same distance as an accelerated body in the same time if its velocity is half the final speed of the accelerated body. They also demonstrated this theorem – the foundation of "The Law of Falling Bodies" – long before [Galileo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galileo_Galilei "Galileo Galilei"), who is generally credited with it.

The mathematical physicist and historian of science [Clifford Truesdell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clifford_Truesdell "Clifford Truesdell"), wrote:

> The now published sources prove to us, beyond contention, that the main kinematical properties of uniformly accelerated motions, still attributed to [Galileo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galileo_Galilei "Galileo Galilei") by the physics texts, were discovered and proved by scholars of Merton college.... In principle, the qualities of Greek physics were replaced, at least for motions, by the numerical quantities that have ruled Western science ever since. The work was quickly diffused into [France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/France "France"), [Italy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italy "Italy"), and other parts of [Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Europe "Europe"). Almost immediately, [Giovanni di Casale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giovanni_di_Casali "Giovanni di Casali") and [Nicole Oresme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicole_Oresme "Nicole Oresme") found how to represent the results by geometrical [graphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graph_of_a_function "Graph of a function"), introducing the connection between [geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometry "Geometry") and the physical world that became a second characteristic habit of Western thought ...

In *Tractatus de proportionibus* (1328), Bradwardine extended the theory of proportions of [Eudoxus of Cnidus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eudoxus_of_Cnidus "Eudoxus of Cnidus") to anticipate the concept of [exponential growth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exponential_growth "Exponential growth"), later developed by the [Bernoulli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jakob_Bernoulli "Jakob Bernoulli") and [Euler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonhard_Euler "Leonhard Euler"), with [compound interest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interest_(economics) "Interest (economics)") as a special case. Arguments for the mean speed theorem (above) require the modern mathematical concept of [limit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limit_(mathematics) "Limit (mathematics)"), so Bradwardine had to use arguments of his day. Mathematician and mathematical historian [Carl Benjamin Boyer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Benjamin_Boyer "Carl Benjamin Boyer") writes, "Bradwardine developed the [Boethian theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musical_note "Musical note") of double or triple or, more generally, what we would call 'n-tuple' proportion". Bradwardine attempted to reconcile contradictions in physics, where he largely adopted [Aristotle's description of the physical universe.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotelian_physics "Aristotelian physics")

Bradwardine rejected four opinions concerning the link between power, resistance, and speed on the basis that were inconsistent with Aristotle's or because they did not align with what could be easily observed regarding motion. He does this by examining the nature of ratios. The first opinion Bradwardine contemplates before rejecting is one he attributes to Avempace that states " that speeds follow the excesses of motive powers over resistances", following the formula (V ∝ [M−R], where V = speed M = motive power, and R = resistance). he second opinion follows the formula (V ∝ [M−R]/R), which states "that speeds follow the ratio of the excesses of the motive over the resisting powers to the resisting powers". Bradwardine claims this as the work of [Averroes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Averroes "Averroes"). The third opinion concerns the traditional interpretation of the Aristotelian rules of motion and states "that the speeds follow the inverse of the resistances when the moving powers are the same (V ∝ 1/R when M is constant) and follow the moving powers when the resistances are the same (V ∝ M when R is constant)". His last rejection was "that speeds do not follow any ratio because motive and resistive powers are quantities of different species and so cannot form ratios with each other". "Bradwardine's own rule is that the ratio of speeds follows the ratios of motive to resistive powers."

Bradwardine did identify one measurement error in Aristotle's law of motion. Bradwardine's identification of this error was described by [Ernest Moody](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_Addison_Moody "Ernest Addison Moody") as a "radical shift from Aristotelian dynamics to modern dynamics, initiated in the early fourteenth century." Aristotle's calculation of average speed was criticized by Bradwardine for not examining "the whole question of how moment-to-moment velocities are related within the whole time of the movement." Bradwardine also believed Aristotle contradicted himself with his explanation of resistance in motion. Aristotle believed "that a force has to be greater than its resistance in order to move, and the "proportion" (Bradwardine's word; we would say ratio) of force to resistance equaling the proportion of distance to time." Bradwardine did not accept the explanation and instead proposed "that the rate of velocity is the ratio of an exponential increase in force to resistance." Bradwardine's explanation does not align with the modern rules of the rates of motion, yet his goal to reconcile Aristotle's claim was accomplished and he was the first person to be credited for using exponential functions in an attempt to explain the laws of motion.

Boyer also writes that "the works of Bradwardine had contained some fundamentals of [trigonometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigonometry "Trigonometry") gleaned from [Muslim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslims "Muslims") sources". Yet "Bradwardine and his Oxford colleagues did not quite make the breakthrough to modern science" (Cantor 2001, p. 122).

[Al-Kindi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Kindi "Al-Kindi") in particular seemed to influence Bradwardine, though it is unclear whether this was directly or indirectly. Nonetheless, Bradwardine's work bears many similarities to the work of Al-Kindi, *Quia* *primos* (or *[De Gradibus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Gradibus "De Gradibus")*). [Gerard of Cremona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerard_of_Cremona "Gerard of Cremona")'s Latin translation of Quia primos (or [De Gradibus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Gradibus "De Gradibus")) would have been available to Bradwardine, but Roger Bacon seemed to be the only European philosopher to have had a direct connection to the book, but not to the degree of [Arnald of Villanova](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arnaldus_de_Villa_Nova "Arnaldus de Villa Nova"). Nonetheless, Bradwardine's work bears many similarities to the work of Al-Kindi.

## Art of memory

Bradwardine was also a practitioner and exponent of the [art of memory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_of_memory "Art of memory"), a loosely associated group of mnemonic principles and techniques used to organise memory impressions, improve recall, and assist in the combination and 'invention' of ideas. His *De Memoria Artificiali* (*c.* 1335) discusses memory training current during his time.

Bradwardine's *On Acquiring a Trained Memory*, translated by Mary Carruthers, contains, as Carruthers describes it, was similar to [Cicero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cicero "Cicero")'s work on the art of memory. She states, "Bradwardine's art is notable for its detailed description of several techniques for fixing and recalling specific material through the use of graphically detailed, brilliantly colored, and vigorously animated mental images, grouped together in a succession of *pictures* or organized scenes, whose internal order recalls not just particular content but the relationship among its parts." She acknowledges this being similar to active imaging described by Cicero, along with the memory devices for things and words being changed in rhetoric, but are distinct since the imagery Bradwardine uses is decidedly medieval in nature.

## Legacy

Bradwardine's theories on the *[insolubilia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insolubilia "Insolubilia")* including the [liar paradox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liar_paradox "Liar paradox") were a great influence on the work of [Jean Buridan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Buridan "Jean Buridan"). Bradwardine's work on kinematics was also influential to Buridan. Despite never rejecting the papacy, Thomas Bradwardine is cited as holding [Reformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reformation "Reformation") theology before [Luther](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Luther "Martin Luther") and [Calvin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Calvin "John Calvin").

His *De Causa Dei* influenced the theology of [John Wycliffe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Wycliffe "John Wycliffe") on [grace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grace_(Christianity) "Grace (Christianity)") and [predestination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Predestination "Predestination").
