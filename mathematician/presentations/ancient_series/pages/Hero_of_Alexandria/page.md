**Hero of Alexandria** ([/ˈhɪəroʊ/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English"); [Ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_language "Ancient Greek language"): Ἥρων ὁ Ἀλεξανδρεύς, *Hērōn hò Alexandreús*, also known as **Heron of Alexandria** [/ˈhɛrən/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English"); fl. probably 1st or 2nd century AD) was a [Greek mathematician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_mathematics "Greek mathematics") and [engineer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_technology "Ancient Greek technology") who was active in [Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandria "Alexandria") during the [Roman era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Egypt "Roman Egypt"). He has been described as the greatest experimentalist of antiquity and a representative of the [Hellenistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_civilization "Hellenistic civilization") scientific tradition.

Hero published a well-recognized description of a [steam-powered device](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steam_engine "Steam engine") called an *[aeolipile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeolipile "Aeolipile")*, also known as "Hero's engine". Among his most famous inventions was a [windwheel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Windmill "Windmill"), constituting the earliest instance of [wind harnessing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_power "Wind power") on land. In his work *Mechanics*, he described [pantographs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pantograph "Pantograph"). Some of his ideas were derived from the works of [Ctesibius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ctesibius "Ctesibius").

In mathematics, he wrote a commentary on [Euclid's *Elements*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclid's_Elements "Euclid's Elements") and a work on applied geometry known as the *Metrica*. He is mostly remembered for [Heron's formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heron's_formula "Heron's formula"), a way to calculate the area of a triangle using only the lengths of its sides.

Much of Hero's original writings and designs have been [lost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lost_literary_work "Lost literary work"), but some of his works were preserved in manuscripts from the [Byzantine Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Empire "Byzantine Empire") and, to a lesser extent, in Latin or Arabic translations.

## Life and career

Almost nothing is known about Hero's life, including his birthplace and background. The first extant mention of him is references to his works found in Book VIII of [Pappus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pappus_of_Alexandria "Pappus of Alexandria")'s *Collection* (4th century AD), and scholarly estimates for Hero's dates range from 150 BC to 250 AD. [Otto Neugebauer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otto_Neugebauer "Otto Neugebauer") (1938) noted a lunar eclipse observed in Alexandria and Rome used as a hypothetical example in Hero's *Dioptra*, and found that it best matched the details of an eclipse in 62 AD; [A. G. Drachmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A._G._Drachmann "A. G. Drachmann") subsequently surmised that Hero personally observed the eclipse from Alexandria. However, Hero does not explicitly say this, his brief mention of the eclipse is vague, and he might instead have used some earlier observer's data or even made up the example.

Alexandria was founded by [Alexander the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_the_Great "Alexander the Great") in the 4th century BC, and by Hero's time was a cosmopolitan city, part of the [Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Empire "Roman Empire"). The intellectual community, centered around the [Mouseion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mouseion "Mouseion") (which included the [Library of Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Library_of_Alexandria "Library of Alexandria")), spoke and wrote in Greek; however, there was considerable intermarriage between the city's Greek and Egyptian populations. It has been inferred that Hero taught at the Mouseion because some of his writings appear to be lecture notes or textbooks in [mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics "Mathematics"), [mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanics "Mechanics"), [physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics "Physics") and [pneumatics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pneumatics "Pneumatics"). Although the field was not formalized until the twentieth century, it is thought that works of Hero, in particular those on his automated devices, represented some of the first formal research into [cybernetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cybernetics "Cybernetics").

### Inventions

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a1/Aeolipile_illustration.svg/250px-Aeolipile_illustration.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Aeolipile_illustration.svg)

Hero's aeolipile

A number of devices and inventions have been ascribed to Hero, including the following:

- The *[aeolipile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeolipile "Aeolipile")* (a version of which is known as "Hero's engine"), which was a [rocket-like reaction engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocket_engine "Rocket engine") and the first-recorded [steam engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steam_engine "Steam engine") (although [Vitruvius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vitruvius "Vitruvius") mentioned the aeolipile in *[De Architectura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Architectura "De Architectura")*, presumably earlier than Hero). Another engine used air from a closed chamber heated by an altar fire to displace water from a sealed vessel; the water was collected and its weight, pulling on a rope, opened temple doors. Some historians have conflated the two inventions to assert that the aeolipile was capable of useful work.
- A [vending machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vending_machine "Vending machine") that dispensed a set amount of water for ablutions when a coin was introduced via a slot on the top of the machine. This was included in his list of inventions in his book *Mechanics*. When the coin was deposited, it fell upon a pan attached to a lever. The lever opened up a valve which let some water flow out. The pan continued to tilt with the weight of the coin until it fell off, at which point a counter-weight would snap the lever back up and turn off the valve.
- A wind-wheel operating an organ, marking the first documented instance of wind powering a machine.
- Many mechanisms for the Greek [theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theater "Theater"), including an entirely mechanical play almost ten minutes in length, powered by a system of ropes, knots, and simple machines operated by a rotating cylindrical cogwheel. The sound of [thunder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thunder "Thunder") was produced by the mechanically-timed dropping of metal balls onto a hidden drum.
- A [force pump](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force_pump "Force pump") that was widely used in the [Roman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Rome "Ancient Rome") world, and one application was in a fire engine.
- A [syringe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syringe "Syringe")-like device was described by Hero to control the delivery of air or liquids.
- A stand-alone fountain that operates under self-contained hydro-static energy; now called [Heron's fountain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heron's_fountain "Heron's fountain").
- A cart that was powered by a falling weight and strings wrapped around the drive axle.
- A kind of [thermometer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermometer "Thermometer") has been credited to Hero. Although the thermometer was [not a single invention but a development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline_of_temperature_and_pressure_measurement_technology "Timeline of temperature and pressure measurement technology"), Hero knew of the principle that certain substances, notably air, expand and contract and described a demonstration in which a closed tube partially filled with air had its end in a container of water. The expansion and contraction of the air caused the position of the water/air interface to move along the tube.
- A self-filling wine bowl, using a [float valve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Float_valve "Float valve").

### Mathematics

Hero described an iterative algorithm for computing [square roots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_root "Square root"), now called [Heron's method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_root_algorithms#Heron's_method "Square root algorithms"), in his work *Metrica*, alongside other algorithms and approximations. Today, however, his name is most closely associated with [Heron's formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heron's_formula "Heron's formula") for the area of a triangle in terms of its side lengths. Hero also reported on a method for calculating cube roots. In [solid geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid_geometry "Solid geometry"), the [Heronian mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heronian_mean "Heronian mean") may be used in finding the [volume](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volume "Volume") of a [frustum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frustum "Frustum") of a [pyramid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyramid "Pyramid") or [cone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cone_(geometry) "Cone (geometry)").

Hero also described a shortest path algorithm, that is, given two points A and B on one side of a line, find a point C on the straight line that minimizes AC + BC. This led him to formulate the [principle of the shortest path of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermat's_principle "Fermat's principle"): If a ray of light propagates from point A to point B within the same medium, the path-length followed is the shortest possible (Hero's principle). In the Middle Ages, [Ibn al-Haytham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_al-Haytham "Ibn al-Haytham") expanded the principle to both reflection and refraction, and the principle was later stated in this form by [Pierre de Fermat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_de_Fermat "Pierre de Fermat") in 1662; the most modern form is that the optical path is [stationary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stationary_point "Stationary point").

## Bibliography

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/01/Hero_-_De_automatis%2C_1589_-_116959.jpg/250px-Hero_-_De_automatis%2C_1589_-_116959.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Hero_-_De_automatis,_1589_-_116959.jpg)

The book *About [automata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automata "Automata")* by Hero of Alexandria (1589 edition)

The most comprehensive edition of Hero's works was published in five volumes in [Leipzig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leipzig "Leipzig") by the publishing house [Teubner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teubner "Teubner") in 1903.

Works known to have been written by Hero include:

- *Pneumatica* (Πνευματικά), a description of machines working on [air](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air "Air"), [steam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steam "Steam") or [water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water "Water") [pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure "Pressure"), including the *hydraulis* or [water organ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_organ "Water organ")
- *Automata*, a description of machines which enable wonders in banquets and possibly also theatrical contexts by mechanical or pneumatical means (e.g. automatic opening or closing of temple doors, statues that pour wine and milk, etc.)
- *Belopoeica*, a description of [war machines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanized_warfare "Mechanized warfare")
- *Dioptra*, a collection of methods to measure lengths, where the [odometer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odometer "Odometer") and the [dioptra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dioptra "Dioptra") (an apparatus resembling the [theodolite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodolite "Theodolite")) are described
- *Metrica*, a work describing how to calculate [surface areas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_area "Surface area") and [volumes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volume "Volume") of diverse geometrical objects

Works that have been preserved only in Arabic translations:

- *Mechanica*, written for [architects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architect "Architect"), containing means to lift heavy objects
- *Catoptrica*, about the progression of [light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light "Light"), [reflection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflection_(physics) "Reflection (physics)"), and the use of [mirrors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mirror "Mirror")

Works that sometimes have been attributed to Hero, but are now thought most likely to have been written by someone else:

- *Geometrica*, a collection of problems similar to the first chapter of *Metrica*
- *Stereometrica*, examples of three-dimensional calculations similar to the second chapter of *Metrica*
- *Mensurae*, tools which can be used to conduct measurements for problems based on *Stereometrica* and *Metrica*
- *Cheiroballistra*, about [catapults](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catapult "Catapult")
- *Definitiones*, containing definitions of terms for geometry

Works that are preserved only in fragments:

- *Geodesia*
- *Geoponica*
- A commentary to Euclid's *[Elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclid's_Elements "Euclid's Elements")*, attested by Arabic authors but no longer extant
