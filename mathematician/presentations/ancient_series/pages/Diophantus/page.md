**Diophantus of [Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandria "Alexandria")** ([/daɪoʊˈfæntəs/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English"); [Ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_language "Ancient Greek language"): Διόφαντος, [romanized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanization_of_Ancient_Greek "Romanization of Ancient Greek"): *Diophantos*; fl. 250 CE) was a [Greek mathematician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_mathematics "Greek mathematics") who was the author of the *[Arithmetica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arithmetica "Arithmetica")* in thirteen books, ten of which are still extant, made up of arithmetical problems that are solved through [algebraic equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_equation "Algebraic equation").

[Joseph-Louis Lagrange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph-Louis_Lagrange "Joseph-Louis Lagrange") called Diophantus "the inventor of [algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebra "Algebra")"; his exposition became the standard within the [Neoplatonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoplatonism "Neoplatonism") schools of [Late antiquity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_antiquity "Late antiquity"), and its translation into Arabic in the 9th century AD and had influence in the development of later algebra: Diophantus' method of solution matches medieval Arabic algebra in its concepts and overall procedure. The 1621 edition of *Arithmetica* by [Bachet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachet "Bachet") gained fame after [Pierre de Fermat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_de_Fermat "Pierre de Fermat") wrote his famous "[Last Theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermat's_Last_Theorem "Fermat's Last Theorem")" in the margins of his copy.

In modern use, [Diophantine equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diophantine_equation "Diophantine equation") are algebraic equations with [integer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integer "Integer") coefficients for which integer solutions are sought. [Diophantine geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diophantine_geometry "Diophantine geometry") and [Diophantine approximations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diophantine_approximation "Diophantine approximation") are two other subareas of [number theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_theory "Number theory") that are named after him. Some problems from the *Arithmetica* have inspired modern work in both [abstract algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstract_algebra "Abstract algebra") and [number theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_theory "Number theory"). As a result of his work, he has sometimes been referred to as the father of algebra.

## Biography

The exact details of Diophantus' life are obscure. Although he probably flourished in the third century CE, he may have lived anywhere between 170 BCE, roughly contemporaneous with [Hypsicles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypsicles "Hypsicles"), the latest author he quotes from, and 350 CE, when [Theon of Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theon_of_Alexandria "Theon of Alexandria") quotes from him. [Paul Tannery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Tannery "Paul Tannery") suggested that a reference to an "Anatolius" as a student of Diophantus in the works of [Michael Psellos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Psellos "Michael Psellos") may refer to the early Christian bishop [Anatolius of Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatolius_of_Alexandria "Anatolius of Alexandria"), who may possibly the same Anatolius mentioned by [Eunapius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eunapius "Eunapius") as a teacher of the pagan [Neopythagorean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neopythagorean "Neopythagorean") philosopher [Iamblichus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iamblichus "Iamblichus"), either of which would place him in the 3rd century CE.

The only definitive piece of information about his life is derived from a set of mathematical puzzles attributed to the 5th or 6th century CE grammarian [Metrodorus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metrodorus_(grammarian) "Metrodorus (grammarian)") preserved in book 14 of the [Greek Anthology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_Anthology "Greek Anthology"). One of the problems (sometimes called Diophantus' epitaph) states:

> :   Here lies Diophantus, the wonder behold.
> :   Through art algebraic, the stone tells how old:
> :   God gave him his boyhood one-sixth of his life,
> :   One twelfth more as youth while whiskers grew rife;
>
> :   And then yet one-seventh ere marriage begun;
> :   In five years there came a bouncing new son.
>
> :   Alas, the dear child of master and sage
> :   After attaining half the measure of his father's life chill fate took him.
> :   After consoling his fate by the science of numbers for four years, he ended his life.'

This puzzle implies that Diophantus' age *x* can be expressed as

:   *x* = ⁠*x*/6⁠ + ⁠*x*/12⁠ + ⁠*x*/7⁠ + 5 + ⁠*x*/2⁠ + 4

which gives *x* a value of 84 years. However, the accuracy of the information cannot be confirmed.

*Arithmetica* is the major work of Diophantus and the most prominent work on premodern [algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebra "Algebra") in Greek mathematics. It is a collection of 290 [algebraic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebra "Algebra") problems giving numerical solutions of determinate [equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equations "Equations") (those with a unique solution) and [indeterminate equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indeterminate_equation "Indeterminate equation"). *Arithmetica* was originally written in thirteen books, but only six of them survive in Greek, while another four books survive in Arabic, which were discovered in 1968. The books in Arabic correspond to books 4 to 7 of the original treatise, while the Greek books correspond to books 1 to 3 and 8 to 10.

*Arithmetica* is the earliest extant work present that solve arithmetic problems by algebra. Diophantus however did not invent the method of algebra, which existed before him. Algebra was practiced and diffused orally by practitioners, with Diophantus picking up technique to solve problems in arithmetic.

The book considers finding integer solutions to equations with integer coefficients, a class of problem presently called [Diophantine equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diophantine_equation "Diophantine equation"). The methods for solving such equations is called [Diophantine analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diophantine_analysis "Diophantine analysis"). Most of the *Arithmetica* problems lead to [quadratic equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_equation "Quadratic equation").

### Notation

Diophantus introduced an algebraic symbolism that used an abridged notation for frequently occurring operations, and an abbreviation for the unknown and for the powers of the unknown.

Similar to medieval Arabic algebra, Diophantus uses three stages to solution of a problem by algebra:

1. An unknown is named and an equation is set up
2. An equation is simplified to a standard form (*al-jabr* and *al-muqābala* in Arabic)
3. Simplified equation is solved

Diophantus does not give classification of equations in six types like [Al-Khwarizmi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Khwarizmi "Al-Khwarizmi") in extant parts of *Arithmetica*. He does say that he would give solution to three terms equations later, so this part of work is possibly just lost.

### Contents

In Book 3, Diophantus solves problems of finding values which make two linear expressions simultaneously into squares or cubes. In book 4, he finds rational powers between given numbers. He also noticed that numbers of the form

4
n
+
3
{\displaystyle 4n+3}
![{\displaystyle 4n+3}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/8b64683d9a6ebd4e5644740d0bbe76ffa42e90ef) cannot be the sum of two squares. Diophantus also appears to know that [every number can be written as the sum of four squares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagrange's_four-square_theorem "Lagrange's four-square theorem"). If he did know this result (in the sense of having proved it as opposed to merely conjectured it), his doing so would be truly remarkable: even Fermat, who stated the result, failed to provide a proof of it and it was not settled until [Joseph-Louis Lagrange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph-Louis_Lagrange "Joseph-Louis Lagrange") proved it using results due to [Leonhard Euler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonhard_Euler "Leonhard Euler").

## Other works

Another work by Diophantus, *On Polygonal Numbers* is transmitted in an incomplete form in four Byzantine manuscripts along with the *Arithmetica*. Two other lost works by Diophantus are known: *Porisms* and *On Parts*.

Recently, [Wilbur Knorr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilbur_Knorr "Wilbur Knorr") has suggested that another book, *Preliminaries to the Geometric Elements*, traditionally attributed to [Hero of Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hero_of_Alexandria "Hero of Alexandria"), may actually be by Diophantus.

### On polygonal numbers

This work on [polygonal numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polygonal_number "Polygonal number"), a topic that was of great interest to the [Pythagoreans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagoreans "Pythagoreans") consists of a preface and five propositions in its extant form. The treatise breaks off in the middle of a proposition about how many ways a number can be a polygonal number.

### The *Porisms*

The *Porisms* was a collection of [lemmas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lemma_(mathematics) "Lemma (mathematics)") along with accompanying proofs. Although *The Porisms* is lost, we know three lemmas contained there, since Diophantus quotes them in the *Arithmetica* and refers the reader to the *Porisms* for the proof.

One lemma states that the difference of the cubes of two rational numbers is equal to the sum of the cubes of two other rational numbers, i.e. given any rational *a* and *b*, with *a* > *b* > 0, there exist rational *c*, *d* > 0 such that

:   *a*3 − *b*3 = *c*3 + *d*3.

### *On Parts*

This work, on [fractions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fractions "Fractions"), is known by a single reference, a [Neoplatonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoplatonic "Neoplatonic") [scholium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scholia "Scholia") to [Iamblichus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iamblichus "Iamblichus")' treatise on [Nicomachus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicomachus "Nicomachus")' *[Introduction to Arithmetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Introduction_to_Arithmetic "Introduction to Arithmetic")*. Next to a line where Iamblichus writes "Some of the Pythagoreans said that the [unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_(number) "Unit (number)") is the borderline between number and parts" the scholiast writes "So Diophantus writes in *On Parts*, for parts involve progress in diminution carried to infinity."

## Influence

Diophantus' work has had a large influence in history. Although [Joseph-Louis Lagrange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph-Louis_Lagrange "Joseph-Louis Lagrange") called Diophantus "the inventor of [algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebra "Algebra")", he did not invent it, however his work *Arithmetica* created a foundation for work on algebra and in fact much of advanced mathematics is based on algebra. Diophantus and his works influenced [mathematics in the medieval Islamic world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics_in_the_medieval_Islamic_world "Mathematics in the medieval Islamic world"), and editions of *Arithmetica* exerted a profound influence on the development of algebra in Europe in the late sixteenth and through the 17th and 18th centuries.

### Later antiquity

After its publication, Diophantus' work continued to be read in the Greek-speaking Mediterranean from the 4th through the 7th centuries. The earliest known reference to Diophantus, in the 4th century, is the *Commentary on the [Almagest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almagest "Almagest")* [Theon of Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theon_of_Alexandria "Theon of Alexandria"), which quotes from the introduction to the *Arithmetica*. According to the [Suda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suda "Suda"), [Hypatia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypatia "Hypatia"), who was Theon's daughter and frequent collaborator, wrote a now lost commentary on Diophantus' *Arithmetica*, which suggests that this work may have been closely studied by [Neoplatonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoplatonism "Neoplatonism") mathematicians in Alexandria during [Late antiquity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_antiquity "Late antiquity"). References to Diophantus also survive in a number of Neoplatonic [scholia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scholia "Scholia") to the works of [Iamblichus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iamblichus "Iamblichus"). A 6th century Neoplatonic commentary on [Porphyry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porphyry_of_Tyre "Porphyry of Tyre")'s *[Isagoge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isagoge "Isagoge")* by [Pseudo-Elias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudo-Elias "Pseudo-Elias") also mentions Diophantus; after outlining the [quadrivium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadrivium "Quadrivium") of [arithmetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arithmetic "Arithmetic"), [geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometry "Geometry"), [music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music "Music"), and [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy "Astronomy") and four other disciplines adjacent to them ("logistic", "geodesy", "music in matter" and "spherics"), it mentions that [Nicomachus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicomachus "Nicomachus") (author of the *Introduction to Arithmetic*) occupies the first place in arithmetic but Diophantus occupies the first place in "logistic", showing that, despite the title of *Arithmetica*, the more algebraic work of Diophantus was already seen as distinct from arithmetic prior to the medieval era.

### Medieval era

Like many other Greek mathematical treatises, Diophantus was forgotten in Western Europe during the [Dark Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dark_Ages_(historiography) "Dark Ages (historiography)"), since the study of ancient Greek, and literacy in general, had greatly declined. The portion of the Greek *Arithmetica* that survived, however, was, like all ancient Greek texts transmitted to the early modern world, copied by, and thus known to, medieval Byzantine scholars. Scholia on Diophantus by the Byzantine Greek scholar [John Chortasmenos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Chortasmenos "John Chortasmenos") (1370–1437) are preserved together with a comprehensive commentary written by the earlier Greek scholar [Maximos Planudes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximos_Planudes "Maximos Planudes") (1260 – 1305), who produced an edition of Diophantus within the library of the [Chora Monastery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chora_Monastery "Chora Monastery") in Byzantine [Constantinople](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantinople "Constantinople").

*Arithmetica* became known to [mathematicians in the Islamic world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics_in_medieval_Islam "Mathematics in medieval Islam") in the ninth century, when [Qusta ibn Luqa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qusta_ibn_Luqa "Qusta ibn Luqa") translated it into Arabic.

In 1463 German mathematician [Regiomontanus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regiomontanus "Regiomontanus") wrote: "No one has yet translated from the Greek into Latin the thirteen books of Diophantus, in which the very flower of the whole of arithmetic lies hidden." *Arithmetica* was first translated from Greek into [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin "Latin") by [Bombelli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rafael_Bombelli "Rafael Bombelli") in 1570, but the translation was never published. However, Bombelli borrowed many of the problems for his own book *Algebra*. The *[editio princeps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Editio_princeps "Editio princeps")* of *Arithmetica* was published in 1575 by [Xylander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guilielmus_Xylander "Guilielmus Xylander").

### Fermat

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/47/Diophantus-II-8-Fermat.jpg/250px-Diophantus-II-8-Fermat.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Diophantus-II-8-Fermat.jpg)

[Problem II.8](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diophantus_II.VIII "Diophantus II.VIII") in the *Arithmetica* (edition of 1670), annotated with Fermat's comment which became [Fermat's Last Theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermat's_Last_Theorem "Fermat's Last Theorem").

The Latin translation of *Arithmetica* by [Bachet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachet "Bachet") in 1621 became the first Latin edition that was widely available. [Pierre de Fermat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_de_Fermat "Pierre de Fermat") owned a copy, studied it and made notes in the margins. The 1621 edition of *Arithmetica* by [Bachet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachet "Bachet") gained fame after [Pierre de Fermat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_de_Fermat "Pierre de Fermat") wrote his famous "[Last Theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermat's_Last_Theorem "Fermat's Last Theorem")" in the margins of his copy:

> If an integer *n* is greater than 2, then *a**n* + *b**n* = *c**n* has no solutions in non-zero integers *a*, *b*, and *c*. I have a truly marvelous proof of this proposition which this margin is too narrow to contain.

Fermat's proof was never found, and the problem of finding a proof for the theorem went unsolved for centuries. A proof was finally found in 1994 by [Andrew Wiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Wiles "Andrew Wiles") after working on it for seven years. It is believed that Fermat did not actually have the proof he claimed to have. Although the original copy in which Fermat wrote this is lost today, Fermat's son edited the next edition of Diophantus, published in 1670. Even though the text is otherwise inferior to the 1621 edition, Fermat's annotations—including the "Last Theorem"—were printed in this version.

Fermat was not the first mathematician so moved to write in his own marginal notes to Diophantus; the Byzantine scholar [John Chortasmenos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Chortasmenos "John Chortasmenos") (1370–1437) had written "Thy soul, Diophantus, be with Satan because of the difficulty of your other theorems and particularly of the present theorem" next to the same problem.

Diophantus was among the first to recognise positive [rational numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_number "Rational number") as numbers, by allowing fractions for coefficients and solutions. He coined the term παρισότης (*parisotēs*) to refer to an approximate equality. This term was rendered as *adaequalitas* in Latin, and became the technique of [adequality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adequality "Adequality") developed by [Pierre de Fermat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_de_Fermat "Pierre de Fermat") to find maxima for functions and tangent lines to curves.

### Diophantine analysis

Today, Diophantine analysis is the area of study where integer (whole-number) solutions are sought for equations, and Diophantine equations are polynomial equations with integer coefficients to which only integer solutions are sought. It is usually rather difficult to tell whether a given Diophantine equation is solvable. Most of the problems in *Arithmetica* lead to [quadratic equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_equation "Quadratic equation"). Diophantus looked at 3 different types of quadratic equations: *ax*2 + *bx* = *c*, *ax*2 = *bx* + *c*, and *ax*2 + *c* = *bx*. The reason why there were three cases to Diophantus, while today we have only one case, is that he did not have any notion for zero and he avoided [negative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negative_numbers "Negative numbers") coefficients by considering the given numbers *a*, *b*, *c* to all be positive in each of the three cases above. Diophantus was always satisfied with a rational solution and did not require a whole number which means he accepted fractions as solutions to his problems. Diophantus considered negative or [irrational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irrational_number "Irrational number") square root solutions "useless", "meaningless", and even "absurd". To give one specific example, he calls the equation 4 = 4*x* + 20 'absurd' because it would lead to a negative value for *x*. One solution was all he looked for in a quadratic equation. There is no evidence that suggests Diophantus even realized that there could be two solutions to a quadratic equation. He also considered [simultaneous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simultaneous_equations "Simultaneous equations") quadratic equations.

### Rediscovery of books IV-VII

In 1968, [Fuat Sezgin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuat_Sezgin "Fuat Sezgin") found four previously unknown books of *Arithmetica* at the shrine of Imam Rezā in [Mashhad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mashhad "Mashhad") in northeastern Iran. The four books are thought to have been translated from Greek to Arabic by [Qusta ibn Luqa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qusta_ibn_Luqa "Qusta ibn Luqa") (820–912). [Norbert Schappacher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norbert_Schappacher "Norbert Schappacher") has written:

> [The four missing books] resurfaced around 1971 in the [Astan Quds Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Library_of_Astan_Quds_Razavi "Central Library of Astan Quds Razavi") in Meshed (Iran) in a copy from 1198. It was not catalogued under the name of Diophantus (but under that of [Qusta ibn Luqa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qusta_ibn_Luqa "Qusta ibn Luqa")) because the librarian was apparently not able to read the main line of the cover page where Diophantus’s name appears in geometric [Kufi calligraphy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kufic "Kufic").
