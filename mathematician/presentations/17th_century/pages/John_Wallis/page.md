**John Wallis** ([/ˈwɒlɪs/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English"); [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_language "Latin language"): *Wallisius*; 3 December [[O.S.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Style_and_New_Style_dates "Old Style and New Style dates") 23 November] 1616 – 8 November [[O.S.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Style_and_New_Style_dates "Old Style and New Style dates") 28 October] 1703) was an English clergyman and [mathematician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematician "Mathematician"), who is given partial credit for the development of [infinitesimal calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinitesimal_calculus "Infinitesimal calculus").

Between 1643 and 1689 Wallis served as chief [cryptographer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptographer "Cryptographer") for [Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliament_of_the_United_Kingdom "Parliament of the United Kingdom") and, later, the royal court. He extended [Cavalieri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bonaventura_Cavalieri "Bonaventura Cavalieri")'s method of indivisibles through the use of interpolation. Applying [Kepler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johannes_Kepler "Johannes Kepler")'s principle of continuity, he developed techniques for the evaluation of integrals. He is credited with introducing the [symbol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbol "Symbol") ∞ to represent the concept of [infinity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinity "Infinity"). He similarly used [1/∞](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1/∞ "1/∞") for an [infinitesimal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinitesimal "Infinitesimal"). He was a contemporary of [Isaac Newton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Newton "Isaac Newton") and one of the greatest intellectuals of the [early modern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_modern_period "Early modern period") [mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics "Mathematics").

## Life

John Wallis was born in [Ashford, Kent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashford,_Kent "Ashford, Kent"). He was the third of five children of Revd. John Wallis and Joanna Chapman. He was initially educated at a school in Ashford but moved to James Movat's school in [Tenterden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tenterden "Tenterden") in 1625 following an outbreak of [plague](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bubonic_plague "Bubonic plague"). Wallis was first exposed to mathematics in 1631, at Felsted School (then known as Martin Holbeach's school in Felsted); he enjoyed maths, but his study was erratic, since "mathematics, at that time with us, were scarce looked on as academical studies, but rather mechanical" ([Scriba 1970](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Wallis#References)). At the school in [Felsted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Felsted_School "Felsted School"), Wallis learned how to speak and write [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin "Latin"). By this time, he also was proficient in [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language "French language"), [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language "Greek language"), and [Hebrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_language "Hebrew language"). As it was intended he should be a doctor, he was sent in 1632 to [Emmanuel College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emmanuel_College,_Cambridge "Emmanuel College, Cambridge"). While there, he kept an *act* on the doctrine of the [circulation of the blood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circulation_of_the_blood "Circulation of the blood"); that was said to have been the first occasion in Europe on which this theory was publicly maintained in a disputation. His interests, however, centred on mathematics. He received his Bachelor of Arts degree in 1637 and a Master's in 1640, afterwards entering the priesthood. From 1643 to 1649, he served as a nonvoting scribe at the [Westminster Assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westminster_Assembly "Westminster Assembly"). He was elected to a fellowship at [Queens' College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queens'_College,_Cambridge "Queens' College, Cambridge") in 1644, from which he had to resign following his marriage.

Throughout this time, Wallis had been close to the Parliamentarian party, perhaps as a result of his exposure to Holbeach at Felsted School. He rendered them great practical assistance in deciphering Royalist dispatches. The quality of cryptography at that time was mixed; despite the individual successes of mathematicians such as [François Viète](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/François_Viète "François Viète"), the principles underlying cipher design and analysis were very poorly understood. Most ciphers were ad hoc methods relying on a secret [algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algorithm "Algorithm"), as opposed to systems based on a variable [key](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Key_(cryptography) "Key (cryptography)"). Wallis realised that the latter were far more secure – even describing them as "unbreakable", though he was not confident enough in this assertion to encourage revealing cryptographic algorithms. He was also concerned about the use of ciphers by foreign powers, refusing, for example, [Gottfried Leibniz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gottfried_Leibniz "Gottfried Leibniz")'s request of 1697 to teach [Hanoverian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electorate_of_Hanover "Electorate of Hanover") students about cryptography.

Returning to London – he had been made chaplain at [St Gabriel Fenchurch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Gabriel_Fenchurch "St Gabriel Fenchurch") in 1643 – Wallis joined the group of scientists that was later to evolve into the [Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society "Royal Society"). He was finally able to indulge his mathematical interests, mastering [William Oughtred](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Oughtred "William Oughtred")'s *Clavis Mathematicae* in a few weeks in 1647. He soon began to write his own treatises, dealing with a wide range of topics, which he continued for the rest of his life. Wallis wrote the first survey about mathematical concepts in England where he discussed the Hindu-Arabic system.

Wallis joined the moderate Presbyterians in signing the remonstrance against the execution of [Charles I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_I_of_England "Charles I of England"), by which he incurred the lasting hostility of the Independents. In spite of their opposition he was appointed in 1649 to the [Savilian Chair of Geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Savilian_Professor_of_Geometry "Savilian Professor of Geometry") at Oxford University, where he lived until his death on 8 November [[O.S.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Style_and_New_Style_dates "Old Style and New Style dates") 28 October] 1703. In 1650, Wallis was ordained as a minister. After, he spent two years with Sir Richard Darley and Lady Vere as a private [chaplain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chaplain "Chaplain"). In 1661, he was one of twelve [Presbyterian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presbyterian "Presbyterian") representatives at the [Savoy Conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Savoy_Conference "Savoy Conference").

Besides his mathematical works he wrote on [theology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theology "Theology"), [logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logic "Logic"), [English grammar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_grammar "English grammar") and philosophy, and he was involved in devising a system for teaching a deaf boy to speak at [Littlecote House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Littlecote_House "Littlecote House"). [William Holder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Holder "William Holder") had earlier taught a deaf man, Alexander Popham, to speak "plainly and distinctly, and with a good and graceful tone". Wallis later claimed credit for this, leading Holder to accuse Wallis of "rifling his Neighbours, and adorning himself with their spoyls".

### Wallis' appointment as Savilian Professor of Geometry at the Oxford University

The [Parliamentary visitation of Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliamentary_visitation_of_Oxford "Parliamentary visitation of Oxford"), that began in 1647, removed many senior academics from their positions, including in November 1648, the [Savilian Professors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Savilian_Professor_of_Geometry "Savilian Professor of Geometry") of Geometry and Astronomy. In 1649 Wallis was appointed as Savilian Professor of Geometry. Wallis seems to have been chosen largely on political grounds (as perhaps had been his Royalist predecessor [Peter Turner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Turner_(mathematician) "Peter Turner (mathematician)"), who despite his appointment to two professorships never published any mathematical works); while Wallis was perhaps the nation's leading cryptographer and was part of an informal group of scientists that would later become the [Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society "Royal Society"), he had no particular reputation as a mathematician. Nonetheless, Wallis' appointment proved richly justified by his subsequent work during the 54 years he served as Savilian Professor.

## Contributions to mathematics

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/9a/Wallis_-_Opera_mathematica%2C_1699_-_4760514_980122_3_00541.tif/lossy-page1-250px-Wallis_-_Opera_mathematica%2C_1699_-_4760514_980122_3_00541.tif.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Wallis_-_Opera_mathematica,_1699_-_4760514_980122_3_00541.tif)

*Opera mathematica*, 1699

Wallis made significant contributions to [trigonometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigonometry "Trigonometry"), [calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calculus "Calculus"), [geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometry "Geometry"), and the analysis of [infinite series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_series "Infinite series"). In his *Opera Mathematica* I (1695) he introduced the term "[continued fraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continued_fraction "Continued fraction")".

### Analytic geometry

In 1655, Wallis published a treatise on [conic sections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conic_section "Conic section") in which they were defined analytically. This was the earliest book in which these curves are considered and defined as curves of the second [degree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degree_of_a_polynomial "Degree of a polynomial"). It helped to remove some of the perceived difficulty and obscurity of [René Descartes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/René_Descartes "René Descartes")' work on [analytic geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytic_geometry "Analytic geometry").

In *Treatise on the Conic Sections*, Wallis popularised the symbol ∞ for infinity. He wrote, "I suppose any plane (following the *Geometry of Indivisibles* of Cavalieri) to be made up of an infinite number of parallel lines, or as I would prefer, of an infinite number of parallelograms of the same altitude; (let the altitude of each one of these be an infinitely small part [1/∞](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1/∞ "1/∞") of the whole altitude, and let the symbol ∞ denote Infinity) and the altitude of all to make up the altitude of the figure."

(which is now known as the [Wallis product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wallis_product "Wallis product")).

In this work the formation and properties of [continued fractions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continued_fraction "Continued fraction") are also discussed, the subject having been brought into prominence by [Brouncker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Brouncker,_2nd_Viscount_Brouncker "William Brouncker, 2nd Viscount Brouncker")'s use of these fractions.

A few years later, in 1659, Wallis published a tract containing the solution of the problems on the [cycloid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cycloid "Cycloid") which had been proposed by [Blaise Pascal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blaise_Pascal "Blaise Pascal"). In this he incidentally explained how the principles laid down in his *Arithmetica Infinitorum* could be used for the rectification of algebraic curves and gave a solution of the problem to rectify (i.e., find the length of) the semicubical parabola *x*3 = *ay*2, which had been discovered in 1657 by his pupil [William Neile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Neile "William Neile"). Since all attempts to rectify the [ellipse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ellipse "Ellipse") and hyperbola had been (necessarily) ineffectual, it had been supposed that no curves could be rectified, as indeed Descartes had definitely asserted to be the case. The [logarithmic spiral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logarithmic_spiral "Logarithmic spiral") had been rectified by [Evangelista Torricelli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evangelista_Torricelli "Evangelista Torricelli") and was the first curved line (other than the circle) whose length was determined, but the extension by Neile and Wallis to an algebraic curve was novel. The cycloid was the next curve rectified; this was done by [Christopher Wren](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Wren "Christopher Wren") in 1658.

Early in 1658 a similar discovery, independent of that of Neile, was made by [van Heuraët](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Van_Heuraët "Van Heuraët"), and this was published by [van Schooten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Van_Schooten "Van Schooten") in his edition of Descartes's *Geometria* in 1659. Van Heuraët's method is as follows. He supposes the curve to be referred to rectangular axes; if this is so, and if (*x*, *y*) are the coordinates of any point on it, and *n* is the length of the normal, and if another point whose coordinates are (*x*, *η*) is taken such that *η* : *h* = *n* : *y*, where *h* is a constant; then, if *ds* is the element of the length of the required curve, we have by [similar triangles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Similar_triangles "Similar triangles") *ds* : *dx* = *n* : *y*. Therefore, *h ds* = *η* *dx*. Hence, if the area of the [locus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locus_(mathematics) "Locus (mathematics)") of the point (*x*, *η*) can be found, the first curve can be rectified. In this way van Heuraët effected the rectification of the curve *y*3 = *ax*2 but added that the rectification of the parabola *y*2 = *ax* is impossible since it requires the quadrature of the hyperbola. The solutions given by Neile and Wallis are somewhat similar to that given by van Heuraët, though no general rule is enunciated, and the analysis is clumsy. A third method was suggested by [Fermat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_de_Fermat "Pierre de Fermat") in 1660, but it is inelegant and laborious.

### Collision of bodies

The theory of the [collision of bodies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collision_of_bodies "Collision of bodies") was propounded by the [Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society "Royal Society") in 1668 for the consideration of mathematicians. Wallis, [Christopher Wren](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Wren "Christopher Wren"), and [Christiaan Huygens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christiaan_Huygens "Christiaan Huygens") sent correct and similar solutions, all depending on what is now called the [conservation of momentum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_of_momentum "Conservation of momentum"); but, while Wren and Huygens confined their theory to perfectly elastic bodies ([elastic collision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elastic_collision "Elastic collision")), Wallis considered also imperfectly elastic bodies ([inelastic collision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inelastic_collision "Inelastic collision")). This was followed in 1669 by a work on [statics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statics "Statics") (centres of gravity), and in 1670 by one on [dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytical_dynamics "Analytical dynamics"): these provide a convenient synopsis of what was then known on the subject.

### Algebra

In 1685 Wallis published *Algebra*, preceded by a historical account of the development of the subject, which contains a great deal of valuable information. The second edition, issued in 1693 and forming the second volume of his *Opera*, was considerably enlarged. This algebra is noteworthy as containing the first systematic use of formulae. A given magnitude is here represented by the numerical ratio which it bears to the unit of the same kind of magnitude: thus, when Wallis wants to compare two lengths he regards each as containing so many units of length. This perhaps will be made clearer by noting that the relation between the space described in any time by a particle moving with a uniform velocity is denoted by Wallis by the formula

:   *s* = *vt*,

where *s* is the number representing the ratio of the space described to the unit of length; while the previous writers would have denoted the same relation by stating what is equivalent to the proposition

:   *s*1 : *s*2 = *v*1*t*1 : *v*2*t*2.

### Geometry

He is usually credited with the proof of the [Pythagorean theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagorean_theorem "Pythagorean theorem") using [similar triangles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Similar_triangles "Similar triangles"). However, [Thabit Ibn Qurra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thabit_Ibn_Qurra "Thabit Ibn Qurra") (AD 901), an Arab mathematician, had produced a generalisation of the Pythagorean theorem applicable to all triangles six centuries earlier. It is a reasonable conjecture that Wallis was aware of Thabit's work.

Wallis was also inspired by the works of Islamic mathematician Sadr al-Tusi, the son of [Nasir al-Din al-Tusi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nasir_al-Din_al-Tusi "Nasir al-Din al-Tusi"), particularly by al-Tusi's book written in 1298 on the [parallel postulate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallel_postulate "Parallel postulate"). The book was based on his father's thoughts and presented one of the earliest arguments for a non-Euclidean hypothesis equivalent to the parallel postulate. After reading this, Wallis then wrote about his ideas as he developed his own thoughts about the postulate, trying to prove it also with similar triangles.

He found that [Euclid's fifth postulate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallel_postulate "Parallel postulate") is equivalent to the one currently named "Wallis postulate" after him. This postulate states that "On a given finite straight line it is always possible to construct a triangle similar to a given triangle". This result was encompassed in a trend trying to deduce Euclid's fifth from the other four postulates which today is known to be impossible. Unlike other authors, he realised that the unbounded growth of a triangle was not guaranteed by the four first postulates.

### Calculator

Another aspect of Wallis's mathematical skills was his ability to do mental calculations. He slept badly and often did mental calculations as he lay awake in his bed. One night he calculated in his head the square root of a number with 53 digits. In the morning he dictated the 27-digit square root of the number, still entirely from memory. It was a feat that was considered remarkable, and [Henry Oldenburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Oldenburg "Henry Oldenburg"), the Secretary of the Royal Society, sent a colleague to investigate how Wallis did it. It was considered important enough to merit discussion in the *Philosophical Transactions* of the Royal Society of 1685.

### Prince Rupert's Cube

Wallis demonstrated a solution to the *[Prince Rupert's cube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prince_Rupert's_cube "Prince Rupert's cube")*, which involves finding the largest cube that can pass through a hole cut through a unit cube without splitting it into separate pieces.

## Music theory

Wallis translated into Latin works of [Ptolemy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemy "Ptolemy") and [Bryennius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manuel_Bryennios "Manuel Bryennios"), and Porphyrius's commentary on Ptolemy. He also published three letters to [Henry Oldenburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Oldenburg "Henry Oldenburg") concerning tuning. He approved of [equal temperament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equal_temperament "Equal temperament"), which was being used in England's organs.

## Wallis as a cryptographer

While employed as Lady Vere's chaplain in 1642, Wallis was given an enciphered letter about the fall of [Chichester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Chichester "Siege of Chichester") which he managed to decipher within two hours. This started his career as a cryptographer. He was a moderate supporter of the Parliamentarian side in the [First English Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_English_Civil_War "First English Civil War") and therefore worked as a decipherer of intercepted correspondence for the Parliamentarian leaders. For his services, he was rewarded with the [Livings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benefice "Benefice") of [St. Gabriel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Gabriel_Fenchurch "St Gabriel Fenchurch") and [St. Martin's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Martin-in-the-Fields "St Martin-in-the-Fields") in [London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London "London").

Because of his Parliamentarian sympathies, Wallis was not employed as a cryptographer after the [Stuart Restoration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stuart_Restoration "Stuart Restoration"); after the [Glorious Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glorious_Revolution "Glorious Revolution"), he was sought out by [Lord Nottingham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Finch,_2nd_Earl_of_Nottingham "Daniel Finch, 2nd Earl of Nottingham") and frequently employed to decipher encrypted intercepted correspondence, though he thought that he was not always adequately rewarded for his work. King [William III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_III_of_England "William III of England") from 1689 also employed Wallis as a cryptographer, sometimes almost on a daily basis. Couriers would bring him letters to be decrypted and waited in front of his study for the product. The king took a personal interest in Wallis' work and well-being as witnessed by a letter he sent to Dutch [Grand pensionary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_pensionary "Grand pensionary") [Anthonie Heinsius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthonie_Heinsius "Anthonie Heinsius") in 1689.

In these early days of the [Williamite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Williamite "Williamite") reign, directly obtaining foreign intercepted letters was a problem for the English, as they did not have the resources of foreign [*Black Chambers*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabinet_noir "Cabinet noir") as yet, but allies like the [Elector of Brandenburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_I_of_Prussia "Frederick I of Prussia") without their own Black Chambers occasionally made gifts of such intercepted correspondence, like the letter of king [Louis XIV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_XIV "Louis XIV") of France to king [John III Sobieski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_III_Sobieski "John III Sobieski") of [Poland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Polish–Lithuanian_Commonwealth_(1648–1764) "History of the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth (1648–1764)") that king William in 1689 used to cause a crisis in French-Polish diplomatic relations. He was quite open about it and Wallis was rewarded for his role. But Wallis became nervous that the French might take action against him.

Wallis' relationship with the German mathematician [Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gottfried_Wilhelm_Leibniz "Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz") was cordial. But Leibniz also had cryptographic interests and tried to get Wallis to divulge some of his trade secrets, which Wallis declined to do as a matter of patriotic principle.

Smith gives an example of the painstaking work that Wallis performed, as described by himself in an August 1689 letter to [Richard Hampden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Hampden "Richard Hampden"). In it he gives a detailed account of his work on a particular letter and the parts he found difficult.

Wallis' correspondence also shows details of the way he stood up for himself, when he thought he was under-appreciated, financially or otherwise. He lobbied enthusiastically, both on his own behalf, and that of his relatives, as witnessed by letters to Lord Nottingham, Richard Hampden and the [MP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Member_of_parliament "Member of parliament") [Harbord Harbord](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harbord_Harbord "Harbord Harbord") that Smith quotes. In a letter to the English envoy to Prussia, [James Johnston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Johnston_(Secretary_of_State) "James Johnston (Secretary of State)") Wallis bitterly complains that a courtier of the Prussian Elector, by the name of Smetteau, had done him wrong in the matter of just compensation for services rendered to the Elector. In the letter he gives details of what he had done and gives advice on a simple [substitution cipher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Substitution_cipher "Substitution cipher") for the use of Johnston himself.

Wallis' contributions to the art of cryptography were not only of a "technological" character. De Leeuw points out that even the "purely scientific" contributions of Wallis to the science of [linguistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linguistics "Linguistics") in the field of the "rationality" of [Natural language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_language "Natural language") as it developed over time, played a role in the development of cryptology as a science. Wallis' development of a model of English grammar, independent of earlier models based on Latin grammar, is a case in point of the way other sciences helped develop cryptology in his view.

Wallis tried to teach his own son John, and his grandson by his daughter Anne, [William Blencowe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Blencowe "William Blencowe") the tricks of the trade. With William he was so successful that he could persuade the government to allow the grandson to get the [survivorship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concurrent_estate "Concurrent estate") of the annual pension of £100 Wallis had received in compensation for his cryptographic work.

William Blencowe eventually succeeded Wallis as official Cryptographer to Queen [Anne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anne_of_England "Anne of England") after Wallis' death in 1703.
