**Charles Hermite** (French: [[ʃaʁl ɛʁmit]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/French "Help:IPA/French"); 24 December 1822 – 14 January 1901) was a French [mathematician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematician "Mathematician") who studied analysis, number theory, and algebra. One of his most remarkable achievements was the proof of the [transcendence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transcendental_number "Transcendental number") of the number *[e](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E_(mathematical_constant) "E (mathematical constant)")*.

## Life

Hermite was born in [Dieuze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dieuze "Dieuze"), [Moselle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moselle_(department) "Moselle (department)"), on 24 December 1822, with a deformity in his right foot that would impair his [gait](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gait "Gait") throughout his life. He was the sixth of seven children of Ferdinand Hermite and his wife, Madeleine [née](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Née "Née") Lallemand. Ferdinand worked in the drapery business of Madeleine's family while also pursuing a career as an artist. The drapery business relocated to [Nancy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nancy,_France "Nancy, France") in 1828, and so did the family.

Hermite obtained his [secondary education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secondary_education "Secondary education") at [Collège de Nancy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nancy-Université "Nancy-Université") and then, in Paris, at [Collège Henri IV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lycée_Henri-IV "Lycée Henri-IV") and at the [Lycée Louis-le-Grand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lycée_Louis-le-Grand "Lycée Louis-le-Grand"). He read some of [Joseph-Louis Lagrange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph-Louis_Lagrange "Joseph-Louis Lagrange")'s writings on the solution of numerical equations and [Carl Friedrich Gauss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Friedrich_Gauss "Carl Friedrich Gauss")'s publications on [number theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_theory "Number theory").

Hermite wanted to take his higher education at [École Polytechnique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/École_Polytechnique "École Polytechnique"), a [military academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_academy "Military academy") renowned for excellence in mathematics, science, and engineering. Tutored by mathematician [Eugène Charles Catalan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugène_Charles_Catalan "Eugène Charles Catalan"), Hermite devoted a year to preparing for the notoriously difficult [entrance examination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Educational_entrance_examination "Educational entrance examination"). In 1842 he was admitted to the school. However, after one year the school would not allow Hermite to continue his studies there because of his deformed foot. He struggled to regain his admission to the school, but the administration imposed strict conditions. Hermite did not accept this, and he quit the École Polytechnique without graduating.

After spending five years working privately towards his degree, in which he befriended eminent mathematicians [Joseph Bertrand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Bertrand "Joseph Bertrand"), [Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Gustav_Jacob_Jacobi "Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi"), and [Joseph Liouville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Liouville "Joseph Liouville"), he took and passed the examinations for the [baccalauréat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baccalauréat "Baccalauréat"), which he was awarded in 1847. He married Bertrand's sister, Louise, in 1848.

In 1848, Hermite returned to the École Polytechnique as *répétiteur and examinateur d'admission*. In July 1848, he was elected to the [French Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Academy_of_Sciences "French Academy of Sciences"). In 1856 he contracted smallpox. Through the influence of [Augustin-Louis Cauchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustin-Louis_Cauchy "Augustin-Louis Cauchy") and of a nun who nursed him, he resumed the practice of his [Catholic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic "Catholic") faith. From 1862 to 1873 he was lecturer at the [École Normale Supérieure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/École_Normale_Supérieure "École Normale Supérieure"). In 1869, he succeeded [Jean-Marie Duhamel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Marie_Duhamel "Jean-Marie Duhamel") as professor of mathematics, both at the École Polytechnique, where he remained until 1876, and at the [University of Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Paris "University of Paris"), where he remained until his death. Upon his 70th birthday, he was promoted to grand officer in the French [Legion of Honour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legion_of_Honour "Legion of Honour").

He was elected to honorary membership of the [Manchester Literary and Philosophical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manchester_Literary_and_Philosophical_Society "Manchester Literary and Philosophical Society") in 1892. on the same date as [Charles Friedel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Friedel "Charles Friedel"), also of the Sorbonne.

On his last days Hermite suffered from a bout of [asthma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asthma "Asthma"), a weak appetite, and poor sleep. He died in [Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paris "Paris") on 14 January 1901, aged 78.

## Contribution to mathematics

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/65/Charles_Hermite_circa_1887.jpg/250px-Charles_Hermite_circa_1887.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Charles_Hermite_circa_1887.jpg)

Charles Hermite circa 1887

In 1842, *[Nouvelles Annales de Mathématiques](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nouvelles_Annales_de_Mathématiques "Nouvelles Annales de Mathématiques")* published Hermite's first original contribution to mathematics, a simple proof of [Niels Henrik Abel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niels_Henrik_Abel "Niels Henrik Abel")'s proposition concerning the impossibility of an algebraic solution to [equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equation "Equation") of the [fifth degree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quintic_function "Quintic function"), the [Abel–Ruffini theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abel–Ruffini_theorem "Abel–Ruffini theorem"). In a series of papers published in 1858, 1865, and 1866, Hermite showed that roots of the quintic polynomial could be obtained using [elliptic integrals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptic_integral "Elliptic integral").

Since his student days, Hermite was deeply interested in [Abelian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abelian_function "Abelian function") and [elliptic functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptic_function "Elliptic function"), and, with encouragement from Joseph Liouville, corresponded with [Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Gustav_Jacob_Jacobi "Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi") on these topics. This resulted in the inclusion, into the complete edition of Jacobi's works, of two articles by Hermite, one concerning the extension to Abelian functions of one of the theorems of Abel on elliptic functions, and the other concerning the transformation of elliptic functions.

In higher algebra, Hermite, [Arthur Cayley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Cayley "Arthur Cayley"), and [James Joseph Sylvester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Joseph_Sylvester "James Joseph Sylvester") simultaneously developed the theory of [invariants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invariant_theory "Invariant theory"), where Hermite discovered the [law of reciprocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reciprocity_law "Reciprocity law").

Hermite introduced the notion of an [orthogonal matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonal_matrix "Orthogonal matrix"), which is equal to the inverse of its [transpose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transpose "Transpose"), in 1854, though the modern formal definition was first stated by 1878 by [Ferdinand Georg Frobenius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferdinand_Georg_Frobenius "Ferdinand Georg Frobenius"). In 1855, Hermite proved that the [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalues_and_eigenvectors "Eigenvalues and eigenvectors") of matrices equal to their own complex conjugate transposes, [Hermitian matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermitian_matrix "Hermitian matrix"), are always real, thereby generalizing the 1829 result of Cauchy for

In 1864, Hermite presented a new class of special functions, [Hermite polynomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermite_polynomials "Hermite polynomials"), in the context of expansions in terms of continuous functions over unbounded intervals. Hermite functions, which are the product of Hermite polynomials and a [Gaussian function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_function "Gaussian function"), find application in quantum mechanics, wherein they arise as solutions to the [Schrödinger equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schrödinger_equation "Schrödinger equation") for the [quantum harmonic oscillator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_harmonic_oscillator "Quantum harmonic oscillator").

In 1873, he published a lengthy paper demonstrating in two different ways that that [*e*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E_(mathematical_constant) "E (mathematical constant)"), the base of the [natural logarithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_logarithm "Natural logarithm"), is [transcendental](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transcendental_number "Transcendental number"), based on prior work by Joseph Liouville. Later in his life, Hermite turned his attention to the calculus, in particular, the theory of [linear differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_differential_equation "Linear differential equation"), and found solutions to [Lamé's equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lamé_function "Lamé function").

## Legacy

In addition to the mathematics properties named in his honor, the [Hermite crater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermite_(crater) "Hermite (crater)") near the [Moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moon "Moon")'s north pole is named after Hermite.
