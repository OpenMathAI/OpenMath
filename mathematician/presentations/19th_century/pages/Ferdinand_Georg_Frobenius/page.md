**Ferdinand Georg Frobenius** (26 October 1849 – 3 August 1917) was a [German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germans "Germans") [mathematician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematician "Mathematician"), best known for his contributions to the theory of [elliptic functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptic_functions "Elliptic functions"), [differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_equations "Differential equations"), [number theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_theory "Number theory"), and to [group theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_theory "Group theory"). He is known for the famous determinantal identities, known as Frobenius–Stickelberger formulae, governing elliptic functions, and for developing the theory of biquadratic forms. He was also the first to introduce the notion of [rational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_function "Rational function") approximations of functions (nowadays known as [Padé approximants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Padé_approximant "Padé approximant")), and gave the first full proof for the [Cayley–Hamilton theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cayley–Hamilton_theorem "Cayley–Hamilton theorem"). He also lent his name to certain differential-geometric objects in modern [mathematical physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_physics "Mathematical physics"), known as [Frobenius manifolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frobenius_manifold "Frobenius manifold").

## Biography

Ferdinand Georg Frobenius was born on 26 October 1849 in [Charlottenburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlottenburg "Charlottenburg"), a suburb of [Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berlin "Berlin"), from parents Christian Ferdinand Frobenius, a [Protestant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestant "Protestant") [parson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parson "Parson"), and Christine Elizabeth Friedrich. He entered the [Joachimsthal Gymnasium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joachimsthal_Gymnasium "Joachimsthal Gymnasium") in 1860 when he was nearly eleven.

In 1867, after graduating, he went to the [University of Göttingen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Göttingen "University of Göttingen"), where he began his university studies. However, he studied there for only one semester before returning to Berlin, where he attended lectures by [Leopold Kronecker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leopold_Kronecker "Leopold Kronecker"), [Ernst Kummer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernst_Kummer "Ernst Kummer") and [Karl Weierstrass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Weierstrass "Karl Weierstrass"). He received his doctorate (awarded with distinction) in 1870 supervised by Weierstrass. His thesis was on the solution of differential equations. In 1874, after having taught at secondary school level — first at the Joachimsthal Gymnasium, then at the Sophienrealschule — he was appointed to the University of Berlin as an extraordinary professor of mathematics.

Frobenius was in Berlin only a year before he went to [Zürich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zürich "Zürich") to take up an appointment as an ordinary professor at the [Polytechnikum, today's ETH Zurich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ETH_Zurich "ETH Zurich"). For seventeen years, between 1875 and 1892, Frobenius worked in Zürich. It was there that he married, brought up his family, and did much important work in widely differing areas of mathematics.

In the last days of December 1891, Kronecker died, and therefore his chair in Berlin became vacant. Weierstrass, strongly believing that Frobenius was the right person to keep Berlin in the forefront of mathematics, used his considerable influence to have Frobenius appointed. In 1893 he returned to Berlin, where he was elected to the [Prussian Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prussian_Academy_of_Sciences "Prussian Academy of Sciences").

## Contributions to group theory

[Group theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_theory "Group theory") was one of Frobenius' principal interests in the second half of his career. One of his first contributions was the proof of the [Sylow theorems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sylow_theorems "Sylow theorems") for abstract groups. Earlier proofs had been for [permutation groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permutation_group "Permutation group"). His proof of the first Sylow theorem (on the existence of Sylow groups) is one of those frequently used today.

- Frobenius also proved the following fundamental theorem: If a positive integer n divides the [order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_a_group "Order of a group") |G|  of a [finite group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_group "Finite group") G, then the number of solutions of the equation  xn = 1  in G is equal to  k n  for some positive integer k .

- He also posed the following conjecture: If, in the above theorem,  k = 1 , then the solutions of the equation  xn = 1  in G form a subgroup.

Many years ago this conjecture was proved correct for [solvable groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solvable_group "Solvable group"). Only in 1991, after the [classification of finite simple groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classification_of_finite_simple_groups "Classification of finite simple groups"), was this problem solved in general.

More important was his creation of the theory of [group characters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Character_theory "Character theory") and [group representations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_representation "Group representation"), which are fundamental tools for studying the structure of groups. This work led to the notion of [Frobenius reciprocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Character_theory "Character theory") and the definition of what are now called [Frobenius groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frobenius_group "Frobenius group"). ## Contributions to number theory

Frobenius introduced a canonical way of turning primes into [conjugacy classes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjugacy_classes "Conjugacy classes") in [Galois groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galois_group "Galois group") over **Q**. Specifically, if *K*/**Q** is a finite Galois extension then to each (positive) prime *p* which does not [ramify](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramification_(mathematics) "Ramification (mathematics)") in *K* and to each prime ideal *P* lying over *p* in *K* there is a unique element *g* of Gal(*K*/**Q**) satisfying the condition *g*(*x*) = *x**p* (mod *P*) for all integers *x* of *K*. Varying *P* over *p* changes *g* into a conjugate (and every conjugate of *g* occurs in this way), so the conjugacy class of *g* in the Galois group is canonically associated to *p*. This is called the Frobenius conjugacy class of *p* and any element of the conjugacy class is called a Frobenius element of *p*. If we take for *K* the *m*th [cyclotomic field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclotomic_field "Cyclotomic field"), whose Galois group over **Q** is the units modulo *m* (and thus is abelian, so conjugacy classes become elements), then for *p* not dividing *m* the Frobenius class in the Galois group is *p* mod *m*. From this point of view, the distribution of Frobenius conjugacy classes in Galois groups over **Q** (or, more generally, Galois groups over any number field) generalizes Dirichlet's classical result about primes in arithmetic progressions. The study of Galois groups of infinite-degree extensions of **Q** depends crucially on this construction of Frobenius elements, which provides in a sense a dense subset of elements which are accessible to detailed study.

## Differential equations

Frobenius made important contributions to the solution
of linear variable-coefficient ordinary differential equations
by elaborating on the resolution of power-series methods
applied at singular-points where standard Taylor-series methods fail. His algorithm is now referred to as the [Frobenius method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frobenius_method "Frobenius method").
