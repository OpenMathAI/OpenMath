**Jean-Victor Poncelet** (French pronunciation: [[ʒɑ̃ viktɔʁ pɔ̃slɛ]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/French "Help:IPA/French"); 1 July 1788 – 22 December 1867) was a French [engineer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineer "Engineer") and [mathematician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematician "Mathematician") who served most notably as the Commanding General of the [École Polytechnique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/École_Polytechnique "École Polytechnique"). He is considered a reviver of [projective geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_geometry "Projective geometry"), and his work *Traité des propriétés projectives des figures* is considered the first definitive text on the subject since [Gérard Desargues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gérard_Desargues "Gérard Desargues")' work on it in the 17th century. He later wrote an introduction to it: *Applications d'analyse et de géométrie*.

As a mathematician, his most notable work was in [projective geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_geometry "Projective geometry"), although an early collaboration with [Charles Julien Brianchon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Julien_Brianchon "Charles Julien Brianchon") provided a significant contribution to [Feuerbach's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feuerbach's_theorem "Feuerbach's theorem"). He also made discoveries about [projective harmonic conjugates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_harmonic_conjugates "Projective harmonic conjugates"); relating these to the poles and polar lines associated with [conic sections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conic_section "Conic section"). He developed the concept of parallel lines meeting at a [point at infinity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_at_infinity "Point at infinity") and defined the [circular points at infinity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circular_points_at_infinity "Circular points at infinity") that are on every circle of the plane. These discoveries led to the [principle of duality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duality_(projective_geometry) "Duality (projective geometry)"), and the [principle of continuity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principle_of_continuity "Principle of continuity") and also aided in the development of [complex numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_number "Complex number").

As a military engineer, he served in [Napoleon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleon "Napoleon")'s campaign against the [Russian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Empire "Russian Empire") in 1812, in which he was captured and held prisoner until 1814. Later, he served as a [professor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Professor "Professor") of [mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanics "Mechanics") at the École d'application in his home town of [Metz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metz "Metz"), during which time he published *Introduction à la mécanique industrielle*, a work he is famous for, and improved the design of [turbines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turbine "Turbine") and [water wheels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_wheel "Water wheel"). While a professor of applied mechanics, he also, independently from [Coriolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaspard-Gustave_de_Coriolis "Gaspard-Gustave de Coriolis"), pioneered the use of [work](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Work_(physics) "Work (physics)") in mechanics and the [work-energy theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Work_(physics)#Work–energy_principle "Work (physics)"), including coining the term "mechanical work". In 1837, a tenured 'Chaire de mécanique physique et expérimentale' was specially created for him at the Sorbonne (the [University of Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Paris "University of Paris")). In 1848, he became the commanding general of his *alma mater*, the [École Polytechnique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/École_Polytechnique "École Polytechnique"). He is honoured by having his name listed among notable French engineers and scientists displayed around the first stage of the [Eiffel tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_the_72_names_on_the_Eiffel_Tower "List of the 72 names on the Eiffel Tower").

## Biography

### Birth, education, and capture (1788–1814)

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/8e/Clo%C3%AEtre_sainte_Constance_%28lyc%C3%A9e_Fabert_de_Metz%29.JPG/250px-Clo%C3%AEtre_sainte_Constance_%28lyc%C3%A9e_Fabert_de_Metz%29.JPG?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Cloître_sainte_Constance_(lycée_Fabert_de_Metz).JPG)

The [Lycée Fabert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lycée_Fabert "Lycée Fabert") in [Metz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metz "Metz"), where Poncelet was fellow student.

Poncelet was born in [Metz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metz "Metz"), France, on 1 July 1788, the [illegitimate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legitimacy_(family_law) "Legitimacy (family law)") then legitimated son of Claude Poncelet, a [lawyer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawyer "Lawyer") of the Parliament of Metz and wealthy landowner. His mother, Anne-Marie Perrein, had a more modest background. At a young age, he was sent to live with the Olier family at [Saint-Avold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint-Avold "Saint-Avold"). He returned to Metz for his secondary education, at [Lycée Fabert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lycée_Fabert "Lycée Fabert"). After this, he attended the [École Polytechnique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/École_Polytechnique "École Polytechnique"), a prestigious school in [Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paris "Paris"), from 1808 to 1810, though he fell behind in his studies in his third year due to poor health. After graduation, he joined the Corps of [Military](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military "Military") [Engineers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineer "Engineer"). He attended the École d'application in his hometown during this time, and achieved the rank of [lieutenant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lieutenant "Lieutenant") in the [French Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Army "French Army") the same year he graduated.

Poncelet took part in [Napoleon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleon_I_of_France "Napoleon I of France")'s [invasion of Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_invasion_of_Russia "French invasion of Russia") in 1812. His biographer [Didion](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isidore%20Didion "fr:Isidore Didion") writes that he was part of the group which was cut from Marshal [Michel Ney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michel_Ney "Michel Ney")'s army at the [Battle of Krasnoi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Krasnoi "Battle of Krasnoi") and was forced to capitulate to the Russians, though other sources say that he was left for dead. Upon capture, he was interrogated by [General](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General "General") [Mikhail Andreyevich Miloradovich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikhail_Andreyevich_Miloradovich "Mikhail Andreyevich Miloradovich"), but he did not disclose any information. The Russians held him as a [prisoner of war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prisoner_of_war "Prisoner of war") and confined him at [Saratov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saratov "Saratov"). During his imprisonment, in the years 1813–1814, he wrote his most notable work, *Traité des propriétés projectives des figures*, which outlined the foundations of projective geometry, as well as some new results. Poncelet, however, could not publish it until after his release in 1814.

### Release and later employment (1822–1848)

In 1815, the year after his release, Poncelet was employed a military engineer at his hometown of Metz. In 1822, while at this position, he published *Traité des propriétés projectives des figures*. This was the first major work to discuss [projective geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_geometry "Projective geometry") since Desargues', though [Gaspard Monge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaspard_Monge "Gaspard Monge") had written a few minor works about it previously. It is considered the founding work of modern projective geometry. [Joseph Diaz Gergonne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Diaz_Gergonne "Joseph Diaz Gergonne") also wrote about this branch of geometry at approximately the same time, beginning in 1810. Poncelet published several papers about the subject in [Annales de Gergonne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annales_de_Gergonne "Annales de Gergonne") (officially known as *Annales de mathématiques pures et appliquées*). However, Poncelet and Gergonne ultimately engaged in a bitter priority dispute over the Principle of Duality.

In 1825, he became the professor of [mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanics "Mechanics") at the École d'Application in Metz, a position he held until 1835. During his tenure at this school, he improved the design of [turbines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turbine "Turbine") and [water wheels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_wheel "Water wheel"), deriving his work from the mechanics of the [Provençal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Provence "Provence") mill from southern France. Although the turbine of his design was not constructed until 1838, he envisioned such a design twelve years previous to that. In 1835, he left École d'Application, and in December 1837 became a tenured professor at Sorbonne (the [University of Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Paris "University of Paris")), where a 'Chaire de mécanique physique et expérimentale' was specially created for him with the support of [Louis Jacques Thénard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Jacques_Thénard "Louis Jacques Thénard").

### Commanding General at École Polytechnique (1848–1867)

In 1848, Poncelet became the Commanding General of his *alma mater*, the École Polytechnique. He held the position until 1850, when he retired.

During this time, he wrote *Applications d'analyse et de géométrie*, which served as an introduction to his earlier work *Traité des propriétés projectives des figures*. It was published in two volumes in 1862 and 1864. He was elected a Foreign Honorary Member of the [American Academy of Arts and Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Academy_of_Arts_and_Sciences "American Academy of Arts and Sciences") in 1865.

### Poncelet–Steiner theorem

Poncelet discovered the following theorem in 1822: [Euclidean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclid "Euclid") [compass and straightedge constructions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compass_and_straightedge_constructions "Compass and straightedge constructions") can be carried out using only a [straightedge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Straightedge "Straightedge") if a single [circle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circle "Circle") and its center is given. Swiss mathematician [Jakob Steiner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jakob_Steiner "Jakob Steiner") proved this theorem in 1833, leading to the name of the theorem. The constructions that this theorem states are possible are known as Steiner constructions.

### Poncelet's porism

In [geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometry "Geometry"), **Poncelet's porism** (sometimes referred to as **Poncelet's closure theorem**) states that whenever a [polygon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polygon "Polygon") is [inscribed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inscribed_figure "Inscribed figure") in one [conic section](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conic_section "Conic section") and [circumscribes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumscribe "Circumscribe") another one, the polygon must be part of an infinite family of polygons that are all inscribed in and circumscribe the same two conics.
