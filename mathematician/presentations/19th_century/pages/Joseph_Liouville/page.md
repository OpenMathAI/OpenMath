**Joseph Liouville** ([/ˌliːuˈvɪl/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English") [*LEE-oo-VIL*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:Pronunciation_respelling_key "Help:Pronunciation respelling key"); French: [[ʒozɛf ljuvil]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/French "Help:IPA/French"); 24 March 1809 – 8 September 1882) was a French mathematician who worked on a number of different fields in mathematics, including [number theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_theory "Number theory"), [complex analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_analysis "Complex analysis"), and [mathematical physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_physics "Mathematical physics"). Many mathematical concepts are named after him, including [Liouville's theorem in Hamiltonian mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liouville's_theorem_(Hamiltonian) "Liouville's theorem (Hamiltonian)") and [Liouville's theorem for complex numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liouville's_theorem_(complex_analysis) "Liouville's theorem (complex analysis)").

## Life and work

He was born in [Saint-Omer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint-Omer,_Pas-de-Calais "Saint-Omer, Pas-de-Calais") in France on 24 March 1809. His parents were Claude-Joseph Liouville (an army officer) and Thérèse Liouville (née Balland).

Liouville gained admission to the [École Polytechnique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/École_Polytechnique "École Polytechnique") in 1825 and graduated in 1827. Just like [Augustin-Louis Cauchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustin-Louis_Cauchy "Augustin-Louis Cauchy") before him, Liouville studied engineering at [École des Ponts et Chaussées](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/École_des_Ponts_et_Chaussées "École des Ponts et Chaussées") after graduating from the Polytechnique, but opted instead for a career in mathematics. After some years as an assistant at various institutions including the [École Centrale Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/École_Centrale_Paris "École Centrale Paris"), he was appointed as professor at the École Polytechnique in 1838. He began delivering lectures on mathematics at the [Collège de France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collège_de_France "Collège de France") in 1851 secured a chair in [rational mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_mechanics "Classical mechanics") at the Faculté des Sciences in 1857. However, he suffered under a heavy teaching load and his health started to deteriorate.

Liouville founded the *[Journal de Mathématiques Pures et Appliquées](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Journal_de_Mathématiques_Pures_et_Appliquées "Journal de Mathématiques Pures et Appliquées")* in 1836, basing it on *[Crelle's Journal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crelle's_Journal "Crelle's Journal")*. It soon became a leading mathematical periodical in France and became known as "Liouville's Journal" even after he had resigned as editor-in-chief in 1875. He was elected to the [French Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Academy_of_Sciences "French Academy of Sciences") in 1839, and became an associate member of the [Bureau des Longitudes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bureau_des_Longitudes "Bureau des Longitudes").

Liouville was also involved in politics for some time, and he became a member of the [Constituting Assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Assembly_of_France "National Assembly of France") in following the [1848 Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution_of_1848 "French Revolution of 1848"). However, following the rise of [Napoleon III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleon_III "Napoleon III") to power, Liouville ended his political activities.

In 1851, he was elected a foreign member of the [Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Swedish_Academy_of_Sciences "Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences"). In 1853, he was elected as a member of the [American Philosophical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Philosophical_Society "American Philosophical Society"). As a mathematician, he maintained contact with many foreign colleagues, including William Thomson ([Lord Kelvin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_Kelvin "Lord Kelvin")), [Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Gustav_Jacob_Jacobi "Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi"), and [Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Gustav_Lejeune_Dirichlet "Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet"). As a lecturer, he offered support and encouragement to many young talents, among them, [Charles Hermite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Hermite "Charles Hermite"), [Joseph Bertrand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Bertrand "Joseph Bertrand"), and [Joseph-Alfred Serret](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph-Alfred_Serret "Joseph-Alfred Serret").

## Contributions

### Analysis, algebra, and number theory

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/2b/Journal_math_liouville.jpg/250px-Journal_math_liouville.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Journal_math_liouville.jpg)

In 1838, Liouville published a method for establishing the existence of solutions to [ordinary differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordinary_differential_equation "Ordinary differential equation") of the second order involving successive approximations, now attached to the name of [Émile Picard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Émile_Picard "Émile Picard"), who gave [a more general approach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Picard's_Method "Picard's Method") in the early 1890s.

In algebra, Liouville was one of the first to grasp the significance of the contributions of the late [Évariste Galois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Évariste_Galois "Évariste Galois"), whose work had been forwarded to him by Auguste Chevalier, a friend of Galois. Liouville edited and published the work of Galois in his own journal in 1846, after which the [Galois theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galois_theory "Galois theory") attracted the attention of many mathematicians, among them, [Paolo Ruffini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paolo_Ruffini "Paolo Ruffini"), [Joseph-Alfred Serret](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph-Alfred_Serret "Joseph-Alfred Serret"), and [Augustin-Louis Cauchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustin-Louis_Cauchy "Augustin-Louis Cauchy"). By helping to popularize the works of Galois, Liouville participated, albeit indirectly, in the development of [modern algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstract_algebra "Abstract algebra") in general and [group theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_theory "Group theory") in particular.

Research on the solutions of algebraic equations spurred interest in [algebraic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_number "Algebraic number") and transcendental [irrational numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irrational_number "Irrational number"). In 1844, Liouville was the first to prove the existence of [transcendental numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transcendental_number "Transcendental number"). He did so by demonstrating some results on approximating algebraic irrationals using rational numbers and established [an inequality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liouville_number "Liouville number") that served as a criterion for transcendence. In essence, his inequality proclaims that rationals are poor approximations of irrational algebraic numbers. He then gave an explicit example. He showed that any number of the form

The [Liouville function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liouville_function "Liouville function"), an important concept in number theory, is named in his honor.

In his work on [elliptic integrals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptic_integral "Elliptic integral"), he based the whole subject on the general properties of [doubly periodic functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doubly_periodic_function "Doubly periodic function") and he demonstrated the [transcendence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transcendental_number "Transcendental number") of [Abelian functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abelian_integral "Abelian integral"). It was in this context that he discovered [Liouville's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liouville's_theorem_(complex_analysis) "Liouville's theorem (complex analysis)") in complex analysis, bounded [entire functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entire_function "Entire function") are constant. He also gave a short proof of the [fundamental theorem of algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_theorem_of_algebra "Fundamental theorem of algebra").

A similar result is Liouville's theorem for [harmonic functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harmonic_function "Harmonic function"), or solutions to [Laplace's equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laplace's_equation "Laplace's equation"). It states that bounded harmonic functions in Euclidean space are constant. [Edward Nelson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Nelson "Edward Nelson") gave a short proof in 1961, exploiting the mean-value property of harmonic functions.

### Mathematical physics

Since the middle eighteenth century, mathematicians and physicists had been studying a variety of [partial differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_differential_equation "Partial differential equation") with [boundary values](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boundary_value_problem "Boundary value problem") using the [separation of variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Separation_of_variables#Partial_differential_equations "Separation of variables") to resolve them into systems ordinary differential equations, which carried their own parameters. Solutions found for specific values of these parameters, called [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalues_and_eigenvectors "Eigenvalues and eigenvectors"), were known as [eigenfunctions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenfunction "Eigenfunction"). The separation of variables in different coordinate systems led to new special functions, such as the [Bessel functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bessel_function "Bessel function") and [Legendre polynomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legendre_polynomials "Legendre polynomials"), as eigenfunctions of ordinary differential equations. Liouville and his friend, [Jacques Charles François Sturm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques_Charles_François_Sturm "Jacques Charles François Sturm"), sought to tackle the general problem for any linear differential equations of the second order. In a series papers published in the 1830s, the two men established the [Sturm–Liouville theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sturm–Liouville_theory "Sturm–Liouville theory"). Though the significance of this contribution took some time for mathematicians to grasp, it is now a standard procedure to solve certain types of [integral equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integral_equation "Integral equation"). Their work was inspired by the analysis of [heat diffusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_equation "Heat equation") in a cylinder by [Jean-Baptiste Joseph Fourier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Baptiste_Joseph_Fourier "Jean-Baptiste Joseph Fourier"), and may be viewed as a generalization of [Fourier analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourier_analysis "Fourier analysis"). In its original formulation, the Sturm–Liouville theory was not fully rigorous in that it did not adequately address the completeness of the set of eigenfunctions (or [orthogonal basis set)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonal_basis "Orthogonal basis") and the convergence of the expansion in terms of the eigenfunctions. These issues were subsequently addressed by mathematicians such as [Maxime Bôcher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxime_Bôcher "Maxime Bôcher"), [Max Mason](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Mason "Max Mason"), and [Garrett Birkhoff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garrett_Birkhoff "Garrett Birkhoff").

Independent of [Niels Henrik Abel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niels_Henrik_Abel "Niels Henrik Abel"), Liouville studied special integral equations and employed [a method of successive substitutions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liouville–Neumann_series "Liouville–Neumann series"), anticipating [Carl Neumann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Neumann "Carl Neumann") by a few decades. However, his contributions in this domain were subsequently subsumed by the work of [Vito Volterra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vito_Volterra "Vito Volterra"), a pioneer of the general theory of integral equations. Liouville's research on the Sturm–Liouville theory and linear integral equations constituted early advances in the study of [functional analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functional_analysis "Functional analysis").

In 1837, Liouville sought approximate solutions to linear second-order differential equations with spatially varying coefficients and obtained, in modern language, an [asymptotic series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asymptotic_expansion "Asymptotic expansion"). [George Green](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Green_(mathematician) "George Green (mathematician)") independently found this technique in the same year in a paper on water waves in a canal. The [Liouville–Green method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WKB_approximation "WKB approximation") was rediscovered in 1923 by [Harold Jeffreys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Jeffreys "Harold Jeffreys"), and again in 1926 by [Gregor Wentzel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregor_Wentzel "Gregor Wentzel"), [Hans Kramers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Kramers "Hans Kramers"), [Léon Brillouin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Léon_Brillouin "Léon Brillouin"), who were studying the [Schrödinger equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schrödinger_equation "Schrödinger equation") of quantum mechanics.

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/ea/Simple_Harmonic_Motion_Orbit.gif/250px-Simple_Harmonic_Motion_Orbit.gif?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Simple_Harmonic_Motion_Orbit.gif)

An animation illustrating Liouville's theorem for [simple harmonic motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_harmonic_motion "Simple harmonic motion"). The area of the phase-space portrait is constant.

Liouville was attracted to the study of mechanics thanks to the works of [William Rowan Hamilton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Rowan_Hamilton "William Rowan Hamilton") and [Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Gustav_Jacob_Jacobi "Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi"). Liouville proved in a 1838 paper on differential equations that [phase space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_space "Phase space") volume of a conservative mechanical system constant, a result now known as [Liouville's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liouville's_theorem_(Hamiltonian) "Liouville's theorem (Hamiltonian)") in [Hamiltonian mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamiltonian_mechanics "Hamiltonian mechanics"). Following [Josiah Willard Gibbs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josiah_Willard_Gibbs "Josiah Willard Gibbs"), Liouville's theorem is recognized a fundamental result for statistical mechanics. In a related context, Liouville introduced the notion of [action-angle coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action-angle_coordinates "Action-angle coordinates") as a description of completely [integrable systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrable_systems "Integrable systems"). The modern formulation of this is sometimes called the [Liouville–Arnold theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liouville–Arnold_theorem "Liouville–Arnold theorem"), and the underlying concept of integrability is referred to as Liouville integrability.

In his study of electrodynamics, Liouville developed the [Riemann–Liouville integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann–Liouville_integral "Riemann–Liouville integral") to consider differentiation and integration of a [fractional order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fractional_calculus "Fractional calculus").

He also studied [potential theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potential_theory "Potential theory") and ellipsoidal figures of equilibrium, including those of unequal axes, that is, [Jacobi ellipsoids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobi_ellipsoid "Jacobi ellipsoid").

His unpublished manuscripts indicated that he had already known of the [Rayleigh–Ritz method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rayleigh–Ritz_method "Rayleigh–Ritz method") for approximating the eigenvalues of a [boundary-value problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boundary_value_problem "Boundary value problem") as early as 1845, decades before William Strutt ([Lord Rayleigh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_Rayleigh "Lord Rayleigh")) introduced it his *Theory of Sound* (1877).
