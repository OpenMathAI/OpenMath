**Johann Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet** ([/ˌdɪərɪˈkleɪ/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English"); German: [[ləˈʒœn diʁiˈkleː]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Standard_German "Help:IPA/Standard German"); 13 February 1805 – 5 May 1859) was a German [mathematician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematician "Mathematician"). In [number theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_theory "Number theory"), he proved special cases of [Fermat's Last Theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermat's_Last_Theorem "Fermat's Last Theorem") and created [analytic number theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytic_number_theory "Analytic number theory"). In [analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_analysis "Mathematical analysis"), he advanced the theory of [Fourier series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourier_series "Fourier series") and was one of the first to give the modern formal definition of a [function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Function_(mathematics) "Function (mathematics)"). In mathematical physics, he studied [potential theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potential_theory "Potential theory"), [boundary-value problems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boundary-value_problem "Boundary-value problem"), [heat diffusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_diffusion "Heat diffusion"), and [hydrodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrodynamics "Hydrodynamics").

Although his surname is Lejeune Dirichlet, he is commonly referred to by his [mononym](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mononym "Mononym") **Dirichlet**, in particular for results named after him.

## Biography

### Early life (1805–1822)

Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet was born on 13 February 1805 in [Düren](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Düren "Düren"), a town on the left bank of the [Rhine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhine "Rhine") which at the time was part of the [First French Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_French_Empire "First French Empire"), reverting to [Prussia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prussia "Prussia") after the [Congress of Vienna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congress_of_Vienna "Congress of Vienna") in 1815. His father Johann Arnold Lejeune Dirichlet was the postmaster, merchant, and city councilor. His paternal grandfather had come to Düren from Richelette (or more likely [Richelle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richelle*?action=edit&redlink=1 "Richelle*") ), a small community 5 km (3 miles) north east of [Liège](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liège "Liège") in [Belgium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belgium "Belgium"), from which his surname "Lejeune Dirichlet" ("*le jeune de Richelette*", [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language "French language") for "the youth from Richelette") was derived.

Although his family was not wealthy and he was the youngest of seven children, his parents supported his education. They enrolled him in an elementary school and then private school in hope that he would later become a merchant. The young Dirichlet, who showed a strong interest in mathematics before age 12, persuaded his parents to allow him to continue his studies. In 1817 they sent him to the [Gymnasium Bonn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beethoven-Gymnasium_Bonn?action=edit&redlink=1 "Beethoven-Gymnasium Bonn") under the care of [Peter Joseph Elvenich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Joseph_Elvenich "Peter Joseph Elvenich"), a student his family knew. In 1820, Dirichlet moved to the [Jesuit Gymnasium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dreikönigsgymnasium "Dreikönigsgymnasium") in [Cologne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cologne "Cologne"), where his lessons with [Georg Ohm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georg_Ohm "Georg Ohm") helped widen his knowledge in mathematics. He left the gymnasium a year later with only a certificate, as his inability to speak fluent [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin "Latin") prevented him from earning the [Abitur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abitur "Abitur").

### Studies in Paris (1822–1826)

Dirichlet again persuaded his parents to provide further financial support for his studies in mathematics, against their wish for a career in law. As Germany provided little opportunity to study higher mathematics at the time, with only [Gauss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Friedrich_Gauss "Carl Friedrich Gauss") at the [University of Göttingen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Göttingen "University of Göttingen") who was nominally a professor of [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy "Astronomy") and anyway disliked teaching, Dirichlet decided to go to [Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paris "Paris") in May 1822. There he attended classes at the [Collège de France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collège_de_France "Collège de France") and at the [University of Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Paris "University of Paris"), learning mathematics from [Hachette](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Nicolas_Pierre_Hachette "Jean Nicolas Pierre Hachette") among others, while undertaking private study of Gauss's *[Disquisitiones Arithmeticae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disquisitiones_Arithmeticae "Disquisitiones Arithmeticae")*, a book he kept close for his entire life. In 1823 he was recommended to [General Maximilien Foy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximilien_Sébastien_Foy "Maximilien Sébastien Foy"), who hired him as a private tutor to teach his children [German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_language "German language"), the wage finally allowing Dirichlet to become independent from his parents' financial support.

His first original research, comprising part of a proof of [Fermat's Last Theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermat's_Last_Theorem "Fermat's Last Theorem") for the case *n* = 5, brought him immediate fame, being the first advance in the theorem since [Fermat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_de_Fermat "Pierre de Fermat")'s own proof of the case *n* = 4 and [Euler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonhard_Euler "Leonhard Euler")'s proof for *n* = 3. [Adrien-Marie Legendre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adrien-Marie_Legendre "Adrien-Marie Legendre"), one of the referees, soon completed the proof for this case; Dirichlet completed his own proof a short time after Legendre, and a few years later produced a full proof for the case *n* = 14. In June 1825 he was accepted to lecture on his partial proof for the case *n* = 5 at the [French Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Academy_of_Sciences "French Academy of Sciences"), an exceptional feat for a 20-year-old student with no degree. His lecture at the Academy had also put Dirichlet in close contact with [Fourier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Fourier "Joseph Fourier") and [Poisson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siméon_Denis_Poisson "Siméon Denis Poisson"), who raised his interest in [theoretical physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theoretical_physics "Theoretical physics"), especially Fourier's [analytic theory of heat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_equation "Heat equation").

### Back to Prussia, Breslau (1825–1828)

As General Foy died in November 1825 and he could not find any paying position in France, Dirichlet had to return to Prussia. Fourier and Poisson introduced him to [Alexander von Humboldt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_von_Humboldt "Alexander von Humboldt"), who had been called to join the court of King [Friedrich Wilhelm III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_William_III_of_Prussia "Frederick William III of Prussia"). Humboldt, planning to make [Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berlin "Berlin") a centre of science and research, immediately offered his help to Dirichlet, sending letters in his favour to the Prussian government and to the [Prussian Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prussian_Academy_of_Sciences "Prussian Academy of Sciences"). Humboldt also secured a recommendation letter from Gauss, who upon reading his memoir on Fermat's theorem wrote with an unusual amount of praise that "Dirichlet showed excellent talent". With the support of Humboldt and Gauss, Dirichlet was offered a teaching position at the [University of Breslau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Breslau "University of Breslau"). However, as he had not passed a doctoral dissertation, he submitted his memoir on the Fermat theorem as a thesis to the [University of Bonn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Bonn "University of Bonn"). Again his lack of fluency in Latin rendered him unable to hold the required public disputation of his thesis; after much discussion, the university decided to bypass the problem by awarding him an [honorary doctorate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honorary_doctorate "Honorary doctorate") in February 1827. Also, the Minister of Education granted him a dispensation for the Latin disputation required for the [Habilitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habilitation "Habilitation"). Dirichlet earned the Habilitation and lectured in the 1827–28 year as a [Privatdozent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Privatdozent "Privatdozent") at [Breslau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breslau "Breslau").

While in Breslau, Dirichlet continued his number-theoretic research, publishing important contributions to the [biquadratic reciprocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quartic_reciprocity "Quartic reciprocity") law which at the time was a focal point of Gauss's research. Alexander von Humboldt took advantage of these new results, which had also drawn enthusiastic praise from [Friedrich Bessel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Bessel "Friedrich Bessel"), to arrange for him the desired transfer to Berlin. Given Dirichlet's young age (he was 23 years old at the time), Humboldt was able to get him only a trial position at the [Prussian Military Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prussian_Military_Academy "Prussian Military Academy") in Berlin while remaining nominally employed by the University of Breslau. The probation was extended for three years until the position becoming definite in 1831.

### Marriage to Rebecka Mendelssohn

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/af/Rebecka_Mendelssohn_-_Zeichnung_von_Wilhelm_Hensel_1823.jpg/250px-Rebecka_Mendelssohn_-_Zeichnung_von_Wilhelm_Hensel_1823.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Rebecka_Mendelssohn_-_Zeichnung_von_Wilhelm_Hensel_1823.jpg)

Dirichlet was married in 1832 to Rebecka Mendelssohn. They had two children, Walter (born 1833) and Flora (born 1845). Drawing by [Wilhelm Hensel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilhelm_Hensel "Wilhelm Hensel"), 1823

After Dirichlet's move to Berlin, Humboldt introduced him to the [great salons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salon_(gathering) "Salon (gathering)") held by the banker [Abraham Mendelssohn Bartholdy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraham_Mendelssohn_Bartholdy "Abraham Mendelssohn Bartholdy") and his family. Their house was a weekly gathering point for Berlin artists and scientists, including Abraham's children [Felix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Felix_Mendelssohn "Felix Mendelssohn") and [Fanny Mendelssohn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fanny_Mendelssohn "Fanny Mendelssohn"), both outstanding musicians, and the painter [Wilhelm Hensel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilhelm_Hensel "Wilhelm Hensel") (Fanny's husband). Dirichlet showed great interest in Abraham's other daughter Rebecka, whom he married in 1832.

Rebecka Henriette Lejeune Dirichlet (née Rebecka Mendelssohn; 11 April 1811 – 1 December 1858) was a granddaughter of [Moses Mendelssohn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moses_Mendelssohn "Moses Mendelssohn") and the youngest sister of [Felix Mendelssohn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Felix_Mendelssohn "Felix Mendelssohn") and [Fanny Mendelssohn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fanny_Mendelssohn "Fanny Mendelssohn"). Rebecka was born in [Hamburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamburg "Hamburg"). In 1816 her parents arranged for her to be [baptised](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baptism "Baptism") at which point she took the names Rebecka Henriette Mendelssohn Bartholdy. She became a part of the notable *[salon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salon_(gathering) "Salon (gathering)")* of her parents, [Abraham Mendelssohn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraham_Mendelssohn "Abraham Mendelssohn") and his wife Lea, having social contacts with the important musicians, artists and scientists in a highly creative period of German intellectual life. In 1829 she sang a small role in the premiere, given at the Mendelssohn house, of Felix's [Singspiel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singspiel "Singspiel") *[Die Heimkehr aus der Fremde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Die_Heimkehr_aus_der_Fremde "Die Heimkehr aus der Fremde").* She later wrote:

> My older brother and sister stole my reputation as an artist. In any other family I would have been highly regarded as a musician and perhaps been leader of a group. Next to Felix and Fanny, I could not aspire to any recognition.

In 1832 she married Dirichlet, who was introduced to the [Mendelssohn family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mendelssohn_family "Mendelssohn family") by [Alexander von Humboldt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_von_Humboldt "Alexander von Humboldt"). In 1833 their first son, Walter, was born. She died in [Göttingen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Göttingen "Göttingen") in 1858.

### Berlin (1826–1855)

As soon as he came to Berlin, Dirichlet applied to lecture at the [University of Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humboldt_University_of_Berlin "Humboldt University of Berlin"), and the Education Minister approved the transfer and in 1831 assigned him to the faculty of [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy "Philosophy"). The faculty required him to undertake a renewed [habilitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habilitation "Habilitation") qualification, and although Dirichlet wrote a *Habilitationsschrift* as needed, he postponed giving the mandatory lecture in Latin for another 20 years, until 1851. As he had not completed this formal requirement, he remained attached to the faculty with less than full rights, including restricted emoluments, forcing him to keep in parallel his teaching position at the Military School. In 1832 Dirichlet became a member of the [Prussian Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prussian_Academy_of_Sciences "Prussian Academy of Sciences"), the youngest member at only 27 years old.

Dirichlet had a good reputation with students for the clarity of his explanations and enjoyed teaching, especially as his University lectures tended to be on the more advanced topics in which he was doing research: number theory (he was the first German professor to give lectures on number theory), analysis and [mathematical physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_physics "Mathematical physics"). He advised the doctoral theses of several important German mathematicians, as [Gotthold Eisenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gotthold_Eisenstein "Gotthold Eisenstein"), [Leopold Kronecker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leopold_Kronecker "Leopold Kronecker"), [Rudolf Lipschitz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudolf_Lipschitz "Rudolf Lipschitz") and [Carl Wilhelm Borchardt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Wilhelm_Borchardt "Carl Wilhelm Borchardt"), while being influential in the mathematical formation of many other scientists, including [Elwin Bruno Christoffel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elwin_Bruno_Christoffel "Elwin Bruno Christoffel"), [Wilhelm Weber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilhelm_Eduard_Weber "Wilhelm Eduard Weber"), [Eduard Heine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eduard_Heine "Eduard Heine"), [Ludwig von Seidel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philipp_Ludwig_von_Seidel "Philipp Ludwig von Seidel") and [Julius Weingarten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julius_Weingarten "Julius Weingarten"). At the Military Academy, Dirichlet managed to introduce [differential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_calculus "Differential calculus") and [integral calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integral_calculus "Integral calculus") in the curriculum, raising the level of scientific education there. However, he gradually started feeling that his double teaching load, at the Military academy and at the university, was limiting the time available for his research.

While in Berlin, Dirichlet kept in contact with other mathematicians. In 1829, during a trip, he met [Carl Jacobi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Gustav_Jacob_Jacobi "Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi"), at the time professor of mathematics at [Königsberg University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Königsberg_University "Königsberg University"). Over the years they kept meeting and corresponding on research matters, in time becoming close friends. In 1839, during a visit to Paris, Dirichlet met [Joseph Liouville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Liouville "Joseph Liouville"), the two mathematicians becoming friends, keeping in contact and even visiting each other with the families a few years later. In 1839, Jacobi sent Dirichlet a paper by [Ernst Kummer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernst_Kummer "Ernst Kummer"), at the time a schoolteacher. Realizing Kummer's potential, they helped him get elected in the Berlin Academy and, in 1842, obtained for him a full professor position at the University of Breslau. In 1840 Kummer married Ottilie Mendelssohn, a cousin of Rebecka's.

In 1843, when Jacobi fell ill, Dirichlet traveled to Königsberg to help him, then obtained for him the assistance of [King Friedrich Wilhelm IV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_William_IV_of_Prussia "Frederick William IV of Prussia")'s personal physician. When the physician recommended that Jacobi spend some time in Italy, Dirichlet joined him on the trip together with his family. They were accompanied to Italy by [Ludwig Schläfli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludwig_Schläfli "Ludwig Schläfli"), who came as a translator; as he was strongly interested in mathematics, both Dirichlet and Jacobi lectured to him during the trip, and he later became an important mathematician himself. The Dirichlet family extended their stay in Italy to 1845, their daughter Flora being born there. In 1844, Jacobi moved to Berlin as a royal pensioner, their friendship becoming even closer. In 1846, when the [Heidelberg University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heidelberg_University "Heidelberg University") tried to recruit Dirichlet, Jacobi provided von Humboldt the needed support to obtain a doubling of Dirichlet's pay at the university in order to keep him in Berlin; however, even then he was not paid a full professor wage and could not leave the Military Academy.

Holding liberal views, Dirichlet and his family supported the [1848 revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolutions_of_1848_in_the_German_states "Revolutions of 1848 in the German states"); he even guarded with a rifle the palace of the Prince of Prussia. After the revolution failed, the Military Academy closed temporarily, causing him a large loss of income. When it reopened, the environment became more hostile to him, as officers he was teaching were expected to be loyal to the constituted government. Some of the press who had not sided with the revolution pointed him out, as well as Jacobi and other liberal professors, as "the red contingent of the staff".

In 1849 Dirichlet participated, together with his friend Jacobi, in the jubilee of Gauss's doctorate.

### Göttingen (1855–1859)

Despite Dirichlet's expertise and the honours he received, and even though, by 1851, he had finally completed all formal requirements for a full professor, the issue of raising his pay at the university still dragged on and he was still unable to leave the Military Academy. In 1855, upon Gauss's death, the [University of Göttingen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Göttingen "University of Göttingen") decided to call Dirichlet as his successor. Given the difficulties faced in Berlin, he decided to accept the offer and immediately moved to Göttingen with his family. [Kummer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernst_Kummer "Ernst Kummer") was called to assume his position as a professor of mathematics in Berlin.

Dirichlet enjoyed his time in Göttingen, as the lighter teaching load allowed him more time for research and he came into close contact with the new generation of researchers, especially [Richard Dedekind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Dedekind "Richard Dedekind") and [Bernhard Riemann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernhard_Riemann "Bernhard Riemann"). After moving to Göttingen he was able to obtain a small annual stipend for Riemann to retain him in the teaching staff there. Dedekind, Riemann, [Moritz Cantor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moritz_Cantor "Moritz Cantor") and [Alfred Enneper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Enneper "Alfred Enneper"), although they had all already earned their PhDs, attended Dirichlet's classes to study with him. Dedekind, who felt that there were gaps in his mathematics education, considered that the occasion to study with Dirichlet made him "a new human being". He later edited and published Dirichlet's lectures and other results in [number theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_theory "Number theory") under the title *[Vorlesungen über Zahlentheorie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vorlesungen_über_Zahlentheorie "Vorlesungen über Zahlentheorie")* (*Lectures on Number Theory*).

In the summer of 1858, during a trip to [Montreux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montreux "Montreux"), Dirichlet suffered a heart attack. On 5 May 1859, he died in Göttingen, several months after the death of his wife Rebecka. Dirichlet's brain is preserved in the department of physiology at the University of Göttingen, along with the brain of Gauss. The Academy in Berlin honored him with a formal memorial speech presented by Kummer in 1860, and later ordered the publication of his collected works edited by Kronecker and [Lazarus Fuchs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lazarus_Fuchs "Lazarus Fuchs").

## Mathematics research

### Number theory

[Number theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_theory "Number theory") was Dirichlet's main research interest, a field in which he found several deep results and in proving them introduced some fundamental tools, many of which were later named after him. In 1837, Dirichlet proved his [theorem on arithmetic progressions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirichlet's_theorem_on_arithmetic_progressions "Dirichlet's theorem on arithmetic progressions") using concepts from [mathematical analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_analysis "Mathematical analysis") to tackle an algebraic problem, thus creating the branch of [analytic number theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytic_number_theory "Analytic number theory"). In proving the theorem, he introduced the [Dirichlet characters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirichlet_character "Dirichlet character") and [L-functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirichlet_L-function "Dirichlet L-function"). In that article, he also noted the difference between the [absolute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolute_convergence "Absolute convergence") and [conditional convergence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conditional_convergence "Conditional convergence") of [series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Series_(mathematics) "Series (mathematics)") and its impact in what was later called the [Riemann series theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann_series_theorem "Riemann series theorem"). In 1841, he generalized his arithmetic progressions theorem from integers to the [ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_(mathematics) "Ring (mathematics)") of [Gaussian integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_integer "Gaussian integer")

In a couple of papers in 1838 and 1839, he proved the first [class number formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Class_number_formula "Class number formula") for [quadratic forms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_form "Quadratic form") (later refined by his student Kronecker). The formula, which Jacobi called a result "touching the utmost of human acumen", opened the way for similar results regarding more general [number fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_field "Number field"). Based on his research of the structure of the [unit group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_group "Unit group") of [quadratic fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_field "Quadratic field"), he proved the [Dirichlet unit theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirichlet_unit_theorem "Dirichlet unit theorem"), a fundamental result in [algebraic number theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_number_theory "Algebraic number theory").

He first used the [pigeonhole principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pigeonhole_principle "Pigeonhole principle"), a basic counting argument, in the proof of a theorem in [diophantine approximation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diophantine_approximation "Diophantine approximation"), later named after him [Dirichlet's approximation theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirichlet's_approximation_theorem "Dirichlet's approximation theorem"). He published important contributions to [Fermat's Last Theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermat's_Last_Theorem "Fermat's Last Theorem"), for which he proved the cases *n* = 5 and *n* = 14, and to the [biquadratic reciprocity law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quartic_reciprocity "Quartic reciprocity"). The [Dirichlet divisor problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirichlet_divisor_problem "Dirichlet divisor problem"), for which he found the first results by introducing the [Dirichlet hyperbola method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirichlet_hyperbola_method "Dirichlet hyperbola method"), is still an unsolved problem in number theory despite later contributions by other mathematicians.

### Analysis

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/2c/Fourier_Series.svg/250px-Fourier_Series.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Fourier_Series.svg)

Dirichlet found and proved the convergence conditions for Fourier series decomposition. Pictured: the first four Fourier series approximations for a [square wave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_wave_(waveform) "Square wave (waveform)").

Inspired by the work of his mentor in Paris, Dirichlet published in 1829 a famous memoir giving the [conditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirichlet_conditions "Dirichlet conditions"), showing for which functions the convergence of the [Fourier series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourier_series "Fourier series") holds. Before Dirichlet's solution, not only Fourier, but also Poisson and [Cauchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustin-Louis_Cauchy "Augustin-Louis Cauchy") had tried unsuccessfully to find a rigorous proof of convergence. The memoir pointed out Cauchy's mistake and introduced [Dirichlet's test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirichlet's_test "Dirichlet's test") for the convergence of series. It also introduced the [Dirichlet function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirichlet_function "Dirichlet function") as an example of a function that is not integrable (the [definite integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Definite_integral "Definite integral") was still a developing topic at the time) and, in the proof of the theorem for the Fourier series, introduced the [Dirichlet kernel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirichlet_kernel "Dirichlet kernel") and the [Dirichlet integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirichlet_integral "Dirichlet integral").

Dirichlet also studied the first [boundary-value problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boundary_value_problem "Boundary value problem"), for the [Laplace equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laplace_equation "Laplace equation"), proving the uniqueness of the solution; this type of problem in the theory of [partial differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_differential_equation "Partial differential equation") was later named the [Dirichlet problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirichlet_problem "Dirichlet problem") after him. A function satisfying a partial differential equation subject to the Dirichlet boundary conditions must have fixed values on the boundary. In the proof he notably used the principle that the solution is the function that minimizes the so-called [Dirichlet energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirichlet's_energy "Dirichlet's energy"). Riemann later named this approach the [Dirichlet principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirichlet_principle "Dirichlet principle"), although he knew it had also been used by Gauss and by [Lord Kelvin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Thomson,_1st_Baron_Kelvin "William Thomson, 1st Baron Kelvin").

### Introduction of the modern concept of function

While trying to gauge the range of functions for which convergence of the Fourier series can be shown, Dirichlet defines a [function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Function_(mathematics) "Function (mathematics)") by the property that "to any *x* there corresponds a single finite *y*", but then restricts his attention to [piecewise continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piecewise_continuous "Piecewise continuous") functions. Based on this, he is credited with introducing the modern concept of a function, as opposed to the older vague understanding of a function as an analytic formula. [Imre Lakatos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imre_Lakatos "Imre Lakatos") cites [Hermann Hankel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann_Hankel "Hermann Hankel") as the early origin of this attribution, but disputes the claim saying that "there is ample evidence that he had no idea of this concept [...] for instance when he discusses piecewise continuous functions, he says that at points of discontinuity, the function has two values".

### Other fields

Dirichlet also worked in [mathematical physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_physics "Mathematical physics"), lecturing and publishing research in [potential theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potential_theory "Potential theory") (including the Dirichlet problem and Dirichlet principle mentioned above), the [theory of heat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theory_of_heat "Theory of heat") and [hydrodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrodynamics "Hydrodynamics"). He improved on [Lagrange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagrange "Lagrange")'s work on [conservative systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservative_system "Conservative system") by showing that the condition for [equilibrium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanical_equilibrium "Mechanical equilibrium") is that the [potential energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potential_energy "Potential energy") is minimal.

Dirichlet also lectured on [probability theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability_theory "Probability theory") and [least squares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Least_squares "Least squares"), introducing some original methods and results, in particular for [limit theorems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asymptotic_theory_(statistics) "Asymptotic theory (statistics)") and an improvement of [Laplace's method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laplace's_method "Laplace's method") of approximation related to the [central limit theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_limit_theorem "Central limit theorem"). The [Dirichlet distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirichlet_distribution "Dirichlet distribution") and the [Dirichlet process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirichlet_process "Dirichlet process"), based on the [Dirichlet integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirichlet_integral "Dirichlet integral"), are named after him.

## Honors

Dirichlet was elected as a member of several academies:

- [Prussian Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prussian_Academy_of_Sciences "Prussian Academy of Sciences") (1832)
- [Saint Petersburg Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Academy_of_Sciences "Russian Academy of Sciences") (1833) – corresponding member
- [Göttingen Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Göttingen_Academy_of_Sciences "Göttingen Academy of Sciences") (1846)
- [French Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Academy_of_Sciences "French Academy of Sciences") (1854) – foreign member
- [Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Swedish_Academy_of_Sciences "Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences") (1854)
- [Royal Belgian Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Royal_Academies_for_Science_and_the_Arts_of_Belgium "The Royal Academies for Science and the Arts of Belgium") (1855)
- [Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society "Royal Society") (1855) – foreign member

In 1855 Dirichlet was awarded the civil class medal of the [Pour le Mérite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pour_le_Mérite "Pour le Mérite") order at [Alexander von Humboldt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_von_Humboldt "Alexander von Humboldt")'s recommendation. The [Dirichlet crater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirichlet_(crater) "Dirichlet (crater)") on the [Moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moon "Moon") and the [11665 Dirichlet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/11665_Dirichlet "11665 Dirichlet") asteroid are named after him.
