**Jean-Baptiste Joseph Fourier** ([/ˈfʊrieɪ, -iər/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English"); French: [[ʒɑ̃ batist ʒozɛf fuʁje]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/French "Help:IPA/French"); 21 March 1768 – 16 May 1830) was a French mathematician and [physicist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physicist "Physicist") born in [Auxerre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auxerre "Auxerre"), [Burgundy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burgundy "Burgundy") and best known for initiating the investigation of [Fourier series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourier_series "Fourier series"), which eventually developed into [Fourier analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourier_analysis "Fourier analysis") and [harmonic analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harmonic_analysis "Harmonic analysis"), and their applications to problems of [heat transfer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_transfer "Heat transfer") and [vibrations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vibration "Vibration"). The [Fourier transform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourier_transform "Fourier transform") and [Fourier's law of conduction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_conduction#Fourier's_law "Thermal conduction") are also named in his honour. Fourier is also generally credited with the discovery of the [greenhouse effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenhouse_effect "Greenhouse effect").

## Biography

Fourier was born in [Auxerre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auxerre "Auxerre") (now in the [Yonne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yonne "Yonne") [département](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Département "Département") of France), the son of a [tailor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tailor "Tailor"). He was [orphaned](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orphaned "Orphaned") at the age of nine. Fourier was recommended to the [Bishop of Auxerre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bishop_of_Auxerre "Bishop of Auxerre") and, through this introduction, he was educated by the [Benedictine Order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benedictine_Order "Benedictine Order") of the Convent of St. Mark. The commissions in the scientific corps of the army were reserved for those of good birth, and being thus ineligible, he accepted a military lectureship on mathematics. He took a prominent part in his own district in promoting the [French Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution "French Revolution"), serving on the local Revolutionary Committee. He was imprisoned briefly during the [Terror](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reign_of_Terror "Reign of Terror") but, in 1795, was appointed to the *[École Normale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/École_Normale_Supérieure "École Normale Supérieure")* and subsequently succeeded [Joseph-Louis Lagrange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph-Louis_Lagrange "Joseph-Louis Lagrange") at the *[École Polytechnique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/École_Polytechnique "École Polytechnique")*.

Fourier accompanied [Napoleon Bonaparte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleon_Bonaparte "Napoleon Bonaparte") on his [Egyptian expedition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_campaign_in_Egypt_and_Syria "French campaign in Egypt and Syria") in 1798, as scientific adviser, and was appointed secretary of the [Institut d'Égypte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institut_d'Égypte "Institut d'Égypte"). Cut off from France by the British fleet, he organized the workshops on which the French army had to rely for their munitions of war. He also contributed several mathematical papers to the Egyptian Institute (also called the Cairo Institute) which Napoleon founded at [Cairo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cairo "Cairo"), with a view of weakening British influence in the East. After the British victories and the capitulation of the French under [Jacques-François Menou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques-François_Menou "Jacques-François Menou") in 1801, Fourier returned to France.

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d5/Legendre_and_Fourier_%281820%29.jpg/250px-Legendre_and_Fourier_%281820%29.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Legendre_and_Fourier_(1820).jpg)

1820 watercolor [caricatures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caricature "Caricature") of French mathematicians [Adrien-Marie Legendre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adrien-Marie_Legendre "Adrien-Marie Legendre") (left) and **Joseph Fourier** (right) by French artist [Julien-Léopold Boilly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julien-Léopold_Boilly "Julien-Léopold Boilly"), watercolor portrait numbers 29 and 30 of *Album de 73 Portraits-Charge Aquarellés des Membres de I’Institut*

In 1801, [Napoleon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleon "Napoleon") appointed Fourier [Prefect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prefect_(France) "Prefect (France)") (Governor) of the [Department of Isère](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isère "Isère") in [Grenoble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grenoble "Grenoble"), where he oversaw road construction and other projects. However, Fourier had previously returned home from the Napoleon expedition to Egypt to resume his academic post as professor at [École Polytechnique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/École_Polytechnique "École Polytechnique") when [Napoleon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleon "Napoleon") decided otherwise in his remark

> ... the Prefect of the Department of Isère having recently died, I would like to express my confidence in citizen Fourier by appointing him to this place.

Hence being faithful to Napoleon, he took the office of Prefect. It was while at Grenoble that he began to experiment on the propagation of heat. He presented his paper *On the Propagation of Heat in Solid Bodies* to the Paris Institute on 21 December 1807. He also contributed to the monumental *[Description de l'Égypte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Description_de_l'Égypte "Description de l'Égypte")*.

In 1822, Fourier succeeded [Jean Baptiste Joseph Delambre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Baptiste_Joseph_Delambre "Jean Baptiste Joseph Delambre") as Permanent Secretary of the [French Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Academy_of_Sciences "French Academy of Sciences"). In 1830, he was elected a foreign member of the [Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Swedish_Academy_of_Sciences "Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences").

Fourier never married.

In 1830, his diminished health began to take its toll:

> Fourier had already experienced, in Egypt and Grenoble, some attacks of [aneurysm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aneurysm "Aneurysm") of the heart. At Paris, it was impossible to be mistaken with respect to the primary cause of the frequent suffocations which he experienced. A fall, however, which he sustained on the 4th of May 1830, while descending a flight of stairs, aggravated the malady to an extent beyond what could have been ever feared.

Shortly after this event, he died in his bed on 16 May 1830.

Fourier was buried in the [Père Lachaise Cemetery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Père_Lachaise_Cemetery "Père Lachaise Cemetery") in Paris, a tomb decorated with an Egyptian motif to reflect his position as secretary of the Cairo Institute, and his collation of *Description de l'Égypte*. His name is one of the [72 names inscribed on the Eiffel Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_72_names_on_the_Eiffel_Tower "The 72 names on the Eiffel Tower").

A bronze statue was erected in Auxerre in 1849, but it was melted down for armaments during World War II. [Joseph Fourier University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Fourier_University "Joseph Fourier University") in Grenoble was named after him.

## *The Analytic Theory of Heat*

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/0a/Fourier_in_his_coat_of_prefect.jpg/250px-Fourier_in_his_coat_of_prefect.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Fourier_in_his_coat_of_prefect.jpg)

Portrait of Fourier by [Claude Gautherot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claude_Gautherot "Claude Gautherot"), c. 1806

In 1822, Fourier published his treatise on [heat flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_transfer "Heat transfer") in *Théorie analytique de la chaleur* (*The Analytical Theory of Heat*), in which he based his reasoning on [Newton's law of cooling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton's_law_of_cooling "Newton's law of cooling"), namely, that the flow of heat between two adjacent particles is proportional to the extremely small difference of their temperatures. This treatise was translated, with editorial 'corrections', into English 56 years later by Freeman (1878). The treatise was also edited, with many editorial corrections, by mathematician [Jean Gaston Darboux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Gaston_Darboux "Jean Gaston Darboux") and republished in French in 1888.

There were three important contributions in this publication, one purely mathematical, two essentially physical. In mathematics, Fourier claimed that any function of a variable, whether [continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_function "Continuous function") or [discontinuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classification_of_discontinuities "Classification of discontinuities"), can be expanded in a series of [sines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sine_wave "Sine wave") of multiples of the variable. Though this result is not correct without additional conditions, Fourier's observation that some discontinuous functions are the sum of infinite series was a breakthrough. The question of determining when a Fourier series converges has been fundamental for centuries. [Joseph-Louis Lagrange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph-Louis_Lagrange "Joseph-Louis Lagrange") had given particular cases of this (false) theorem, and had implied that the method was general, but he had not pursued the subject. [Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Gustav_Lejeune_Dirichlet "Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet") was the first to give a satisfactory demonstration of it with some restrictive conditions. This work provides the foundation for what is today known as the [Fourier transform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourier_transform "Fourier transform").

One important physical contribution in the book was the concept of [dimensional homogeneity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimensional_analysis "Dimensional analysis") in equations; i.e. an equation can be formally correct only if the dimensions match on either side of the equality; Fourier made important contributions to [dimensional analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimensional_analysis "Dimensional analysis"). The other physical contribution was Fourier's proposal of his [partial differential equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_equation "Heat equation") for conductive diffusion of heat,
often called the [heat equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_equation "Heat equation"). This equation is now taught to every student of mathematical physics and is the most basic example of a [parabolic partial differential equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parabolic_partial_differential_equation "Parabolic partial differential equation").

Fourier's treatment of the heat diffusion in a cylinder was an inspiration for the development of the [Sturm–Liouville theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sturm–Liouville_theory "Sturm–Liouville theory") by [Joseph Liouville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Liouville "Joseph Liouville") and [Jacques Charles François Sturm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques_Charles_François_Sturm "Jacques Charles François Sturm"). In the same context, Fourier also gave a thorough discussion of [Bessel functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bessel_function "Bessel function") years before [Friedrich Wilhelm Bessel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Wilhelm_Bessel "Friedrich Wilhelm Bessel").

## Roots of polynomials

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/26/Grenoble_-_ancien_%C3%A9v%C3%AAch%C3%A9_-_Joseph_Fourier.JPG/250px-Grenoble_-_ancien_%C3%A9v%C3%AAch%C3%A9_-_Joseph_Fourier.JPG?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Grenoble_-_ancien_évêché_-_Joseph_Fourier.JPG)

Bust of Fourier in Grenoble

In his youth, Fourier discovered an inductive proof of [Descartes' rule of signs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Descartes'_rule_of_signs "Descartes' rule of signs"). He later left an unfinished work on determining and locating real roots of polynomials, which was edited by [Claude-Louis Navier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claude-Louis_Navier "Claude-Louis Navier") and published in 1831. This work contains much original matter—in particular, [Fourier's theorem on polynomial real roots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourier's_theorem_on_polynomial_real_roots "Fourier's theorem on polynomial real roots"), published in 1820. [François Budan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/François_Budan_de_Boislaurent "François Budan de Boislaurent"), in 1807 and 1811, had published independently his [theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Budan's_theorem "Budan's theorem") (also known by the name of Fourier), which is very close to Fourier's theorem (each theorem is a corollary of the other). Fourier's proof is the one that was usually given, during 19th century, in textbooks on the theory of equations. A [complete solution of the problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sturm's_theorem "Sturm's theorem") was given in 1829 by Sturm.

## Discovery of the greenhouse effect

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/69/Fourier_P%C3%A8re_Lachaise_2024.jpg/250px-Fourier_P%C3%A8re_Lachaise_2024.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Fourier_Père_Lachaise_2024.jpg)

The grave of Jean-Baptiste Joseph Fourier in Père Lachaise cemetery, Paris

In the 1820s, Fourier calculated that an object the size of the Earth, and at its distance from the Sun, should be considerably colder than the planet actually is if warmed by only the effects of incoming solar radiation. He examined various possible sources of the additional observed heat in articles published in 1824 and 1827. However, in the end, because of the large 33-degree difference between his calculations and observations, Fourier mistakenly believed that there is a significant contribution of radiation from interstellar space. (We now know that the balance is made up by [radiogenic heat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiogenic_heat "Radiogenic heat") from within the Earth). Still, Fourier's consideration of the possibility that the Earth's atmosphere might act as an insulator of some kind is widely recognized as the first proposal of what is now known as the [greenhouse effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenhouse_effect "Greenhouse effect"), although Fourier never called it that.

In his articles, Fourier referred to an experiment by [Horace Bénédict de Saussure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horace_Bénédict_de_Saussure "Horace Bénédict de Saussure"), who lined a vase with blackened cork. Into the cork, he inserted several panes of transparent glass, separated by intervals of air. Midday sunlight was allowed to enter at the top of the vase through the glass panes. The temperature became more elevated in the more interior compartments of this device. Fourier noted that if gases in the atmosphere could form a stable barrier like the glass panes they would have a similar effect on planetary temperatures. This conclusion may have contributed to the later use of the metaphor of the "greenhouse effect" to refer to the processes that determine atmospheric temperatures. Fourier noted that the actual mechanisms that determine the temperatures of the atmosphere included [convection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convection "Convection"), which was not present in de Saussure's experimental device.
