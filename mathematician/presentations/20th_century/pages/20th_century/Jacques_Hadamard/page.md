**Jacques Salomon Hadamard** (French: [[adamaʁ]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/French "Help:IPA/French"); 8 December 1865 – 17 October 1963) was a French mathematician who made major contributions in [number theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_theory "Number theory"), [complex analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_analysis "Complex analysis"), [differential geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_geometry "Differential geometry"), and [partial differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_differential_equations "Partial differential equations").

## Biography

The son of a teacher, Amédée Hadamard, of [Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish "Jewish") descent, and Claire Marie Jeanne Picard, Hadamard was born in [Versailles, France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Versailles_(city) "Versailles (city)") and attended the [Lycée Charlemagne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lycée_Charlemagne "Lycée Charlemagne") and [Lycée Louis-le-Grand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lycée_Louis-le-Grand "Lycée Louis-le-Grand"), where his father taught. In 1884 Hadamard entered the [École Normale Supérieure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/École_Normale_Supérieure "École Normale Supérieure"), having placed first in the entrance examinations both there and at the [École Polytechnique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/École_Polytechnique "École Polytechnique"). His teachers included [Tannery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Tannery "Paul Tannery"), [Hermite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermite "Hermite"), [Darboux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Gaston_Darboux "Jean Gaston Darboux"), [Appell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Émile_Appell "Paul Émile Appell"), [Goursat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Édouard_Goursat "Édouard Goursat"), and [Picard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Émile_Picard "Charles Émile Picard"). He obtained his doctorate in 1892 and in the same year was awarded the *Grand Prix des Sciences Mathématiques* for his essay on the [Riemann zeta function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann_zeta_function "Riemann zeta function").

In 1892 Hadamard married Louise-Anna Trénel, also of Jewish descent, with whom he had three sons and two daughters. The following year he took up a lectureship in the [University of Bordeaux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Bordeaux "University of Bordeaux"), where he proved his [celebrated inequality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadamard's_inequality "Hadamard's inequality") on [determinants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Determinant "Determinant"), which led to the discovery of [Hadamard matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadamard_matrix "Hadamard matrix") when equality holds. In 1896 he made two important contributions: he proved the [prime number theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_number_theorem "Prime number theorem"), using [complex function theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_function_theory "Complex function theory") (also proved independently by [Charles Jean de la Vallée-Poussin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Jean_de_la_Vallée-Poussin "Charles Jean de la Vallée-Poussin")); and he was awarded the Bordin Prize of the [French Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Academy_of_Sciences "French Academy of Sciences") for his work on [geodesics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geodesics "Geodesics") in the [differential geometry of surfaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_geometry_of_surfaces "Differential geometry of surfaces") and [dynamical systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamical_systems "Dynamical systems"). In the same year he was appointed Professor of Astronomy and Rational Mechanics in Bordeaux. His foundational work on geometry and [symbolic dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbolic_dynamics "Symbolic dynamics") continued in 1898 with the study of geodesics on surfaces of [negative curvature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negative_curvature "Negative curvature"). For his cumulative work, he was awarded the [Prix Poncelet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prix_Poncelet "Prix Poncelet") in 1898.

After the [Dreyfus affair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dreyfus_affair "Dreyfus affair"), which involved him personally because his second cousin Lucie was the wife of Dreyfus, Hadamard became politically active and a staunch supporter of [Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jew "Jew") causes though he professed to be an atheist in his religion.

In 1897 he moved back to Paris, holding positions in the [Sorbonne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Paris "University of Paris") and the [Collège de France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collège_de_France "Collège de France"), where he was appointed Professor of Mechanics in 1909. In addition to this post, he was appointed to chairs of analysis at the [École Polytechnique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/École_Polytechnique "École Polytechnique") in 1912 and at the [École Centrale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/École_Centrale "École Centrale") in 1920, succeeding [Jordan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camille_Jordan "Camille Jordan") and Appell. In Paris Hadamard concentrated his interests on the problems of mathematical physics, in particular [partial differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_differential_equations "Partial differential equations"), the [calculus of variations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calculus_of_variations "Calculus of variations") and the foundations of [functional analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functional_analysis "Functional analysis"). He introduced the idea of *[well-posed problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Well-posed_problem "Well-posed problem")* and the *[method of descent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Method_of_descent "Method of descent")* in the theory of [partial differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_differential_equation "Partial differential equation"), culminating in his seminal book on the subject, based on lectures given at [Yale University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yale_University "Yale University") in 1922. Later in his life he wrote on [probability theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability_theory "Probability theory") and [mathematical education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_education "Mathematical education").

Hadamard was elected to the [French Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Academy_of_Sciences "French Academy of Sciences") in 1916, in succession to [Poincaré](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_Poincaré "Henri Poincaré"), whose complete works he helped edit. He became foreign member of the [Royal Netherlands Academy of Arts and Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Netherlands_Academy_of_Arts_and_Sciences "Royal Netherlands Academy of Arts and Sciences") in 1920. He was elected a foreign member of the [Academy of Sciences of the USSR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academy_of_Sciences_of_the_USSR "Academy of Sciences of the USSR") in 1929. He visited the [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union "Soviet Union") in 1930 and 1934 and China in 1936 at the invitation of Soviet and Chinese mathematicians.

Hadamard stayed in France at the beginning of the [Second World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_World_War "Second World War") and escaped to southern France in 1940. The [Vichy government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vichy_France "Vichy France") permitted him to leave for the United States in 1941 and he obtained a visiting position at [Columbia University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia_University "Columbia University") in New York. He moved to London in 1944 and returned to France when the war ended in 1945.

Hadamard was awarded an honorary doctorate ([LL.D.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legum_Doctor "Legum Doctor")) by [Yale University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yale_University "Yale University") in October 1901, during celebrations for the bicentenary of the university. He was awarded the [CNRS Gold medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CNRS_Gold_medal "CNRS Gold medal") for his lifetime achievements in 1956. He died in Paris in 1963, aged ninety-seven.

Hadamard's students included [Maurice Fréchet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maurice_René_Fréchet "Maurice René Fréchet"), [Paul Lévy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Lévy_(mathematician) "Paul Lévy (mathematician)"), [Szolem Mandelbrojt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Szolem_Mandelbrojt "Szolem Mandelbrojt"), and [André Weil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/André_Weil "André Weil").

## On creativity

In his book *Psychology of Invention in the Mathematical Field*, Hadamard uses the results of introspection to study mathematical thought processes, and tries to report and interpret observations, personal or gathered from other scholars engaged in the work of invention. In sharp contrast to authors who identify [language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Language "Language") and [cognition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognition "Cognition"), he describes his own mathematical thinking as largely wordless, often accompanied by [mental images](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mental_images "Mental images") that represent the entire solution to a problem. He surveyed 100 of the leading physicists of the day (approximately 1900), asking them how they did their work.

Hadamard described the experiences of the mathematicians/theoretical physicists [Carl Friedrich Gauss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Friedrich_Gauss "Carl Friedrich Gauss"), [Hermann von Helmholtz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann_von_Helmholtz "Hermann von Helmholtz"), [Henri Poincaré](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_Poincaré "Henri Poincaré") and others as viewing entire solutions with "sudden spontaneousness".

Hadamard described the process as having four steps of the five-step [Graham Wallas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graham_Wallas "Graham Wallas") [creative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creativity "Creativity") process model, with the first three also having been put forth by Helmholtz: Preparation, Incubation, Illumination, and Verification (Wallas' five stages added "Intimation" prior to Illumination, a sudden feeling of being about to find the solution to a problem).
