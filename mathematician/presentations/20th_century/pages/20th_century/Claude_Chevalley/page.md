**Claude Chevalley** (French: [[ʃəvalɛ]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/French "Help:IPA/French"); 11 February 1909 – 28 June 1984) was a French mathematician who made important contributions to [number theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_theory "Number theory"), [algebraic geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_geometry "Algebraic geometry"), [class field theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Class_field_theory "Class field theory"), [finite group theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_group_theory "Finite group theory") and the theory of [algebraic groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_groups "Algebraic groups"). He was a founding member of the [Bourbaki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolas_Bourbaki "Nicolas Bourbaki") group.

## Life

His father, Abel Chevalley, was a French diplomat who, jointly with his wife Marguerite Chevalley née [Sabatier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Auguste_Sabatier "Louis Auguste Sabatier"), wrote *The Concise Oxford French Dictionary*. Chevalley graduated from the [École Normale Supérieure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/École_Normale_Supérieure "École Normale Supérieure") in 1929, where he studied under [Émile Picard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Émile_Picard "Émile Picard"). He then spent time at the [University of Hamburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Hamburg "University of Hamburg"), studying under [Emil Artin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emil_Artin "Emil Artin") and at the [University of Marburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Marburg "University of Marburg"), studying under [Helmut Hasse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helmut_Hasse "Helmut Hasse"). In Germany, Chevalley discovered Japanese mathematics in the person of [Shokichi Iyanaga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shokichi_Iyanaga "Shokichi Iyanaga"). Chevalley was awarded a doctorate in 1933 from the [University of Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Paris "University of Paris") for a thesis on [class field theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Class_field_theory "Class field theory").

When World War II broke out, Chevalley was at [Princeton University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University "Princeton University"). After reporting to the French Embassy, he stayed in the U.S., first at Princeton and then (after 1947) at [Columbia University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia_University "Columbia University"). His American students included [Leon Ehrenpreis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leon_Ehrenpreis "Leon Ehrenpreis") and [Gerhard Hochschild](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerhard_Hochschild "Gerhard Hochschild"). During his time in the U.S., Chevalley became an American citizen and wrote a substantial part of his lifetime's output in English.

When Chevalley applied for a chair at the [Sorbonne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Paris "University of Paris"), the difficulties he encountered were the subject of a polemical piece by his friend and fellow *Bourbakiste* [André Weil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/André_Weil "André Weil"), titled "Science Française?" and published in the *[Nouvelle Revue Française](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nouvelle_Revue_Française "Nouvelle Revue Française")*. Chevalley was the "professeur B" of the piece, as confirmed in the endnote to the reprint in Weil's collected works, *Oeuvres Scientifiques, tome II*. Chevalley eventually did obtain a position in 1957 at the faculty of sciences of the [University of Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Paris "University of Paris") and after 1970 at the [Université de Paris VII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Université_de_Paris_VII "Université de Paris VII").

Chevalley had artistic and political interests, and was a minor member of the French [non-conformists of the 1930s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-conformists_of_the_1930s "Non-conformists of the 1930s"). The following quote by the co-editor of Chevalley's collected works attests to these interests:

> "Chevalley was a member of various avant-garde groups, both in politics and in the arts... Mathematics was the most important part of his life, but he did not draw any boundary between his mathematics and the rest of his life."

## Work

In his PhD thesis, Chevalley made an important contribution to the technical development of [class field theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Class_field_theory "Class field theory"), removing a use of [L-functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L-function "L-function") and replacing it by an algebraic method. At that time use of [group cohomology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_cohomology "Group cohomology") was implicit, cloaked by the language of [central simple algebras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_simple_algebra "Central simple algebra"). In the introduction to [André Weil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/André_Weil "André Weil")'s *Basic Number Theory*, Weil attributed the book's adoption of that path to an unpublished manuscript by Chevalley.

Around 1950, Chevalley wrote a three-volume treatment of [Lie groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lie_group "Lie group"). A few years later, he published the work for which he is best remembered, his investigation into what are now called [Chevalley groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chevalley_group "Chevalley group"). Chevalley groups make up 9 of the 18 families of [finite simple groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_simple_group "Finite simple group").

Chevalley's accurate discussion of integrality conditions in the [Lie algebras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lie_algebra "Lie algebra") of [semisimple groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semisimple_group "Semisimple group") enabled abstracting their theory from the [real](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_number "Real number") and [complex fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_field "Complex field"). As a consequence, analogues over [finite fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_field "Finite field") could be defined. This was an essential stage in the evolving [classification of finite simple groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classification_of_finite_simple_groups "Classification of finite simple groups"). After Chevalley's work, the distinction between "classical groups" falling into the [Dynkin diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynkin_diagram "Dynkin diagram") classification, and [sporadic groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sporadic_groups "Sporadic groups") which did not, became sharp enough to be useful. What are called 'twisted' groups of the classical families could be fitted into the picture.

"Chevalley's theorem" (also called the [Chevalley–Warning theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chevalley–Warning_theorem "Chevalley–Warning theorem")) usually refers to his result on the solubility of equations over a finite field. Another theorem of his concerns the [constructible sets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constructible_set_(topology) "Constructible set (topology)") in [algebraic geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_geometry "Algebraic geometry"), i.e. those in the [Boolean algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boolean_algebra_(structure) "Boolean algebra (structure)") generated by the [Zariski-open](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zariski-open "Zariski-open") and [Zariski-closed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zariski-closed "Zariski-closed") sets. It states that the [image](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Image_(mathematics) "Image (mathematics)") of such a set by a [morphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morphism "Morphism") of [algebraic varieties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_variety "Algebraic variety") is of the same type. Logicians call this an [elimination of quantifiers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elimination_of_quantifiers "Elimination of quantifiers").

In the 1950s, Chevalley led some Paris seminars of major importance: the *Séminaire Cartan–Chevalley* of the academic year 1955-6, with [Henri Cartan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_Cartan "Henri Cartan") and the *Séminaire Chevalley* of 1956-7 and 1957-8. These dealt with topics on [algebraic groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_group "Algebraic group") and the foundations of algebraic geometry, as well as pure [abstract algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstract_algebra "Abstract algebra"). The Cartan–Chevalley seminar was the genesis of [scheme theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scheme_theory "Scheme theory"), but its subsequent development in the hands of [Alexander Grothendieck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Grothendieck "Alexander Grothendieck") was so rapid, thorough and inclusive that its historical tracks can appear well covered. Grothendieck's work subsumed the more specialised contribution of [Serre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Pierre_Serre "Jean-Pierre Serre"), Chevalley, [Gorō Shimura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gorō_Shimura "Gorō Shimura") and others such as [Erich Kähler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erich_Kähler "Erich Kähler") and [Masayoshi Nagata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masayoshi_Nagata "Masayoshi Nagata").
