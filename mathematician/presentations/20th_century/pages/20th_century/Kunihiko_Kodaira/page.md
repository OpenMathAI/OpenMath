**Kunihiko Kodaira** (小平 邦彦, *Kodaira Kunihiko*; Japanese pronunciation: [[kodaꜜiɾa kɯɲiꜜçi̥ko]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Japanese "Help:IPA/Japanese"), 16 March 1915 – 26 July 1997) was a Japanese [mathematician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematician "Mathematician") known for distinguished work in [algebraic geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_geometry "Algebraic geometry") and the theory of [complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_manifold "Complex manifold") [manifolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manifold "Manifold"), and as the founder of the Japanese school of algebraic geometers. He was awarded a [Fields Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fields_Medal "Fields Medal") in 1954, being the first Japanese national to receive this honour.

## Early life and education

Kunihiko Kodaira was born in [Tokyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo "Tokyo") on Tuesday, March 16, 1915. He graduated from the [University of Tokyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Tokyo "University of Tokyo") in 1938 with a degree in mathematics and also graduated from the physics department at the University of Tokyo in 1941. During the [war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II") years he worked in isolation, but was able to master [Hodge theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hodge_theory "Hodge theory") as it then stood. He obtained his [PhD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PhD "PhD") from the [University of Tokyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Tokyo "University of Tokyo") in 1949, with a thesis entitled *Harmonic fields in Riemannian manifolds*. He was involved in [cryptographic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptography "Cryptography") work from about 1944, while holding an academic post in Tokyo.

## Institute for Advanced Study and Princeton University

In 1949 he travelled to the [Institute for Advanced Study](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_for_Advanced_Study "Institute for Advanced Study") in [Princeton, New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton,_New_Jersey "Princeton, New Jersey") at the invitation of [Hermann Weyl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann_Weyl "Hermann Weyl"). He was subsequently also appointed Associate Professor at [Princeton University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University "Princeton University") in 1952 and promoted to Professor in 1955. At this time the foundations of Hodge theory were being brought in line with contemporary technique in [operator theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operator_theory "Operator theory"). Kodaira rapidly became involved in exploiting the tools it opened up in algebraic geometry, adding [sheaf theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheaf_theory "Sheaf theory") as it became available. This work was particularly influential, for example on [Friedrich Hirzebruch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Hirzebruch "Friedrich Hirzebruch").

In a second research phase, Kodaira wrote a long series of papers in collaboration with [Donald C. Spencer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_C._Spencer "Donald C. Spencer"), founding the [deformation theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deformation_theory "Deformation theory") of complex structures on manifolds. This gave the possibility of constructions of [moduli spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moduli_space "Moduli space"), since in general such structures depend [continuously](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuity_(mathematics) "Continuity (mathematics)") on parameters. It also identified the [sheaf cohomology groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheaf_cohomology_group "Sheaf cohomology group"), for the sheaf associated with the [holomorphic tangent bundle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holomorphic_tangent_bundle "Holomorphic tangent bundle"), that carried the basic data about the dimension of the moduli space, and obstructions to deformations. This theory is still foundational, and also had an influence on the (technically very different) [scheme theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scheme_theory "Scheme theory") of [Grothendieck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grothendieck "Grothendieck"). Spencer then continued this work, applying the techniques to structures other than complex ones, such as [G-structures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G-structure "G-structure").

In a third major part of his work, Kodaira worked again from around 1960 through the [classification of algebraic surfaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classification_of_algebraic_surfaces "Classification of algebraic surfaces") from the point of view of [birational geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birational_geometry "Birational geometry") of complex manifolds. This resulted in a typology of seven kinds of two-dimensional [compact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_space "Compact space") complex manifolds, recovering the five algebraic types known classically; the other two being non-algebraic. He provided also detailed studies of [elliptic fibrations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptic_fibration "Elliptic fibration") of surfaces over a curve, or in other language [elliptic curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptic_curve "Elliptic curve") over [algebraic function fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_function_field "Algebraic function field"), a theory whose arithmetic analogue proved important soon afterwards. This work also included a characterisation of [K3 surfaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K3_surface "K3 surface") as deformations of [quartic surfaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quartic_surface "Quartic surface") in *P*3, and the theorem that they form a single [diffeomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffeomorphism "Diffeomorphism") class. Again, this work has proved foundational. (The K3 surfaces were named after [Ernst Kummer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernst_Kummer "Ernst Kummer"), [Erich Kähler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erich_Kähler "Erich Kähler"), and Kodaira).

## Later years

Kunihiko Kodaira left Princeton University and the Institute for Advanced Study in 1961, and briefly served as chair at the [Johns Hopkins University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johns_Hopkins_University "Johns Hopkins University") and [Stanford University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford_University "Stanford University"). In 1967, he returned to the [University of Tokyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Tokyo "University of Tokyo"). He was awarded a [Wolf Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolf_Prize "Wolf Prize") in 1984/5. He died in [Kofu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kofu "Kofu") on Saturday, 26 July 1997, at the age of eighty-two.

He was honoured with the membership of the [Japan Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan_Academy "Japan Academy"), the [Mathematical Society of Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_Society_of_Japan "Mathematical Society of Japan") and the [American Academy of Arts and Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Academy_of_Arts_and_Sciences "American Academy of Arts and Sciences") in 1978. He was the foreign associate of the US [National Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Academy_of_Sciences "National Academy of Sciences") in 1975, member of the [Göttingen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Göttingen "Göttingen") Academy of Sciences in 1974 and honorary member of the [London Mathematical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Mathematical_Society "London Mathematical Society") in 1979. He received the Order of Culture and the [Japan Academy Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan_Academy_Prize_(academics) "Japan Academy Prize (academics)") in 1957 and the Fujiwara Prize in 1975.
