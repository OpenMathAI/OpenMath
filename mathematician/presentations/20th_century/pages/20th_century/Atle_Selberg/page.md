**Atle Selberg** (14 June 1917 – 6 August 2007) was a Norwegian [mathematician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematician "Mathematician") known for his work in [analytic number theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytic_number_theory "Analytic number theory") and the theory of [automorphic forms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automorphic_form "Automorphic form"), and in particular for bringing them into relation with [spectral theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectral_theory "Spectral theory"). He was awarded the [Fields Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fields_Medal "Fields Medal") in 1950 and an honorary [Abel Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abel_Prize "Abel Prize") in 2002.

## Early years

Selberg was born in [Langesund](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Langesund "Langesund"), Norway, the son of teacher Anna Kristina Selberg and mathematician [Ole Michael Ludvigsen Selberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ole_Michael_Ludvigsen_Selberg "Ole Michael Ludvigsen Selberg"). Two of his three brothers, [Sigmund](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigmund_Selberg "Sigmund Selberg") and [Henrik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henrik_Selberg "Henrik Selberg"), were also mathematicians. His other brother, [Arne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arne_Selberg "Arne Selberg"), was a professor of engineering.
While he was still at school, he was influenced by the work of [Srinivasa Ramanujan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Srinivasa_Ramanujan "Srinivasa Ramanujan") and he found an exact analytical formula for the [partition function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partition_function_(number_theory) "Partition function (number theory)") as suggested by the works of Ramanujan; however, this result was first published by [Hans Rademacher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Rademacher "Hans Rademacher").

He studied at the [University of Oslo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Oslo "University of Oslo") and completed his [doctorate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PhD "PhD") in 1943.

## World War II

During [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II"), Selberg worked in isolation due to the [German occupation of Norway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_occupation_of_Norway "German occupation of Norway"). After the war, his accomplishments became known, including a proof that a positive proportion of the zeros of the [Riemann zeta function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann_zeta_function "Riemann zeta function") lie on the line

During the war, he fought against the German invasion of Norway, and was imprisoned several times.

## Post-war in Norway

After the war, he turned to [sieve theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sieve_theory "Sieve theory"), a previously neglected topic which Selberg's work brought into prominence. In a 1947 paper he introduced the [Selberg sieve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selberg_sieve "Selberg sieve"), a method well adapted in particular to providing auxiliary upper bounds, and which contributed to [Chen's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chen's_theorem "Chen's theorem"), among other important results.

In 1948, Selberg submitted two papers in *[Annals of Mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annals_of_Mathematics "Annals of Mathematics")* in which he proved by elementary means the theorems for [primes in arithmetic progression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primes_in_arithmetic_progression "Primes in arithmetic progression") and the [density of primes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_number_theorem "Prime number theorem"). This challenged the widely held view of his time that certain theorems are only obtainable with the advanced methods of [complex analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_analysis "Complex analysis"). He established this result by elementary means in March 1948, and by July of that year, Selberg and [Paul Erdős](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Erdős "Paul Erdős") each obtained [elementary proofs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elementary_proof "Elementary proof") of the [prime number theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_number_theorem "Prime number theorem"), both using the asymptotic formula above as a starting point. Circumstances leading up to the proofs, as well as publication disagreements, led to a bitter [dispute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Priority_dispute "Priority dispute") between the two mathematicians.

For his fundamental accomplishments during the 1940s, Selberg received the 1950 [Fields Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fields_Medal "Fields Medal").

## Institute for Advanced Study

C.L. Siegel invited Selberg to visit the [Institute for Advanced Study](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_for_Advanced_Study "Institute for Advanced Study") in [Princeton, New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton,_New_Jersey "Princeton, New Jersey") in 1947. He moved to the United States and was an associate professor at [Syracuse University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syracuse_University "Syracuse University") briefly. He returned to the IAS in 1949, where he remained until his death. During the 1950s, he worked on introducing [spectral theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectral_theory "Spectral theory") into [number theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_theory "Number theory"), culminating in his development of the [Selberg trace formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selberg_trace_formula "Selberg trace formula"), the most famous and influential of his results. In its simplest form, this establishes a duality between the lengths of [closed geodesics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_geodesic "Closed geodesic") on a [compact Riemann surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_Riemann_surface "Compact Riemann surface") and the [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalues "Eigenvalues") of the [Laplacian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laplace-Beltrami_operator "Laplace-Beltrami operator"), which is analogous to the duality between the [prime numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_number "Prime number") and the zeros of the zeta function.

He generally worked alone. His only coauthor was [Sarvadaman Chowla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarvadaman_Chowla "Sarvadaman Chowla").

Selberg was awarded the 1986 [Wolf Prize in Mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolf_Prize_in_Mathematics "Wolf Prize in Mathematics"). He was also awarded an honorary [Abel Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abel_Prize "Abel Prize") in 2002, its founding year, before the awarding of the regular prizes began.

Selberg received many distinctions for his work, in addition to the Fields Medal, the [Wolf Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolf_Prize "Wolf Prize") and the [Gunnerus Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gunnerus_Medal "Gunnerus Medal"). He was elected to the [Norwegian Academy of Science and Letters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norwegian_Academy_of_Science_and_Letters "Norwegian Academy of Science and Letters"), the [Royal Danish Academy of Sciences and Letters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Danish_Academy_of_Sciences_and_Letters "Royal Danish Academy of Sciences and Letters") and the [American Academy of Arts and Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Academy_of_Arts_and_Sciences "American Academy of Arts and Sciences").

In 1972, he was awarded an [honorary degree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honorary_degree "Honorary degree"), doctor philos. honoris causa, at the [Norwegian Institute of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norwegian_Institute_of_Technology "Norwegian Institute of Technology"), later part of [Norwegian University of Science and Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norwegian_University_of_Science_and_Technology "Norwegian University of Science and Technology").

His first wife, Hedvig Liebermann, who Selberg married in 1947, died in 1995. With her, Selberg had two children: Ingrid Selberg (married to playwright [Mustapha Matura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mustapha_Matura "Mustapha Matura")) and Lars Selberg. In 2003, Atle Selberg married Betty Frances "Mickey" Compton (born in 1929).

He died at home in [Princeton, New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton,_New_Jersey "Princeton, New Jersey") on 6 August 2007 of heart failure. Upon his death, he was survived by his widow, daughter, son, and four grandchildren.
