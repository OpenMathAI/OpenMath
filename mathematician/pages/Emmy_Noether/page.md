**Amalie Emmy Noether** (23 March 1882 – 14 April 1935) was a German mathematician who made many important contributions to [abstract algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstract_algebra "Abstract algebra"). She also proved [Noether's first](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noether's_theorem "Noether's theorem") and [second theorems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noether's_second_theorem "Noether's second theorem"), which are fundamental in [mathematical physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_physics "Mathematical physics"). Noether was described by [Pavel Alexandrov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pavel_Alexandrov "Pavel Alexandrov"), [Albert Einstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Einstein "Albert Einstein"), [Jean Dieudonné](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Dieudonné "Jean Dieudonné"), [Hermann Weyl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann_Weyl "Hermann Weyl"), and [Norbert Wiener](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norbert_Wiener "Norbert Wiener") as the most important [woman in the history of mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_women_in_mathematics "List of women in mathematics"). As one of the leading mathematicians of her time, she developed theories of [rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_(mathematics) "Ring (mathematics)"), [fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_(mathematics) "Field (mathematics)"), and [algebras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebras "Algebras"). In physics, [Noether's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noether's_theorem "Noether's theorem") explains the connection between [symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetry_(physics) "Symmetry (physics)") and [conservation laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_law "Conservation law").

Noether was born to a [Jewish family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_family "Jewish family") in the [Franconian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franconia "Franconia") town of [Erlangen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erlangen "Erlangen"); her father was the mathematician [Max Noether](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Noether "Max Noether"). She originally planned to teach French and English after passing the required examinations, but instead studied mathematics at the [University of Erlangen–Nuremberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Erlangen–Nuremberg "University of Erlangen–Nuremberg"), where her father lectured. After completing her doctorate in 1907 under the supervision of [Paul Gordan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Gordan "Paul Gordan"), she worked at the Mathematical Institute of Erlangen without pay for seven years. At the time, women were largely excluded from academic positions. In 1915, she was invited by [David Hilbert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Hilbert "David Hilbert") and [Felix Klein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Felix_Klein "Felix Klein") to join the mathematics department at the [University of Göttingen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Göttingen "University of Göttingen"), a world-renowned center of mathematical research. The philosophical faculty objected, and she spent four years lecturing under Hilbert's name. Her [habilitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habilitation "Habilitation") was approved in 1919, allowing her to obtain the rank of *[Privatdozent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Privatdozent "Privatdozent")*.

Noether remained a leading member of the [Göttingen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Göttingen "Göttingen") mathematics department until 1933; her students were sometimes called the "Noether Boys". In 1924, Dutch mathematician [B. L. van der Waerden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B._L._van_der_Waerden "B. L. van der Waerden") joined her circle and soon became the leading expositor of Noether's ideas; her work was the foundation for the second volume of his influential 1931 textbook, *[Moderne Algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moderne_Algebra "Moderne Algebra")*. By the time of her plenary address at the 1932 [International Congress of Mathematicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Congress_of_Mathematicians "International Congress of Mathematicians") in [Zürich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zürich "Zürich"), her algebraic acumen was recognized around the world. The following year, Germany's Nazi government [dismissed Jews from university positions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-Jewish_legislation_in_pre-war_Nazi_Germany "Anti-Jewish legislation in pre-war Nazi Germany"), and Noether moved to the United States to take up a position at [Bryn Mawr College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bryn_Mawr_College "Bryn Mawr College") in [Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pennsylvania "Pennsylvania"). There, she taught graduate and post-doctoral women including [Marie Johanna Weiss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marie_Johanna_Weiss "Marie Johanna Weiss") and [Olga Taussky-Todd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olga_Taussky-Todd "Olga Taussky-Todd"). At the same time, she lectured and conducted research at the [Institute for Advanced Study](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_for_Advanced_Study "Institute for Advanced Study") in [Princeton, New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton,_New_Jersey "Princeton, New Jersey").

Noether's mathematical work has been divided into three "[epochs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epoch "Epoch")". In the first (1908–1919), she made contributions to the theories of [algebraic invariants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_invariant "Algebraic invariant") and [number fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_(mathematics) "Field (mathematics)"). Her work on differential invariants in the [calculus of variations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calculus_of_variations "Calculus of variations"), [Noether's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noether's_theorem "Noether's theorem"), has been called "one of the most important mathematical theorems ever proved in guiding the development of modern physics". In the second epoch (1920–1926), she began work that "changed the face of [abstract] algebra". In her classic 1921 paper *Idealtheorie in Ringbereichen* (*Theory of Ideals in Ring Domains*), Noether developed the theory of [ideals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ideal_(ring_theory) "Ideal (ring theory)") in [commutative rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative_ring "Commutative ring") into a tool with wide-ranging applications. She made elegant use of the [ascending chain condition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ascending_chain_condition "Ascending chain condition"), and objects satisfying it are named *[Noetherian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noetherian "Noetherian")* in her honor. In the third epoch (1927–1935), she published works on [noncommutative algebras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noncommutative_algebra "Noncommutative algebra") and [hypercomplex numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypercomplex_number "Hypercomplex number") and united the [representation theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Representation_theory "Representation theory") of [groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_(mathematics) "Group (mathematics)") with the theory of [modules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Module_(mathematics) "Module (mathematics)") and ideals. In addition to her own publications, Noether was generous with her ideas and is credited with several lines of research published by other mathematicians, even in fields far removed from her main work, such as [algebraic topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_topology "Algebraic topology").

## Biography

### Early life

[![1916 postcard depicting Universitätstraße in Erlangen](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/8d/Erlangen_1916.jpg/250px-Erlangen_1916.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Erlangen_1916.jpg)

Noether grew up in the Bavarian city of [Erlangen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erlangen "Erlangen"), depicted here in a 1916 postcard.

Amalie Emmy Noether was born on 23 March 1882 in [Erlangen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erlangen "Erlangen"), Bavaria. She was the first of four children of mathematician [Max Noether](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Noether "Max Noether") and Ida Amalia Kaufmann, both from wealthy Jewish merchant families. Her first name was "Amalie", but she began using her middle name at a young age and invariably continued to do so in her adult life and her publications.

In her youth, Noether did not stand out academically, but was known for being clever and friendly. She was [near-sighted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Near-sighted "Near-sighted") and talked with a minor [lisp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lisp "Lisp") during her childhood. A family friend recounted a story years later about young Noether quickly solving a brain teaser at a children's party, showing logical acumen at an early age. She was taught to cook and clean, as were most girls of the time, and took piano lessons. She pursued none of these activities with passion, but loved to dance.

[![Family portrait of Alfred, Emmy, Fritz and Robert Noether](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/0b/NoetherFamily_MFO3120.jpg/250px-NoetherFamily_MFO3120.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:NoetherFamily_MFO3120.jpg)

Emmy Noether with her brothers Alfred, [Fritz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fritz_Noether "Fritz Noether"), and Robert, before 1918

Noether had three younger brothers. The eldest, Alfred Noether, was born in 1883 and was awarded a doctorate in [chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemistry "Chemistry") from Erlangen in 1909, but died nine years later. [Fritz Noether](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fritz_Noether "Fritz Noether") was born in 1884, studied in [Munich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Munich "Munich") and made contributions to [applied mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Applied_mathematics "Applied mathematics"). He was likely executed in the [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union "Soviet Union") in 1941 during the [Second World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_World_War "Second World War"). The youngest, Gustav Robert Noether, was born in 1889. Very little is known about his life; he suffered from chronic illness and died in 1928.

### Education

Noether showed early proficiency in French and English. In early 1900, she took the examination for teachers of these languages and received an overall score of *sehr gut* (very good). Her performance qualified her to teach languages at schools reserved for girls, but she chose instead to continue her studies at the [University of Erlangen–Nuremberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Erlangen–Nuremberg "University of Erlangen–Nuremberg"), at which her father was a professor.

This was an unconventional decision; two years earlier, the Academic Senate of the university had declared that allowing [mixed-sex education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mixed-sex_education "Mixed-sex education") would "overthrow all academic order". One of just two women in a university of 986 students, Noether was allowed only to [audit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academic_audit "Academic audit") classes rather than participate fully, and she required the permission of individual professors whose lectures she wished to attend. Despite these obstacles, on 14 July 1903, she passed the graduation exam at a *[Realgymnasium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Realgymnasium "Realgymnasium")* in [Nuremberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuremberg "Nuremberg").

During the 1903–1904 winter semester, she studied at the [University of Göttingen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Göttingen "University of Göttingen"), attending lectures given by astronomer [Karl Schwarzschild](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Schwarzschild "Karl Schwarzschild") and mathematicians [Hermann Minkowski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann_Minkowski "Hermann Minkowski"), [Otto Blumenthal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otto_Blumenthal "Otto Blumenthal"), [Felix Klein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Felix_Klein "Felix Klein"), and [David Hilbert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Hilbert "David Hilbert").

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/07/Paul_Albert_Gordan.jpg/250px-Paul_Albert_Gordan.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Paul_Albert_Gordan.jpg)

[Paul Gordan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Gordan "Paul Gordan") supervised Noether's doctoral dissertation on [invariants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invariant_(mathematics) "Invariant (mathematics)") of biquadratic forms.

In 1903, restrictions on women's full enrollment in Bavarian universities were rescinded. Noether returned to Erlangen and officially reentered the university in October 1904, declaring her intention to focus solely on mathematics. She was one of six women in her year (two auditors) and the only woman in her chosen school. Under the supervision of [Paul Gordan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Gordan "Paul Gordan"), she wrote her dissertation, *Über die Bildung des Formensystems der ternären biquadratischen Form* (*On Complete Systems of Invariants for Ternary Biquadratic Forms*), in 1907, graduating *summa cum laude* later that year. Gordan was a member of the "computational" school of invariant researchers, and Noether's thesis ended with a list of over 300 explicitly worked-out invariants. This approach to invariants was later superseded by the more abstract and general approach pioneered by Hilbert. It had been well received, but Noether later described her thesis and some subsequent similar papers she produced as "crap". All of her later work was in a completely different field.

### University of Erlangen–Nuremberg

From 1908 to 1915, Noether taught at Erlangen's Mathematical Institute without pay, occasionally substituting for her father, [Max Noether](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Noether "Max Noether"), when he was too ill to lecture. She joined the [Circolo Matematico di Palermo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circolo_Matematico_di_Palermo "Circolo Matematico di Palermo") in 1908 and the [Deutsche Mathematiker-Vereinigung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deutsche_Mathematiker-Vereinigung "Deutsche Mathematiker-Vereinigung") in 1909. In 1910 and 1911, she published an extension of her thesis work from three variables to *n* variables.

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/9e/Emmy_noether_postcard_1915.jpg/330px-Emmy_noether_postcard_1915.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Emmy_noether_postcard_1915.jpg)

Noether sometimes used postcards to discuss abstract algebra with her colleague, [Ernst Fischer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernst_Sigismund_Fischer "Ernst Sigismund Fischer"). This card is postmarked 10 April 1915.

Gordan retired in 1910, and Noether taught under his successors, [Erhard Schmidt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erhard_Schmidt "Erhard Schmidt") and [Ernst Fischer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernst_Sigismund_Fischer "Ernst Sigismund Fischer"), who took over from the former in 1911. According to her colleague [Hermann Weyl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann_Weyl "Hermann Weyl") and her biographer [Auguste Dick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auguste_Dick "Auguste Dick"), Fischer was an important influence on Noether, in particular by introducing her to the work of [David Hilbert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Hilbert "David Hilbert"). Noether and Fischer shared lively enjoyment of mathematics and would often discuss lectures long after they were over; Noether is known to have sent postcards to Fischer continuing her train of mathematical thoughts.

From 1913 to 1916, Noether published several papers extending and applying Hilbert's methods to mathematical objects such as [fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_(mathematics) "Field (mathematics)") of [rational functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_function "Rational function") and the [invariants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invariant_theory "Invariant theory") of [finite groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_group "Finite group"). This phase marked Noether's first exposure to [abstract algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstract_algebra "Abstract algebra"), the field to which she would make groundbreaking contributions.

In Erlangen, Noether advised two doctoral students: Hans Falckenberg and Fritz Seidelmann, who defended their theses in 1911 and 1916. Despite Noether's significant role, they were both officially under the supervision of her father. Following the completion of his doctorate, Falckenberg spent time in [Braunschweig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Braunschweig "Braunschweig") and [Königsberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Königsberg "Königsberg") before becoming a professor at the [University of Giessen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Giessen "University of Giessen") while Seidelmann became a professor in [Munich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Munich "Munich").

### University of Göttingen

#### Habilitation and Noether's theorem

In early 1915, Noether was invited to return to the University of Göttingen by David Hilbert and [Felix Klein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Felix_Klein "Felix Klein"). Their effort to recruit her was initially blocked by the [philologists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philologists "Philologists") and [historians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historian "Historian") among the philosophical faculty, who insisted that women should not become *[privatdozenten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Privatdozent "Privatdozent")*. In a joint department meeting on the matter, one faculty member protested: "What will our soldiers think when they return to the university and find that they are required to learn at the feet of a woman?" Hilbert, who believed Noether's qualifications were the only important issue and that the sex of the candidate was irrelevant, objected with indignation and scolded those protesting her habilitation. His exact words have not been preserved, but his objection is often said to have included the remark that the university was "not a bathhouse". According to [Pavel Alexandrov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pavel_Alexandrov "Pavel Alexandrov")'s recollection, faculty members' opposition to Noether was based not just in sexism, but also in their objections to her [social-democratic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialist_democracy "Socialist democracy") political beliefs and Jewish ancestry.

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/79/Hilbert.jpg/250px-Hilbert.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Hilbert.jpg)

[David Hilbert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Hilbert "David Hilbert") invited Noether to join Göttingen mathematics department in 1915, challenging the views of some of his colleagues that a woman should not teach at a university.

Noether left for Göttingen in late April; two weeks later her mother died suddenly in Erlangen. She had previously received medical care for an eye condition, but its nature and impact on her death is unknown. At about the same time, Noether's father retired and her brother joined the [German Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Army_(German_Empire) "German Army (German Empire)") to serve in [World War I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I "World War I"). She returned to Erlangen for several weeks, mostly to care for her aging father.

During her first years teaching at Göttingen, she did not have an official position and was not paid. Her lectures often were advertised under Hilbert's name, and Noether would provide "assistance".

Soon after arriving at Göttingen, she demonstrated her capabilities by proving the [theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theorem "Theorem") now known as [Noether's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noether's_theorem "Noether's theorem") which shows that a [conservation law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_law "Conservation law") is associated with any differentiable [symmetry of a physical system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetry_in_physics "Symmetry in physics"). The paper, *Invariante Variationsprobleme*, was presented by a colleague, [Felix Klein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Felix_Klein "Felix Klein"), on 26 July 1918 at a meeting of the Royal Society of Sciences at Göttingen. Noether presumably did not present it herself because she was not a member of the society. American physicists [Leon M. Lederman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leon_M._Lederman "Leon M. Lederman") and [Christopher T. Hill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_T._Hill "Christopher T. Hill") argue in their book *Symmetry and the Beautiful Universe* that Noether's theorem is "certainly one of the most important mathematical theorems ever proved in guiding the development of [modern physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_physics "Modern physics"), possibly on a par with the [Pythagorean theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagorean_theorem "Pythagorean theorem")".

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/29/Mathematik_G%C3%B6ttingen.jpg/250px-Mathematik_G%C3%B6ttingen.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Mathematik_Göttingen.jpg)

The University of Göttingen allowed Noether's *[habilitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habilitation "Habilitation")* in 1919, four years after she had begun lecturing at the school.

When World War I ended, the [German Revolution of 1918–1919](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Revolution_of_1918–1919 "German Revolution of 1918–1919") brought a significant change in social attitudes, including more rights for women. In 1919, the University of Göttingen allowed Noether to proceed with her *[habilitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habilitation "Habilitation")* (eligibility for tenure). Her oral examination was held in late May, and she successfully delivered her *habilitation* lecture in June 1919. Noether became a *privatdozent*, and she delivered that fall semester the first lectures listed under her own name. She was still not paid for her work.

Three years later, she received a letter from [Otto Boelitz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otto_Boelitz?action=edit&redlink=1 "Otto Boelitz"), the [Prussian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prussia "Prussia") Minister for Science, Art, and Public Education, in which he conferred on her the title of *nicht beamteter [ausserordentlicher Professor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ausserordentlicher_Professor "Ausserordentlicher Professor")* (an untenured professor with limited internal administrative rights and functions). This was an unpaid "extraordinary" [professorship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Professor "Professor"), not the higher "ordinary" professorship, which was a civil-service position. It recognized the importance of her work, but still provided no salary. Noether was not paid for her lectures until she was appointed to the special position of *Lehrbeauftragte für Algebra* (*Lecturer for Algebra*) a year later.

#### Work in abstract algebra

Noether's theorem had a significant effect upon classical and quantum mechanics, but among mathematicians she is best remembered for her contributions to [abstract algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstract_algebra "Abstract algebra"). In his introduction to Noether's *Collected Papers*, [Nathan Jacobson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nathan_Jacobson "Nathan Jacobson") wrote that

> The development of abstract algebra, which is one of the most distinctive innovations of twentieth century mathematics, is largely due to her — in published papers, in lectures, and in personal influence on her contemporaries.

Noether's work in algebra began in 1920 when, in collaboration with her protégé Werner Schmeidler, she published a paper about the [theory of ideals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ideal_theory "Ideal theory") in which they defined [left and right ideals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ideal_(ring_theory) "Ideal (ring theory)") in a [ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_(mathematics) "Ring (mathematics)").

The following year she published the paper *Idealtheorie in Ringbereichen*, analyzing [ascending chain conditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ascending_chain_condition "Ascending chain condition") with regards to (mathematical) [ideals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ideal_(ring_theory) "Ideal (ring theory)"), in which she proved the [Lasker–Noether theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lasker–Noether_theorem "Lasker–Noether theorem") in its full generality. Noted algebraist [Irving Kaplansky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irving_Kaplansky "Irving Kaplansky") called this work "revolutionary". The publication gave rise to the term *[Noetherian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noetherian "Noetherian")* for objects which satisfy the ascending chain condition.

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/75/ETH-BIB-Waerden%2C_Bartel_Leendert_van_der_%281903-1996%29-Portr_12109.tif/lossy-page1-250px-ETH-BIB-Waerden%2C_Bartel_Leendert_van_der_%281903-1996%29-Portr_12109.tif.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:ETH-BIB-Waerden,_Bartel_Leendert_van_der_(1903-1996)-Portr_12109.tif)

[B. L. van der Waerden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B._L._van_der_Waerden "B. L. van der Waerden") (pictured in 1980) was heavily influenced by Noether at Göttingen.

In 1924, a young Dutch mathematician, [Bartel Leendert van der Waerden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bartel_Leendert_van_der_Waerden "Bartel Leendert van der Waerden"), arrived at the University of Göttingen. He immediately began working with Noether, who provided invaluable methods of abstract conceptualization. Van der Waerden later said that her originality was "absolute beyond comparison". After returning to Amsterdam, he wrote *[Moderne Algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moderne_Algebra "Moderne Algebra")*, a central two-volume text in the field; its second volume, published in 1931, borrowed heavily from Noether's work. Noether did not seek recognition, but he included as a note in the seventh edition "based in part on lectures by [E. Artin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E._Artin "E. Artin") and E. Noether". Beginning in 1927, Noether worked closely with [Emil Artin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emil_Artin "Emil Artin"), [Richard Brauer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Brauer "Richard Brauer"), and [Helmut Hasse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helmut_Hasse "Helmut Hasse") on [noncommutative algebras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noncommutative_algebra "Noncommutative algebra").

Van der Waerden's visit was part of a convergence of mathematicians from all over the world to Göttingen, which had become a major hub of mathematical and physical research. Russian mathematicians [Pavel Alexandrov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pavel_Alexandrov "Pavel Alexandrov") and [Pavel Urysohn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pavel_Urysohn "Pavel Urysohn") were the first of several in 1923. Between 1926 and 1930, Alexandrov regularly lectured at the university, and he and Noether became good friends. He dubbed her *der Noether*, using *der* as an epithet rather than as the masculine German article. She tried to arrange for him to obtain a position at Göttingen as a regular professor, but was able only to help him secure a scholarship to [Princeton University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University "Princeton University") for the 1927–1928 academic year from the [Rockefeller Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rockefeller_Foundation "Rockefeller Foundation").

#### Graduate students

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/13/EmmyNoether_MFO3096.jpg/250px-EmmyNoether_MFO3096.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:EmmyNoether_MFO3096.jpg)

Noether c. 1930

In Göttingen, Noether supervised more than a dozen doctoral students; most were with [Edmund Landau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmund_Landau "Edmund Landau") and others as she was not allowed to supervise dissertations on her own. Her first was [Grete Hermann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grete_Hermann "Grete Hermann"), who defended her dissertation in February 1925. She is best remembered for her work on the foundations of [quantum mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_mechanics "Quantum mechanics"), but her dissertation was considered an important contribution to [ideal theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ideal_theory "Ideal theory"). Hermann later spoke reverently of her "dissertation-mother".

Around the same time, Heinrich Grell and Rudolf Hölzer wrote their dissertations under Noether. Hölzer died of [tuberculosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuberculosis "Tuberculosis") shortly before his defense. Grell defended his thesis in 1926 and went on to work at the [University of Jena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Jena "University of Jena") and the [University of Halle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Halle "University of Halle"), before losing his teaching license in 1935 due to accusations of homosexual acts. He was later reinstated and became a professor at [Humboldt University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humboldt_University "Humboldt University") in 1948.

Noether then supervised [Werner Weber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Werner_Weber_(mathematician) "Werner Weber (mathematician)") and [Jakob Levitzki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jakob_Levitzki "Jakob Levitzki"), who both defended their theses in 1929. Weber, who was considered only a modest mathematician, would later take part in driving Jewish mathematicians out of Göttingen. Levitzki worked first at [Yale University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yale_University "Yale University") and then at the [Hebrew University of Jerusalem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_University_of_Jerusalem "Hebrew University of Jerusalem") in then British-ruled [Mandatory Palestine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandatory_Palestine "Mandatory Palestine"), making significant contributions (in particular [Levitzky's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levitzky's_theorem "Levitzky's theorem") and the [Hopkins–Levitzki theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hopkins–Levitzki_theorem "Hopkins–Levitzki theorem")) to [ring theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_theory "Ring theory").

Other Noether Boys included [Max Deuring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Deuring "Max Deuring"), [Hans Fitting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Fitting "Hans Fitting"), [Ernst Witt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernst_Witt "Ernst Witt"), [Chiungtze C. Tsen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chiungtze_C._Tsen "Chiungtze C. Tsen"), and [Otto Schilling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otto_Schilling "Otto Schilling"). Deuring, who had been considered the most promising of Noether's students, was awarded his doctorate in 1930. He worked in Hamburg, Marden, and Göttingen and is known for his contributions to [arithmetic geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arithmetic_geometry "Arithmetic geometry"). Fitting graduated in 1931 with a thesis on abelian groups and is remembered for his work in [group theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_theory "Group theory"), particularly [Fitting's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fitting's_theorem "Fitting's theorem") and the [Fitting lemma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fitting_lemma "Fitting lemma"). He died at the age of 31 from a bone disease.

Witt was initially supervised by Noether, but her position was revoked in April 1933 and he was assigned to [Gustav Herglotz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gustav_Herglotz "Gustav Herglotz") instead. He received his PhD in July 1933 with a thesis on the [Riemann-Roch theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann-Roch_theorem "Riemann-Roch theorem") and [zeta-functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeta-function "Zeta-function"), and went on to make several contributions that [now bear his name](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_things_named_after_Ernst_Witt "List of things named after Ernst Witt"). Tsen, best remembered for proving [Tsen's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsen's_theorem "Tsen's theorem"), received his doctorate that December. He returned to [China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/China "China") in 1935 and started teaching at [National Chekiang University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Chekiang_University "National Chekiang University"), but died only five years later. Schilling also began studying under Noether, but was forced to find a new advisor due to Noether's emigration. Under [Helmut Hasse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helmut_Hasse "Helmut Hasse"), he completed his PhD in 1934 at the [University of Marburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Marburg "University of Marburg"). He later worked as a [post doc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post_doc "Post doc") at [Trinity College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College,_Cambridge "Trinity College, Cambridge"), before moving to the United States.

Noether's other students were Wilhelm Dörnte, who received his doctorate in 1927 with a thesis on groups, Werner Vorbeck, who did so in 1935 with a thesis on [splitting fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Splitting_field "Splitting field"), and Wolfgang Wichmann, who did so 1936 with a thesis on [p-adic theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P-adic_number "P-adic number"). There is no information about the first two, but it is known that Wichmann supported a student initiative that unsuccessfully attempted to revoke Noether's dismissal and died as a soldier on the [Eastern Front](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Front_(World_War_II) "Eastern Front (World War II)") during [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II").

#### Noether school

Noether developed a close circle of mathematicians beyond just her doctoral students who shared her approach to abstract algebra and contributed to the field's development, a group often referred to as the Noether school. An example of this is her close work with [Wolfgang Krull](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolfgang_Krull "Wolfgang Krull"), who greatly advanced [commutative algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative_algebra "Commutative algebra") with his [*Hauptidealsatz*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krull's_principal_ideal_theorem "Krull's principal ideal theorem") and his [dimension theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krull_dimension "Krull dimension") for commutative rings. Another is [Gottfried Köthe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gottfried_Köthe "Gottfried Köthe"), who contributed to the development of the theory of [hypercomplex quantities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypercomplex_number "Hypercomplex number") using Noether and Krull's methods.

In addition to her mathematical insight, Noether was respected for her consideration of others. She sometimes acted rudely toward those who disagreed with her, but gained a reputation for helpfulness and patient guidance of new students. Her loyalty to mathematical precision caused one colleague to name her "a severe critic", but she combined this demand for accuracy with a nurturing attitude. In Noether's obituary, Van der Waerden described her as

> Completely unegotistical and free of vanity, she never claimed anything for herself, but promoted the works of her students above all.

Noether showed a devotion to her subject and her students that extended beyond the academic day. Once, when the building was closed for a state holiday, she gathered the class on the steps outside, led them through the woods, and lectured at a local coffee house. Later, after [Nazi Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi_Germany "Nazi Germany") dismissed her from teaching, she invited students into her home to discuss their plans for the future and mathematical concepts.

#### Influential lectures

Noether's frugal lifestyle was at first due to her being denied pay for her work. Even after the university began paying her a small salary in 1923, she continued to live a simple and modest life. She was paid more generously later in her life, but saved half of her salary to bequeath to her nephew, [Gottfried E. Noether](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gottfried_E._Noether "Gottfried E. Noether").

Biographers suggest that she was mostly unconcerned about appearance and manners, focusing on her studies. [Olga Taussky-Todd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olga_Taussky-Todd "Olga Taussky-Todd"), a distinguished algebraist taught by Noether, described a luncheon during which Noether, wholly engrossed in a discussion of mathematics, "gesticulated wildly" as she ate and "spilled her food constantly and wiped it off from her dress, completely unperturbed". Appearance-conscious students cringed as she retrieved the handkerchief from her blouse and ignored the increasing disarray of her hair during a lecture. Two female students once approached her during a break in a two-hour class to express their concern, but they were unable to break through the energetic mathematical discussion she was having with other students.

Noether did not follow a lesson plan for her lectures. She spoke quickly and her lectures were considered difficult to follow by many, including [Carl Ludwig Siegel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Ludwig_Siegel "Carl Ludwig Siegel") and [Paul Dubreil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Dubreil "Paul Dubreil"). Students who disliked her style often felt alienated. "Outsiders" who occasionally visited Noether's lectures usually spent only half an hour in the room before leaving in frustration or confusion. A regular student said of one such instance: "The enemy has been defeated; he has cleared out."

She used her lectures as a spontaneous discussion time with her students, to think through and clarify important problems in mathematics. Some of her most important results were developed in these lectures, and the lecture notes of her students formed the basis for several important textbooks, such as those of van der Waerden and Deuring. Noether transmitted an infectious mathematical enthusiasm to her most dedicated students, who relished their lively conversations with her.

Several of her colleagues attended her lectures and she sometimes allowed others (including her students) to receive credit for her ideas, resulting in much of her work appearing in papers not under her name. Noether was recorded as having given at least five semester-long courses at Göttingen:

- Winter 1924–1925: *Gruppentheorie und hyperkomplexe Zahlen* [*Group Theory and Hypercomplex Numbers*]
- Winter 1927–1928: *Hyperkomplexe Grössen und Darstellungstheorie* [*Hypercomplex Quantities and Representation Theory*]
- Summer 1928: *Nichtkommutative Algebra* [*Noncommutative Algebra*]
- Summer 1929: *Nichtkommutative Arithmetik* [*Noncommutative Arithmetic*]
- Winter 1929–1930: *Algebra der hyperkomplexen Grössen* [*Algebra of Hypercomplex Quantities*]

### Moscow State University

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/cc/Moscow_05-2012_Mokhovaya_05.jpg/250px-Moscow_05-2012_Mokhovaya_05.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Moscow_05-2012_Mokhovaya_05.jpg)

Noether taught at [Moscow State University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moscow_State_University "Moscow State University") in 1928–1929.

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b2/Paul_S_Alexandroff_2.jpg/250px-Paul_S_Alexandroff_2.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Paul_S_Alexandroff_2.jpg)

[Pavel Alexandrov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pavel_Alexandrov "Pavel Alexandrov")

In 1928–1929, Noether accepted an invitation to [Moscow State University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moscow_State_University "Moscow State University"), where she continued working with [P. S. Alexandrov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P._S._Alexandrov "P. S. Alexandrov"). In addition to carrying on with her research, she taught classes in abstract algebra and [algebraic geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_geometry "Algebraic geometry"). She worked with the topologists [Lev Pontryagin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lev_Pontryagin "Lev Pontryagin") and [Nikolai Chebotaryov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nikolai_Chebotaryov "Nikolai Chebotaryov"), who later praised her contributions to the development of [Galois theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galois_theory "Galois theory").

Politics was not central to her life, but Noether took a keen interest in political matters and, according to Alexandrov, showed considerable support for the [Russian Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Revolution "Russian Revolution"). She was especially happy to see [Soviet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet "Soviet") advances in the fields of science and mathematics, which she considered indicative of new opportunities made possible by the [Bolshevik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bolshevik "Bolshevik") project. This attitude caused her problems in Germany, culminating in her eviction from a [pension lodging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pension_lodging "Pension lodging") building, after student leaders complained of living with "a Marxist-leaning Jewess". [Hermann Weyl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann_Weyl "Hermann Weyl") recalled that "During the wild times after the [Revolution of 1918](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Revolution_of_1918–1919 "German Revolution of 1918–1919")," Noether "sided more or less with the [Social Democrats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_Democratic_Party_of_Germany "Social Democratic Party of Germany")". She was a member of the [Independent Social Democrats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_Social_Democratic_Party_of_Germany "Independent Social Democratic Party of Germany") from 1919 to 1922, a short-lived splinter party. In the words of logician and historian [Colin McLarty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colin_McLarty "Colin McLarty"), "she was not a Bolshevist, but was not afraid to be called one."

Noether planned to return to Moscow, an effort for which she received support from Alexandrov. After she left Germany in 1933, he tried to help her gain a chair at Moscow State University through the [Soviet Education Ministry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Narkompros "Narkompros"). This proved unsuccessful, but they corresponded frequently during the 1930s, and in 1935 she made plans for a return to the Soviet Union.

### Recognition

In 1932, Emmy Noether and [Emil Artin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emil_Artin "Emil Artin") received the [Ackermann–Teubner Memorial Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ackermann–Teubner_Memorial_Award "Ackermann–Teubner Memorial Award") for their contributions to mathematics. The prize included a monetary reward of 500 [ℛ︁ℳ︁](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reichsmark "Reichsmark") and was seen as a long-overdue official recognition of her considerable work in the field. Nevertheless, her colleagues expressed frustration at the fact that she was not elected to the [Göttingen *Gesellschaft der Wissenschaften*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Göttingen_Academy_of_Sciences "Göttingen Academy of Sciences") (academy of sciences) and was never promoted to the position of *[Ordentlicher Professor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordentlicher_Professor "Ordentlicher Professor")* (full professor).

Noether's colleagues celebrated her fiftieth birthday, in 1932, in typical mathematicians' style. [Helmut Hasse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helmut_Hasse "Helmut Hasse") dedicated an article to her in the *[Mathematische Annalen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematische_Annalen "Mathematische Annalen")*, wherein he confirmed her suspicion that some aspects of [noncommutative algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noncommutative_algebra "Noncommutative algebra") are simpler than those of [commutative algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative_algebra "Commutative algebra"), by proving a noncommutative [reciprocity law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_reciprocity "Quadratic reciprocity"). This pleased her immensely. He also sent her a mathematical riddle, which he called the "mμν-riddle of syllables". She solved it immediately, but the riddle has been lost.

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/03/Internationaler_Mathematikerkongress_Z%C3%BCrich_1932_-_ETH_BIB_Portr_10680-FL_%28Johannes_Meiner%29.jpg/250px-Internationaler_Mathematikerkongress_Z%C3%BCrich_1932_-_ETH_BIB_Portr_10680-FL_%28Johannes_Meiner%29.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Internationaler_Mathematikerkongress_Zürich_1932_-_ETH_BIB_Portr_10680-FL_(Johannes_Meiner).jpg)

Noether visited [Zürich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zürich "Zürich") in 1932 to deliver a [plenary address at the International Congress of Mathematicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plenary_address_at_the_International_Congress_of_Mathematicians "Plenary address at the International Congress of Mathematicians").

In September of the same year, Noether delivered a plenary address (*großer Vortrag*) on "Hyper-complex systems in their relations to commutative algebra and to number theory" at the [International Congress of Mathematicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Congress_of_Mathematicians "International Congress of Mathematicians") in [Zürich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zürich "Zürich"). The congress was attended by 800 people, including Noether's colleagues [Hermann Weyl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann_Weyl "Hermann Weyl"), [Edmund Landau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmund_Landau "Edmund Landau"), and [Wolfgang Krull](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolfgang_Krull "Wolfgang Krull"). There were 420 official participants and twenty-one plenary addresses presented. Apparently, Noether's prominent speaking position was a recognition of the importance of her contributions to mathematics. The 1932 congress is sometimes described as the high point of her career.

### Expulsion from Göttingen by Nazi Germany

When [Adolf Hitler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adolf_Hitler "Adolf Hitler") became the [German *Reichskanzler*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chancellor_of_Germany_(German_Reich) "Chancellor of Germany (German Reich)") in January 1933, [Nazi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi "Nazi") activity around the country increased dramatically. At the University of Göttingen, the German Student Association led the attack on the "un-German spirit" attributed to Jews and was aided by *[privatdozent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Privatdozent "Privatdozent")* and Noether's former student [Werner Weber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Werner_Weber_(mathematician) "Werner Weber (mathematician)"). [Antisemitic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antisemitic "Antisemitic") attitudes created a climate hostile to Jewish professors. One young protester reportedly demanded: "Aryan students want [Aryan mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deutsche_Mathematik "Deutsche Mathematik") and not Jewish mathematics."

One of the first actions of Hitler's administration was the [Law for the Restoration of the Professional Civil Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_for_the_Restoration_of_the_Professional_Civil_Service "Law for the Restoration of the Professional Civil Service") which removed Jews and politically suspect government employees (including university professors) from their jobs unless they had "demonstrated their loyalty to Germany" by [serving in World War I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frontkämpferprivileg "Frontkämpferprivileg"). In April 1933, Noether received a notice from the Prussian Ministry for Sciences, Art, and Public Education which read: "On the basis of paragraph 3 of the Civil Service Code of 7 April 1933, I hereby withdraw from you the right to teach at the University of Göttingen." Several of Noether's colleagues, including [Max Born](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Born "Max Born") and [Richard Courant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Courant "Richard Courant"), also had their positions revoked.

Noether accepted the decision calmly, providing support for others during this difficult time. [Hermann Weyl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann_Weyl "Hermann Weyl") later wrote that "Emmy Noether – her courage, her frankness, her unconcern about her own fate, her conciliatory spirit – was in the midst of all the hatred and meanness, despair and sorrow surrounding us, a moral solace." Typically, Noether remained focused on mathematics, gathering students in her apartment to discuss [class field theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Class_field_theory "Class field theory"). When one of her students appeared in the uniform of the Nazi [paramilitary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paramilitary "Paramilitary") organization *[Sturmabteilung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sturmabteilung "Sturmabteilung")* (SA), she showed no sign of agitation and, reportedly, even laughed about it later.

### Refuge at Bryn Mawr and Princeton

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d0/Entrance_Bryn_Mawr.JPG/250px-Entrance_Bryn_Mawr.JPG)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Entrance_Bryn_Mawr.JPG)

[Bryn Mawr College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bryn_Mawr_College "Bryn Mawr College") provided a welcoming home for Noether during the last two years of her life.

As dozens of newly unemployed professors began searching for positions outside of Germany, their colleagues in the United States sought to provide assistance and job opportunities for them. [Albert Einstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Einstein "Albert Einstein") and [Hermann Weyl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann_Weyl "Hermann Weyl") were appointed by the [Institute for Advanced Study](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_for_Advanced_Study "Institute for Advanced Study") in [Princeton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton,_New_Jersey "Princeton, New Jersey"), while others worked to find a sponsor required for legal [immigration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immigration "Immigration"). Noether was contacted by representatives of two educational institutions: [Bryn Mawr College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bryn_Mawr_College "Bryn Mawr College"), in the United States, and [Somerville College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somerville_College "Somerville College") at the [University of Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Oxford "University of Oxford"), in England. After a series of negotiations with the [Rockefeller Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rockefeller_Foundation "Rockefeller Foundation"), a grant to Bryn Mawr was approved for Noether and she took a position there, starting in late 1933.

At Bryn Mawr, Noether met and befriended [Anna Wheeler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anna_Johnson_Pell_Wheeler "Anna Johnson Pell Wheeler"), who had studied at Göttingen just before Noether arrived there. Another source of support at the college was the Bryn Mawr president, [Marion Edwards Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marion_Edwards_Park "Marion Edwards Park"), who enthusiastically invited mathematicians in the area to "see Dr. Noether in action!"

During her time at Bryn Mawr, Noether formed a group, sometimes called the Noether girls, of four post-doctoral (Grace Shover Quinn, [Marie Johanna Weiss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marie_Johanna_Weiss "Marie Johanna Weiss"), [Olga Taussky-Todd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olga_Taussky-Todd "Olga Taussky-Todd"), who all went on to have successful careers in mathematics) and doctoral students (Ruth Stauffer). They enthusiastically worked through [van der Waerden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Van_der_Waerden "Van der Waerden")'s *Moderne Algebra I* and parts of [Erich Hecke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erich_Hecke "Erich Hecke")'s *Theorie der algebraischen Zahlen* (*Theory of algebraic numbers*). Stauffer was Noether's only doctoral student in the United States, but Noether died shortly before she graduated. She took her examination with [Richard Brauer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Brauer "Richard Brauer") and received her degree in June 1935, with a thesis concerning separable [normal extensions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_extension "Normal extension"). After her doctorate, Stauffer worked as a teacher for a short period and as a statistician for over 30 years.

In 1934, Noether began lecturing at the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton upon the invitation of [Abraham Flexner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraham_Flexner "Abraham Flexner") and [Oswald Veblen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oswald_Veblen "Oswald Veblen"). She also worked with [Abraham Albert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraham_Albert "Abraham Albert") and [Harry Vandiver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harry_Vandiver "Harry Vandiver"). She remarked about [Princeton University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University "Princeton University") that she was not welcome at "the men's university, where nothing female is admitted".

Her time in the United States was pleasant, as she was surrounded by supportive colleagues and absorbed in her favorite subjects. In mid-1934, she briefly returned to Germany to see Emil Artin and her brother [Fritz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fritz_Noether "Fritz Noether"). The latter, after having been forced out of his job at the [Technische Hochschule Breslau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technische_Hochschule_Breslau "Technische Hochschule Breslau"), had accepted a position at the Research Institute for Mathematics and Mechanics in [Tomsk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomsk "Tomsk"), in the Siberian Federal District of Russia.

Many of her former colleagues had been forced out of the universities, but she was able to use the library in Göttingen as a "foreign scholar". Without incident, Noether returned to the United States and her studies at Bryn Mawr.

### Death

In April 1935, doctors discovered a [tumor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tumor "Tumor") in Noether's [pelvis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pelvis "Pelvis"). Worried about complications from surgery, they ordered two days of bed rest first. During the operation they discovered an [ovarian cyst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ovarian_cyst "Ovarian cyst") "the size of a large [cantaloupe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cantaloupe "Cantaloupe")". Two smaller tumors in her [uterus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uterus "Uterus") appeared to be benign and were not removed to avoid prolonging surgery. For three days she appeared to convalesce normally, and she recovered quickly from a [circulatory collapse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circulatory_collapse "Circulatory collapse") on the fourth. On 14 April, Noether fell unconscious, her temperature soared to 109 °F (42.8 °C), and she died. "[I]t is not easy to say what had occurred in Dr. Noether", one of the physicians wrote. "It is possible that there was some form of unusual and virulent infection, which struck the base of the brain where the heat centers are supposed to be located." She was 53.

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c5/Bryn_Mawr_College_Cloisters.JPG/250px-Bryn_Mawr_College_Cloisters.JPG)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Bryn_Mawr_College_Cloisters.JPG)

Noether's ashes were placed under the cloistered walkway of Bryn Mawr's [Old Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bryn_Mawr_College#Old_Library_(previously_M._Carey_Thomas_Library_and_College_Hall) "Bryn Mawr College").

A few days after Noether's death, her friends and associates at Bryn Mawr held a small memorial service at College President Park's house. Hermann Weyl and Richard Brauer both traveled from Princeton and delivered eulogies. In the months that followed, written tributes began to appear around the globe: Albert Einstein joined van der Waerden, Weyl, and [Pavel Alexandrov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pavel_Alexandrov "Pavel Alexandrov") in paying their respects. Her body was cremated and the ashes interred under the walkway around the cloisters of the [Old Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bryn_Mawr_College#Old_Library_(previously_M._Carey_Thomas_Library_and_College_Hall) "Bryn Mawr College") at Bryn Mawr.

## Contributions to mathematics and physics

Noether's work in [abstract algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstract_algebra "Abstract algebra") and [topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topology "Topology") was influential in mathematics, while [Noether's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noether's_theorem "Noether's theorem") has widespread consequences for [theoretical physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theoretical_physics "Theoretical physics") and [dynamical systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamical_system "Dynamical system"). Noether showed an acute propensity for abstract thought, which allowed her to approach problems of mathematics in fresh and original ways. Her friend and colleague [Hermann Weyl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann_Weyl "Hermann Weyl") described her scholarly output in three epochs:

> (1) the period of relative dependence, 1907–1919
>
> (2) the investigations grouped around the general theory of ideals 1920–1926
>
> (3) the study of the non-commutative algebras, their representations by linear transformations, and their application to the study of commutative number fields and their arithmetics

— [Weyl 1935](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emmy_Noether#CITEREFWeyl1935)

In the first epoch (1907–1919), Noether dealt primarily with [differential and algebraic invariants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invariant_theory "Invariant theory"), beginning with her dissertation under [Paul Gordan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Gordan "Paul Gordan"). Her mathematical horizons broadened, and her work became more general and abstract, as she became acquainted with the work of [David Hilbert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Hilbert "David Hilbert"), through close interactions with a successor to Gordan, [Ernst Sigismund Fischer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernst_Sigismund_Fischer "Ernst Sigismund Fischer"). Shortly after moving to Göttingen in 1915, she proved the two [Noether's theorems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noether's_theorem "Noether's theorem"), "one of the most important mathematical theorems ever proved in guiding the development of modern physics".

In the second epoch (1920–1926), Noether devoted herself to developing the theory of [mathematical rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_(mathematics) "Ring (mathematics)"). In the third epoch (1927–1935), Noether focused on [noncommutative algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noncommutative_algebra "Noncommutative algebra"), [linear transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_transformations "Linear transformations"), and commutative number fields. The results of Noether's first epoch were impressive and useful, but her fame among mathematicians rests more on the groundbreaking work she did in her second and third epochs, as noted by Hermann Weyl and B. L. van der Waerden in their obituaries of her.

In these epochs, she was not merely applying ideas and methods of the earlier mathematicians; rather, she was crafting new systems of mathematical definitions that would be used by future mathematicians. In particular, she developed a completely new theory of [ideals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ideal_(ring_theory) "Ideal (ring theory)") in [rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_(mathematics) "Ring (mathematics)"), generalizing the earlier work of [Richard Dedekind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Dedekind "Richard Dedekind"). She is also renowned for developing ascending chain conditions – a simple finiteness condition that yielded powerful results in her hands. Such conditions and the theory of ideals enabled Noether to generalize many older results and to treat old problems from a new perspective, such as the topics of [algebraic invariants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_invariant "Algebraic invariant") that had been studied by her father and [elimination theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elimination_theory "Elimination theory"), discussed below.

Noether's most important contributions to mathematics were to the development of an emerging new field, [abstract algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstract_algebra "Abstract algebra").

Unlike most mathematicians, she did not make abstractions by generalizing from known examples; rather, she worked directly with the abstractions. In his obituary of Noether, van der Waerden recalled that

> The maxim by which Emmy Noether was guided throughout her work might be formulated as follows: "Any relationships between numbers, functions, and operations become transparent, generally applicable, and fully productive only after they have been isolated from their particular objects and been formulated as universally valid concepts."

This is the *begriffliche Mathematik* (purely conceptual mathematics) that was characteristic of Noether. This style of mathematics was consequently adopted by other mathematicians, especially in the (then new) field of abstract algebra.

### First epoch (1908–1919)

#### Algebraic invariant theory

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/96/Emmy_Noether_-_Table_of_invariants_2.jpg/250px-Emmy_Noether_-_Table_of_invariants_2.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Emmy_Noether_-_Table_of_invariants_2.jpg)

Table 2 from Noether's dissertation on invariant theory. This table collects 202 of the 331 invariants of ternary biquadratic forms. These forms are graded in two variables *x* and *u*. The horizontal direction of the table lists the invariants with increasing grades in *x*, while the vertical direction lists them with increasing grades in *u*.

Much of Noether's work in the first epoch of her career was associated with [invariant theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invariant_theory "Invariant theory"), principally [algebraic invariant theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_invariant_theory "Algebraic invariant theory"). Invariant theory is concerned with expressions that remain constant (invariant) under a [group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_(mathematics) "Group (mathematics)") of transformations. As an everyday example, if a rigid [metre-stick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metre-stick "Metre-stick") is rotated, the coordinates of its endpoints change, but its length remains the same. A more sophisticated example of an *invariant* is the [discriminant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discriminant "Discriminant") *B*2 − 4*AC* of a homogeneous quadratic polynomial *Ax*2 + *Bxy* + *Cy*2, where x and y are [indeterminates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indeterminate_(variable) "Indeterminate (variable)"). The discriminant is called "invariant" because it is not changed by linear substitutions *x* → *ax* + *by* and *y* → *cx* + *dy* with determinant *ad* − *bc* = 1. These substitutions form the [special linear group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_linear_group "Special linear group") *SL*2.

One can ask for all polynomials in A, B, and C that are unchanged by the action of *SL*2; these turn out to be the polynomials in the discriminant. More generally, one can ask for the invariants of [homogeneous polynomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homogeneous_polynomial "Homogeneous polynomial") *A*0*x**r**y*0 + ... + *Arx*0*y**r* of higher degree, which will be certain polynomials in the coefficients *A*0, ..., *Ar*, and more generally still, one can ask the similar question for homogeneous polynomials in more than two variables.

One of the main goals of invariant theory was to solve the "*finite basis problem*". The sum or product of any two invariants is invariant, and the finite basis problem asked whether it was possible to get all the invariants by starting with a finite list of invariants, called *generators*, and then, adding or multiplying the generators together. For example, the discriminant gives a finite basis (with one element) for the invariants of a quadratic polynomial.

Noether's advisor, Paul Gordan, was known as the "king of invariant theory", and his chief contribution to mathematics was his 1870 solution of the finite basis problem for invariants of homogeneous polynomials in two variables. He proved this by giving a constructive method for finding all of the invariants and their generators, but was not able to carry out this constructive approach for invariants in three or more variables. In 1890, David Hilbert proved a similar statement for the invariants of homogeneous polynomials in any number of variables. Furthermore, his method worked, not only for the special linear group, but also for some of its subgroups such as the [special orthogonal group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_orthogonal_group "Special orthogonal group").

Noether followed Gordan's lead, writing her doctoral dissertation and several other publications on invariant theory. She extended Gordan's results and also built upon Hilbert's research. Later, she would disparage this work, finding it of little interest and admitting to forgetting the details of it. Hermann Weyl wrote,

> [A] greater contrast is hardly imaginable than between her first paper, the dissertation, and her works of maturity; for the former is an extreme example of formal computations and the latter constitute an extreme and grandiose example of conceptual axiomatic thinking in mathematics.

#### Galois theory

[Galois theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galois_theory "Galois theory") concerns transformations of [number fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_(mathematics) "Field (mathematics)") that [permute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permute "Permute") the roots of an equation. Consider a polynomial equation of a variable *x* of [degree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degree_of_a_polynomial "Degree of a polynomial") *n*, in which the coefficients are drawn from some [ground field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ground_field "Ground field"), which might be, for example, the field of [real numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_number "Real number"), [rational numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_number "Rational number"), or the [integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integer "Integer") [modulo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modular_arithmetic "Modular arithmetic") 7. There may or may not be choices of *x*, which make this polynomial evaluate to zero. Such choices, if they exist, are called [roots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_of_a_function "Root of a function"). For example, if the polynomial is *x*2 + 1 and the field is the real numbers, then the polynomial has no roots, because any choice of *x* makes the polynomial greater than or equal to one. If the field is [extended](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_extension "Field extension") then the polynomial may gain roots, and if it is extended enough, then it always has a number of roots equal to its degree.

Continuing the previous example, if the field is enlarged to the complex numbers, then the polynomial gains two roots, +*i* and −*i*, where *i* is the [imaginary unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imaginary_unit "Imaginary unit"), that is, *i* 2 = −1. More generally, the extension field in which a polynomial can be factored into its roots is known as the [splitting field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Splitting_field "Splitting field") of the polynomial.

The [Galois group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galois_group "Galois group") of a polynomial is the set of all transformations of the splitting field which preserve the ground field and the roots of the polynomial. (These transformations are called [automorphisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automorphism "Automorphism").) The Galois group of *x*2 + 1 consists of two elements: The identity transformation, which sends every complex number to itself, and [complex conjugation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_conjugation "Complex conjugation"), which sends +*i* to −*i*. Since the Galois group does not change the ground field, it leaves the coefficients of the polynomial unchanged, so it must leave the set of all roots unchanged. Each root can move to another root, so transformation determines a [permutation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permutation "Permutation") of the *n* roots among themselves. The significance of the Galois group derives from the [fundamental theorem of Galois theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_theorem_of_Galois_theory "Fundamental theorem of Galois theory"), which proves that the fields lying between the ground field and the splitting field are in one-to-one correspondence with the [subgroups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subgroup "Subgroup") of the Galois group.

In 1918, Noether published a paper on the [inverse Galois problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse_Galois_problem "Inverse Galois problem"). Instead of determining the Galois group of transformations of a given field and its extension, Noether asked whether, given a field and a group, it always is possible to find an extension of the field that has the given group as its Galois group. She reduced this to "[Noether's problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noether's_problem "Noether's problem")", which asks whether the fixed field of a subgroup *G* of the [permutation group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetric_group "Symmetric group") *S**n* acting on the field *k*(*x*1, ..., *x**n*) always is a pure [transcendental extension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transcendental_extension "Transcendental extension") of the field *k*. (She first mentioned this problem in a 1913 paper, where she attributed the problem to her colleague [Fischer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernst_Sigismund_Fischer "Ernst Sigismund Fischer").) She showed this was true for *n* = 2, 3, or 4. In 1969, [Richard Swan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Swan "Richard Swan") found a counter-example to Noether's problem, with *n* = 47 and *G* a [cyclic group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclic_group "Cyclic group") of order 47 (although this group can be realized as a [Galois group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galois_group "Galois group") over the rationals in other ways). The inverse Galois problem remains unsolved.

#### Physics

Noether was brought to [Göttingen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Göttingen "Göttingen") in 1915 by David Hilbert and Felix Klein, who wanted her expertise in invariant theory to help them in understanding [general relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_relativity "General relativity"), a geometrical theory of [gravitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitation "Gravitation") developed mainly by [Albert Einstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Einstein "Albert Einstein"). Hilbert had observed that the [conservation of energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_of_energy "Conservation of energy") seemed to be violated in general relativity, because gravitational energy could itself gravitate. Noether provided the resolution of this paradox, and a fundamental tool of modern [theoretical physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theoretical_physics "Theoretical physics"), in a 1918 paper. This paper presented two theorems, of which the first is known as [Noether's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noether's_theorem "Noether's theorem"). Together, these theorems not only solve the problem for general relativity, but also determine the conserved quantities for *every* system of physical laws that possesses some continuous symmetry. Upon receiving her work, Einstein wrote to Hilbert:

> Yesterday I received from Miss Noether a very interesting paper on invariants. I'm impressed that such things can be understood in such a general way. The old guard at Göttingen should take some lessons from Miss Noether! She seems to know her stuff.

For illustration, if a physical system behaves the same, regardless of how it is oriented in space, the physical laws that govern it are rotationally symmetric; from this symmetry, Noether's theorem shows the [angular momentum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angular_momentum "Angular momentum") of the system must be conserved. The physical system itself need not be symmetric; a jagged asteroid tumbling in space [conserves angular momentum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_of_angular_momentum "Conservation of angular momentum") despite its asymmetry. Rather, the symmetry of the *physical laws* governing the system is responsible for the conservation law. As another example, if a physical experiment works the same way at any place and at any time, then its laws are symmetric under continuous translations in space and time; by Noether's theorem, these symmetries account for the [conservation laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_laws "Conservation laws") of [linear momentum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_momentum "Linear momentum") and [energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy "Energy") within this system, respectively.

At the time, physicists were not familiar with [Sophus Lie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sophus_Lie "Sophus Lie")'s theory of [continuous groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lie_group "Lie group"), on which Noether had built. Many physicists first learned of Noether's theorem from an article by [Edward Lee Hill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Lee_Hill?action=edit&redlink=1 "Edward Lee Hill") that presented only a special case of it. Consequently, the full scope of her result was not immediately appreciated. During the latter half of the 20th century, Noether's theorem became a fundamental tool of modern [theoretical physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theoretical_physics "Theoretical physics"), because of the insight it gives into conservation laws, and also as a practical calculation tool. Her theorem allows researchers to determine the conserved quantities from the observed symmetries of a physical system. Conversely, it facilitates the description of a physical system based on classes of hypothetical physical laws. For illustration, suppose that a new physical phenomenon is discovered. Noether's theorem provides a test for theoretical models of the phenomenon: If the theory has a continuous symmetry, then Noether's theorem guarantees that the theory has a conserved quantity, and for the theory to be correct, this conservation must be observable in experiments.

### Second epoch (1920–1926)

A collection of subsets of a given set satisfies the [ascending chain condition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ascending_chain_condition "Ascending chain condition") if every ascending sequence becomes constant after a finite number of steps. It satisfies the descending chain condition if any descending sequence becomes constant after a finite number of steps. Chain conditions can be used to show that every set of sub-objects has a maximal/minimal element, or that a complex object can be generated by a smaller number of elements.

Many types of objects in [abstract algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstract_algebra "Abstract algebra") can satisfy chain conditions, and usually if they satisfy an ascending chain condition, they are called *[Noetherian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noetherian_(disambiguation) "Noetherian (disambiguation)")* in her honor. By definition, a [Noetherian ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noetherian_ring "Noetherian ring") satisfies an ascending chain condition on its left and right ideals, whereas a [Noetherian group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noetherian_group "Noetherian group") is defined as a group in which every strictly ascending chain of subgroups is finite. A [Noetherian module](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noetherian_module "Noetherian module") is a [module](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Module_(mathematics) "Module (mathematics)") in which every strictly ascending chain of submodules becomes constant after a finite number of steps. A [Noetherian space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noetherian_space "Noetherian space") is a [topological space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_space "Topological space") whose open subsets satisfy the ascending chain condition; this definition makes the [spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectrum_of_a_ring "Spectrum of a ring") of a Noetherian ring a Noetherian topological space.

The chain condition often is "inherited" by sub-objects. For example, all subspaces of a Noetherian space are Noetherian themselves; all subgroups and quotient groups of a Noetherian group are Noetherian; and, *[mutatis mutandis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutatis_mutandis "Mutatis mutandis")*, the same holds for submodules and quotient modules of a Noetherian module. The chain condition also may be inherited by combinations or extensions of a Noetherian object. For example, finite direct sums of Noetherian rings are Noetherian, as is the [ring of formal power series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_of_formal_power_series "Ring of formal power series") over a Noetherian ring.

Another application of such chain conditions is in [Noetherian induction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noetherian_induction "Noetherian induction") – also known as [well-founded induction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Well-founded_induction "Well-founded induction") – which is a generalization of [mathematical induction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_induction "Mathematical induction"). It frequently is used to reduce general statements about collections of objects to statements about specific objects in that collection. Suppose that *S* is a [partially ordered set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partially_ordered_set "Partially ordered set"). One way of proving a statement about the objects of *S* is to assume the existence of a [counterexample](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counterexample "Counterexample") and deduce a contradiction, thereby proving the [contrapositive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contrapositive "Contrapositive") of the original statement. The basic premise of Noetherian induction is that every non-empty subset of *S* contains a minimal element. In particular, the set of all counterexamples contains a minimal element, the *minimal counterexample*. In order to prove the original statement, therefore, it suffices to prove something seemingly much weaker: For any counter-example, there is a smaller counter-example.

#### Commutative rings, ideals, and modules

Noether's paper, *Idealtheorie in Ringbereichen* (*Theory of Ideals in Ring Domains*, 1921), is the foundation of general commutative [ring theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_theory "Ring theory"), and gives one of the first general definitions of a [commutative ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative_ring "Commutative ring"). Before her paper, most results in commutative algebra were restricted to special examples of commutative rings, such as polynomial rings over fields or rings of algebraic integers. Noether proved that in a ring which satisfies the ascending chain condition on [ideals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ideal_(ring_theory) "Ideal (ring theory)"), every ideal is finitely generated. In 1943, French mathematician [Claude Chevalley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claude_Chevalley "Claude Chevalley") coined the term *[Noetherian ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noetherian_ring "Noetherian ring")* to describe this property. A major result in Noether's 1921 paper is the [Lasker–Noether theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lasker–Noether_theorem "Lasker–Noether theorem"), which extends Lasker's theorem on the primary decomposition of ideals of polynomial rings to all Noetherian rings. The Lasker–Noether theorem can be viewed as a generalization of the [fundamental theorem of arithmetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_theorem_of_arithmetic "Fundamental theorem of arithmetic") which states that any positive integer can be expressed as a product of [prime numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_number "Prime number"), and that this decomposition is unique.

Noether's work *Abstrakter Aufbau der Idealtheorie in algebraischen Zahl- und Funktionenkörpern* (*Abstract Structure of the Theory of Ideals in Algebraic Number and Function Fields*, 1927) characterized the rings in which the ideals have unique factorization into prime ideals (now called [Dedekind domains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dedekind_domain "Dedekind domain")). Noether showed that these rings were characterized by five conditions: they must satisfy the ascending and descending chain conditions, they must possess a unit element, but no [zero divisors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_divisor "Zero divisor"), and they must be [integrally closed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrally_closed_domain "Integrally closed domain") in their associated field of fractions. This paper also contains what now are called the [isomorphism theorems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphism_theorems "Isomorphism theorems"), which describe some fundamental [natural isomorphisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_isomorphism "Natural isomorphism"), and some other basic results on Noetherian and [Artinian modules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artinian_module "Artinian module").

#### Elimination theory

In 1923–1924, Noether applied her ideal theory to [elimination theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elimination_theory "Elimination theory") in a formulation that she attributed to her student, Kurt Hentzelt. She showed that fundamental theorems about the [factorization of polynomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Factorization_of_polynomials "Factorization of polynomials") could be carried over directly.

Traditionally, elimination theory is concerned with eliminating one or more variables from a system of polynomial equations, often by the method of [resultants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resultant "Resultant").
For illustration, a system of equations often can be written in the form

:   Mv = 0

where a matrix (or [linear transform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_transform "Linear transform")) M (without the variable x) times a vector v (that only has non-zero powers of x) is equal to the zero vector, 0. Hence, the [determinant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Determinant "Determinant") of the matrix M must be zero, providing a new equation in which the variable x has been eliminated.

#### Invariant theory of finite groups

Techniques such as Hilbert's original non-constructive solution to the finite basis problem could not be used to get quantitative information about the invariants of a group action, and furthermore, they did not apply to all group actions. In her 1915 paper, Noether found a solution to the finite basis problem for a finite group of transformations *G* acting on a finite-dimensional vector space over a field of characteristic zero. Her solution shows that the ring of invariants is generated by homogeneous invariants whose degree is less than, or equal to, the order of the finite group; this is called **Noether's bound**. #### Topology

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/26/Mug_and_Torus_morph.gif)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Mug_and_Torus_morph.gif)

A continuous deformation ([homotopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homotopy "Homotopy")) of a coffee cup into a doughnut ([torus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torus "Torus")) and back

As noted by [Hermann Weyl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann_Weyl "Hermann Weyl") in his obituary, Noether's contributions to [topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topology "Topology") illustrate her generosity with ideas and how her insights could transform entire fields of mathematics. In topology, mathematicians study the properties of objects that remain invariant even under deformation, properties such as their [connectedness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connected_space "Connected space"). An old joke is that "*a topologist cannot distinguish a donut from a coffee mug*", since they can be [continuously deformed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeomorphism "Homeomorphism") into one another.

Noether is credited with fundamental ideas that led to the development of [algebraic topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_topology "Algebraic topology") from the earlier [combinatorial topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combinatorial_topology "Combinatorial topology"), specifically, the idea of [homology groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homology_theory#Towards_algebraic_topology "Homology theory"). According to Alexandrov, Noether attended lectures given by him and [Heinz Hopf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heinz_Hopf "Heinz Hopf") in 1926 and 1927, where "she continually made observations which were often deep and subtle" and he continues that,

> When ... she first became acquainted with a systematic construction of combinatorial topology, she immediately observed that it would be worthwhile to study directly the [groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_(mathematics) "Group (mathematics)") of algebraic complexes and cycles of a given polyhedron and the [subgroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subgroup "Subgroup") of the cycle group consisting of cycles homologous to zero; instead of the usual definition of [Betti numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Betti_number "Betti number"), she suggested immediately defining the Betti group as the [complementary (quotient) group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quotient_group "Quotient group") of the group of all cycles by the subgroup of cycles homologous to zero. This observation now seems self-evident, but in those years (1925–1928) this was a completely new point of view.

Noether's suggestion that topology be studied algebraically was adopted immediately by Hopf, Alexandrov, and others, and it became a frequent topic of discussion among the mathematicians of Göttingen. Noether observed that her idea of a [Betti group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Betti_group "Betti group") makes the [Euler–Poincaré formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler_characteristic "Euler characteristic") simpler to understand, and Hopf's own work on this subject "bears the imprint of these remarks of Emmy Noether". Noether mentions her own topology ideas only as an aside in a 1926 publication, where she cites it as an application of [group theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_theory "Group theory").

This algebraic approach to topology was also developed independently in [Austria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austria "Austria"). In a 1926–1927 course given in [Vienna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vienna "Vienna"), [Leopold Vietoris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leopold_Vietoris "Leopold Vietoris") defined a [homology group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homology_group "Homology group"), which was developed by [Walther Mayer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walther_Mayer "Walther Mayer") into an axiomatic definition in 1928.

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/dd/Helmut_Hasse.jpg/250px-Helmut_Hasse.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Helmut_Hasse.jpg)

[Helmut Hasse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helmut_Hasse "Helmut Hasse") worked with Noether and others to found the theory of [central simple algebras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_simple_algebra "Central simple algebra").

### Third epoch (1927–1935)

#### Hypercomplex numbers and representation theory

Much work on [hypercomplex numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypercomplex_number "Hypercomplex number") and [group representations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_representation "Group representation") was carried out in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, but remained disparate. Noether united these earlier results and gave the first general representation theory of groups and algebras. This single work by Noether was said to have ushered in a new period in modern algebra and to have been of fundamental importance for its development.

Briefly, Noether subsumed the structure theory of [associative algebras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associative_algebra "Associative algebra") and the representation theory of groups into a single arithmetic theory of [modules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Module_(mathematics) "Module (mathematics)") and [ideals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ideal_(ring_theory) "Ideal (ring theory)") in [rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_(mathematics) "Ring (mathematics)") satisfying [ascending chain conditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ascending_chain_condition "Ascending chain condition").

#### Noncommutative algebra

Noether also was responsible for a number of other advances in the field of algebra. With [Emil Artin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emil_Artin "Emil Artin"), [Richard Brauer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Brauer "Richard Brauer"), and [Helmut Hasse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helmut_Hasse "Helmut Hasse"), she founded the theory of [central simple algebras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_simple_algebra "Central simple algebra").

A paper by Noether, Hasse, and Brauer pertains to [division algebras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Division_algebra "Division algebra"), which are algebraic systems in which division is possible. They proved two important theorems: a [local-global theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hasse_principle "Hasse principle") stating that if a finite-dimensional central division algebra over a [number field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_field "Number field") splits locally everywhere then it splits globally (so is trivial), and from this, deduced their *Hauptsatz* ("main theorem"):

> Every finite-dimensional [central](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_simple_algebra "Central simple algebra") [division algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Division_algebra "Division algebra") over an [algebraic number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_number "Algebraic number") [field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_(mathematics) "Field (mathematics)") F splits over a [cyclic cyclotomic extension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abelian_extension "Abelian extension").

These theorems allow one to classify all finite-dimensional central division algebras over a given number field. A subsequent paper by Noether showed, as a special case of a more general theorem, that all maximal subfields of a division algebra *D* are [splitting fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_simple_algebra#Splitting_field "Central simple algebra"). This paper also contains the [Skolem–Noether theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skolem–Noether_theorem "Skolem–Noether theorem"), which states that any two embeddings of an extension of a field *k* into a finite-dimensional central simple algebra over *k* are conjugate. The [Brauer–Noether theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brauer–Noether_theorem "Brauer–Noether theorem") gives a characterization of the splitting fields of a central division algebra over a field.

## Legacy

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/16/Emmy-noether-campus_siegen.jpg/250px-Emmy-noether-campus_siegen.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Emmy-noether-campus_siegen.jpg)

The Emmy–Noether–Campus at the [University of Siegen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Siegen "University of Siegen") is home to its mathematics and physics departments.

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e6/Ruhmeshalle_Muenchen_Emmy_Noether_Mathematikerin-1_retusche.jpg/250px-Ruhmeshalle_Muenchen_Emmy_Noether_Mathematikerin-1_retusche.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Ruhmeshalle_Muenchen_Emmy_Noether_Mathematikerin-1_retusche.jpg)

Noether is one of the carved stone busts displayed in Germany's [Ruhmeshalle München](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruhmeshalle_München "Ruhmeshalle München") (or Munich Hall of Fame in English).

Noether's work continues to be relevant for the development of theoretical physics and mathematics, and she is considered one of the most important mathematicians of the twentieth century. During her lifetime and even until today, Noether has also been characterized as the greatest woman mathematician in recorded history by mathematicians such as [Pavel Alexandrov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pavel_Alexandrov "Pavel Alexandrov"), [Hermann Weyl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann_Weyl "Hermann Weyl"), and [Jean Dieudonné](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Dieudonné "Jean Dieudonné").

In a letter to *[The New York Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_York_Times "The New York Times")*, [Albert Einstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Einstein "Albert Einstein") wrote:

> In the judgment of the most competent living mathematicians, Fräulein Noether was the most significant creative mathematical [genius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genius "Genius") thus far produced since the higher education of women began. In the realm of algebra, in which the most gifted mathematicians have been busy for centuries, she discovered methods which have proved of enormous importance in the development of the present-day younger generation of mathematicians.

In his obituary, fellow algebraist [B. L. van der Waerden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B._L._van_der_Waerden "B. L. van der Waerden") says that her mathematical originality was "absolute beyond comparison", and Hermann Weyl said that Noether "changed the face of [abstract] algebra by her work". Mathematician and historian [Jeremy Gray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeremy_Gray_(mathematician) "Jeremy Gray (mathematician)") wrote that any textbook on abstract algebra bears the evidence of Noether's contributions: "Mathematicians simply do ring theory her way." Several things [now bear her name](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_things_named_after_Emmy_Noether "List of things named after Emmy Noether"), including many mathematical objects, and an asteroid, 7001 Noether. In 2019 *[Time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_(magazine) "Time (magazine)")* created 89 new covers to celebrate women of the year starting from 1920; it chose Noether for 1921.
