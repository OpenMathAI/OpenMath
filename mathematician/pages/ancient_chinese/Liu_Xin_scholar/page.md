**Liu Xin** (c. 46 BCE – 23 CE), [courtesy name](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Courtesy_name "Courtesy name") **Zijun**, was a Chinese astronomer, classicist, imperial librarian, mathematician, and politician during the [Western Han](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Han_dynasty#Western_Han "Han dynasty") and [Xin dynasties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xin_dynasty "Xin dynasty"). He later changed his name to **Liu Xiu** (劉秀) due to the [naming taboo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naming_taboo "Naming taboo") of [Emperor Ai of Han](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emperor_Ai_of_Han "Emperor Ai of Han"). He was the son of Imperial librarian [Liu Xiang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liu_Xiang_(scholar) "Liu Xiang (scholar)") and an associate of other eminent thinkers such as the philosopher [Huan Tan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huan_Tan "Huan Tan"). Liu was a prominent supporter of the [Old Text](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Text_School "Old Text School") classics.

## Early life

Liu Xin was the son of Confucian scholar [Liu Xiang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liu_Xiang_(scholar) "Liu Xiang (scholar)") (77–6 BCE). Liu was a distant relative of [Liu Bang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liu_Bang "Liu Bang"), the founder of the Han dynasty, and was thus a member of the ruling dynastic clan (the [Liu family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liu_(surname) "Liu (surname)")). Liu Xin's paternal grandfather ranked as a *hou* (侯, roughly '[marquess](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ranged_Marquis#Han_dynasty "Ranged Marquis")'). As a young man, Liu helped his father in cataloguing the contents of the imperial library, and his friendship with the well-connected minister Wang Mang brought him power and rewards, rising under [Emperor Ai of Han](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emperor_Ai_of_Han "Emperor Ai of Han") to the rank of Palace Attendant and Chief Commandant of Imperial Equipages (侍中奉車都尉).

## Literary work

### Librarian

As a curator of the imperial library he was the first to establish a [library classification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Library_classification "Library classification") system and the first book notation system. At this time the [library catalog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Library_catalog "Library catalog") was written on scrolls of fine silk and stored in silk bags. Liu Xin's *Qilüe* (七略; "Seven Surveys") has not survived, but it formed the basis for the later bibliographic treatise *Yiwenzhi* (藝文志; "Treatise on Arts and Letters") in the *[Book of Han](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Han "Book of Han")*, which acted as a model for later imperial bibliographies.

As the imperial librarian, Liu Xin both catalogued and annotated or edited ancient texts. These projects of his produced what became definitive texts of a number of orthodox canons of Chinese philosophy and history.

Liu Xin played an important role in the transmission of the *Zuozhuan*. A scholar of the [Old Text School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Text_School "Old Text School"), he was attracted to the *Zuozhuan*'s earlier graphical forms, whose inaccessibility deterred the compound exegesis found in the rival *[Chunqiu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spring_and_Autumn_Annals "Spring and Autumn Annals")* commentarial traditions. In editing the *Zuozhuan* with the assistance of Yin Xian (尹咸), Liu rearranged the material into chronological order to map more neatly onto the *Chunqiu* chronicle as the *[Gongyang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gongyang "Gongyang")* and *[Guliang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guliang_zhuan "Guliang zhuan")* commentaries did. He aimed to have an imperial academician assigned to the work, a crucial bureaucratic step towards canonization in the official orthodoxy.

Liu's advocacy for the *Zuozhuan* was controversial in his own day, partly due to its lineage and prior reputation, and partly due to his own approach towards the situation. This scholarly dispute is sometimes taken as evidence for a larger dispute about classic texts written using variant scripts.

### Old Text proponent

During Liu Xin's career, there may have been some debate about certain texts called *guwen* (古文, "[Ancient Script Texts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Script_Texts "Ancient Script Texts")"). One set of manuscripts discovered by [Kong Anguo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kong_Anguo "Kong Anguo") in the [Western Han](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Han "Western Han") was consistently labeled as *guwen* owing to the graphical forms it preserved, a script which had diverged during the [Eastern Zhou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Zhou "Eastern Zhou") from the more conservative script of the state of [Qin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qin_(state) "Qin (state)") which became the official and only standard following their unification in 221 BCE. As a consequence of the variant graphical forms, parts of the text were already difficult to decipher for Han scholars.

It is not clear to what extent the content of *guwen* works differed from the transmitted versions of the same titles, nor what criteria allowed for labeling a document *guwen*. It may have been the case that even a handful of words would suffice to impart this characteristic upon the text that contained them.

Liu Xin was attracted to *guwen* texts, and his position in the imperial library meant he was well placed to ensure that these versions would officially be considered the authoritative ones.

### Integrity of transmitted literature

From the 19th through early 20th centuries, antiquarians and historians, beginning with [Kang Youwei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kang_Youwei "Kang Youwei"), accused Liu of excessive editing, to the point of falsifying historical texts. These criticisms were systematically analysed by the [Doubting Antiquity School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doubting_Antiquity_School "Doubting Antiquity School") of historians. According to their theory, first articulated by [Qian Mu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qian_Mu "Qian Mu") in 1930, Liu edited ancient texts for political purposes, particularly the *[Rites of Zhou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rites_of_Zhou "Rites of Zhou")*, the *[Zuozhuan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zuozhuan "Zuozhuan")*, and the Mao commentary to the *[Shijing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shijing "Shijing")*. This accusation of forgery had legitimate precedent: in the late 17th century [Yan Ruoqu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yan_Ruoqu "Yan Ruoqu") demonstrated that the transmitted *[Classic of History](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classic_of_History "Classic of History")* was mostly a forgery dating to the 4th century. This text had been based on the Ancient Script version, and only the parts that were present in the separately transmitted New Script version could be considered authentically early.

Liu Xin was a political ally of the powerful and divisive minister [Wang Mang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wang_Mang "Wang Mang"), who would go on to usurp the Han dynasty around the turn of the millennium for a brief period known as the [Xin dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xin_dynasty "Xin dynasty"). As the imperial librarian, Liu had the power to establish the definitive redactions of ancient texts and expunge variant versions. According to his accusers, the librarian falsified accounts of ancient historical events, and inserted into the legendary lineage of ancient rulers figures or relationships that were either invented, or borrowed from separate legends. In this way, he created a narrative of ancient rulers and successive dynasties which satisfied the "[five phases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wuxing_(Chinese_philosophy) "Wuxing (Chinese philosophy)")" theory, wherein each ruler and/or dynasty represented one of the five traditional Chinese elements ([*wuxing*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wuxing_(Chinese_philosophy) "Wuxing (Chinese philosophy)")), between which the [Mandate of Heaven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandate_of_Heaven "Mandate of Heaven") rotated. An account thus falsified would satisfactorily explain the rule of the Han and Xin dynasties in terms of the phases they were said to represent, and according to the forgery theory, Liu Xin's edited account conveniently showed a series of successions between various claimed ancestors of the Han and Xin houses.

The possibility of Liu Xin's forgeries became a crucial question for the Doubting Antiquity School in their search for a [rationalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rationalism "Rationalism") past for China. They drew evidence from discrepancies between the texts edited by Liu and earlier or contemporaneous texts. For example, figures or events appearing in Liu's edited versions did not appear in earlier or contemporaneous texts. The forgery theory has been largely discredited.

## Scientific work

Wang Mang's *[jialiang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jialiang "Jialiang")*, used by Liu Xin in scientific measurements, held in the [National Palace Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Palace_Museum "National Palace Museum"), [Taipei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taipei "Taipei")

### Calculation of pi (π)

For centuries before the usurpation of [Wang Mang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wang_Mang "Wang Mang") (r.  9–23), the Chinese had used the value of 3 for their calculation of [pi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pi "Pi"), the ratio of a circle's circumference to its diameter. Some time between the years 1 and 5, Liu Xin was the first Chinese researcher to give a geometrical figure which implies the improved approximation π ≈ 3.1547, although the exact method he used to reach this figure is unknown. The original *[jialiang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jialiang "Jialiang") hu* [standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_(metrology) "Standard (metrology)") Liu Xin designed and used in his measurements is still extant. [Sinologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinologist "Sinologist") [Joseph Needham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Needham "Joseph Needham") inspected it in Beijing, describing it as follows:

> The standardised *chia liang hu* (has) a square with each side 1 *chhih* (foot) long, and outside it a circle. The distance from each corner of the square to the circle (*thiao phang*) is 9 *li* 5 *hao*. The area of the circle (*mu*) is 162 (square) *tshun* (inches), the depth 1 *chhih* (foot), and the volume (of the whole) 1620 (cubic) *tshun* (inches).

Later early Chinese mathematicians such as [Zhang Heng](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zhang_Heng "Zhang Heng") (78–139) and [Liu Hui](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liu_Hui "Liu Hui") (fl. 3rd century) would improve Liu's calculation for pi, and were improved upon in turn by [Zu Chongzhi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zu_Chongzhi "Zu Chongzhi") (429–500).

### Standards and measures

As Emperor of Xin, Wang Mang attempted to return to the lost ways of the ancient sage-kings, a legendary golden age of order and peace. Assisting him in some of the practicalities of this ambition was Liu Xin. Upon ascending the throne, Wang Mang named Liu Xin his *Guoshi* (國師, "Professor Laureate"), a newly created office which was one of the Four Viziers (四輔, named after a legend about [a constellation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_Advisors "Four Advisors")), the most powerful and exalted ministers in the empire. As part of the rebranding schema implemented across the government, Liu Xin gained the noble title "Eminence of New Excellence" (嘉新公). He assisted in standardising the measures of liquid volume and the harmonic frequencies of musical instruments. The *jialiang hu* used to calculate pi was designed as the [standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_(metrology) "Standard (metrology)") for the five measures of liquid volume stipulated by statute, and was constructed with a separate compartment for each of them.

In the course of his scientific work, Liu wrote a treatise which survives in the *[Book of Han](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Han "Book of Han")*: *Lüli zhi* (律曆志, "Treatise on Standards and Calendrics"). This text records detailed information about Han dynasty mathematics, measures of length and volume, harmonics, weights and balances, and the calendar via *[wuxing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wuxing_(Chinese_philosophy) "Wuxing (Chinese philosophy)")* theory.

### Astronomy and natural philosophy

As a classicist, Liu Xin was able to support the legitimation of his patron Wang Mang as a restorer of an ancient mode of governance, and as a proponent and theorist of the "[generative cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wuxing_(Chinese_philosophy)#Cycles "Wuxing (Chinese philosophy)")" of the popular "five phases" theory, Liu advised the new emperor on ritual matters to better accord with these fundamental essences. Liu additionally developed a new more accurate model of astronomy, the Triple Concordance calendar (三統歷; *San tong li*), for predicting the motion of heavenly bodies. In the second half of the 20th century, a [crater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liu_Hsin_(crater) "Liu Hsin (crater)") on [Mars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mars "Mars") was named in his honor.

## Death

Although Liu Xin was originally a loyal partisan of Wang Mang, after Wang's troops suffered defeat on July 7, 23 at the [Battle of Kunyang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Kunyang "Battle of Kunyang"), Liu Xin plotted with others to overthrow Wang Mang. The plot was discovered, and all the conspirators committed suicide or were executed.
