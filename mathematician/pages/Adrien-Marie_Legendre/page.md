**Adrien-Marie Legendre** ([/ləˈʒɑːndər, -ˈʒɑːnd/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English"); French: [[adʁiɛ̃ maʁi ləʒɑ̃dʁ]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/French "Help:IPA/French"); 18 September 1752 – 9 January 1833) was a French [mathematician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematician "Mathematician") who made numerous contributions to [mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics "Mathematics"). Well-known and important concepts such as the [Legendre polynomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legendre_polynomials "Legendre polynomials") and [Legendre transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legendre_transformation "Legendre transformation") are named after him. He is also known for his contributions to the [method of least squares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Least_squares "Least squares"), and was the first to officially publish on it, though [Carl Friedrich Gauss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Friedrich_Gauss "Carl Friedrich Gauss") had discovered it before him.

## Life

Adrien-Marie Legendre was born in [Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paris "Paris") on 18 September 1752 to a wealthy family. He received his education at the [Collège Mazarin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collège_Mazarin "Collège Mazarin") in Paris, and defended his thesis in physics and mathematics in 1770. He taught at the [École Militaire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/École_Militaire "École Militaire") in Paris from 1775 to 1780 and at the [École Normale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/École_Normale_Supérieure "École Normale Supérieure") from 1795. At the same time, he was associated with the [Bureau des Longitudes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bureau_des_Longitudes "Bureau des Longitudes"). In 1782, the [Berlin Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prussian_Academy_of_Sciences "Prussian Academy of Sciences") awarded Legendre a prize for his treatise on projectiles in resistant media. This treatise also brought him to the attention of [Lagrange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagrange "Lagrange").

The *[Académie des sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Académie_des_sciences "Académie des sciences")* made Legendre an adjoint member in 1783 and an associate in 1785. In 1789, he was elected a [Fellow of the Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow_of_the_Royal_Society "Fellow of the Royal Society").

He assisted with the [Anglo-French Survey (1784–1790)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-French_Survey_(1784–1790) "Anglo-French Survey (1784–1790)") to calculate the precise distance between the [Paris Observatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paris_Observatory "Paris Observatory") and the [Royal Greenwich Observatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Greenwich_Observatory "Royal Greenwich Observatory") by means of [trigonometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigonometry "Trigonometry"). To this end in 1787 he visited Dover and London together with [Dominique, comte de Cassini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominique,_comte_de_Cassini "Dominique, comte de Cassini") and [Pierre Méchain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Méchain "Pierre Méchain"). The three also visited [William Herschel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Herschel "William Herschel"), the discoverer of the planet [Uranus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranus "Uranus").

Legendre lost his private fortune in 1793 during the [French Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution "French Revolution"). That year, he also married Marguerite-Claudine Couhin, who helped him put his affairs in order. In 1795, Legendre became one of six members of the mathematics section of the reconstituted Académie des Sciences, renamed the Institut National des Sciences et des Arts. Later, in 1803, Napoleon reorganized the Institut National, and Legendre became a member of the Geometry section. From 1799 to 1812, Legendre served as mathematics examiner for graduating artillery students at the École Militaire and from 1799 to 1815 he served as permanent mathematics examiner for the [École Polytechnique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/École_Polytechnique "École Polytechnique"). In 1824, Legendre's pension from the École Militaire was stopped because he refused to vote for the government candidate at the Institut National. In 1831, he was made an officer of the [Légion d'Honneur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Légion_d'Honneur "Légion d'Honneur").

Legendre died in Paris on 9 January 1833, after a long and painful illness, and Legendre's widow carefully preserved his belongings to memorialize him. Upon her death in 1856, she was buried next to her husband in the village of [Auteuil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auteuil,_Paris "Auteuil, Paris"), where the couple had lived, and left their last country house to the village. Legendre's name is one of the [72 names inscribed on the Eiffel Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_the_72_names_on_the_Eiffel_Tower "List of the 72 names on the Eiffel Tower").

## Mathematical work

[Abel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niels_Abel "Niels Abel")'s work on [elliptic functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptic_function "Elliptic function") was built on Legendre's, and some of [Gauss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Friedrich_Gauss "Carl Friedrich Gauss")'s work in statistics and [number theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_theory "Number theory") completed that of Legendre. He developed, and first communicated to his contemporaries before Gauss, the [least squares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Least_squares "Least squares") method which has broad application in [linear regression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_regression "Linear regression"), [signal processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signal_processing "Signal processing"), statistics, and [curve fitting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curve_fitting "Curve fitting"); this was published in 1806 as an appendix to his book on the paths of comets. Today, the term "least squares method" is used as a direct translation from the French "méthode des moindres carrés".

His major work is *Exercices de Calcul Intégral*, published in three volumes in 1811, 1817 and 1819. In the first volume he introduced the basic properties of elliptic integrals, [beta functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beta_function "Beta function") and [gamma functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamma_function "Gamma function"), introducing the symbol Γ and normalizing it to Γ(n+1) = n!. Further results on the beta and gamma functions along with their applications to mechanics – such as the rotation of the earth, and the attraction of ellipsoids – appeared in the second volume. In 1830, he gave a proof of [Fermat's Last Theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermat's_Last_Theorem "Fermat's Last Theorem") for exponent *n* = 5, which was also proven by [Lejeune Dirichlet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Gustav_Lejeune_Dirichlet "Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet") in 1828.

In [number theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_theory "Number theory"), he conjectured the [quadratic reciprocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_reciprocity "Quadratic reciprocity") law, subsequently proved by Gauss; in connection to this, the [Legendre symbol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legendre_symbol "Legendre symbol") is named after him. He also did pioneering work on the distribution of [primes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_number "Prime number"), and on the application of analysis to number theory. His 1798 conjecture of the [prime number theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_number_theorem "Prime number theorem") was rigorously proved by [Hadamard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques_Hadamard "Jacques Hadamard") and [de la Vallée-Poussin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Jean_de_la_Vallée-Poussin "Charles Jean de la Vallée-Poussin") in 1896.

Legendre did an impressive amount of work on [elliptic functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptic_function "Elliptic function"), including the classification of [elliptic integrals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptic_integral "Elliptic integral"), but it took [Abel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niels_Henrik_Abel "Niels Henrik Abel")'s study of the inverses of [Jacobi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Gustav_Jakob_Jacobi "Carl Gustav Jakob Jacobi")'s [functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobi's_elliptic_functions "Jacobi's elliptic functions") to solve the problem completely.

He is known for the [Legendre transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legendre_transformation "Legendre transformation"), which is used to go from the [Lagrangian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagrangian_mechanics "Lagrangian mechanics") to the [Hamiltonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamiltonian_mechanics "Hamiltonian mechanics") formulation of [classical mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_mechanics "Classical mechanics"). In [thermodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermodynamics "Thermodynamics") it is also used to obtain the [enthalpy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enthalpy "Enthalpy") and the [Helmholtz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helmholtz_free_energy "Helmholtz free energy") and [Gibbs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gibbs_free_energy "Gibbs free energy") [(free) energies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermodynamic_free_energy "Thermodynamic free energy") from the [internal energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internal_energy "Internal energy"). He is also the namesake of the [Legendre polynomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legendre_polynomials "Legendre polynomials"), solutions to Legendre's differential equation, which occur frequently in physics and engineering applications, such as [electrostatics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrostatics "Electrostatics").

Legendre is best known as the author of *Éléments de géométrie*, which was published in 1794 and was the leading elementary text on the topic for around 100 years. This text greatly rearranged and simplified many of the propositions from [Euclid's *Elements*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclid's_Elements "Euclid's Elements") to create a more effective textbook.

## Mistaken portrait

For two centuries, until the discovery of the error in 2005, books, paintings and articles had incorrectly shown a profile portrait of the obscure French politician [Louis Legendre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Legendre "Louis Legendre") (1752–1797) as a portrait of the mathematician. The error arose from the fact that the sketch was labelled simply "Legendre" and appeared in a book along with contemporary mathematicians such as Lagrange. One of only two known portraits of Legendre, rediscovered in 2008, is found in the 1820 book *Album de 73 portraits-charge aquarellés des membres de I'Institut*, a book of caricatures of seventy-three members of the Institut de France in Paris by the French artist [Julien-Léopold Boilly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julien-Léopold_Boilly "Julien-Léopold Boilly") as shown below. The other portrait is from the book *Le Panthéon scientifique de la tour Eiffel*.
