**Andrey Andreyevich Markov** (14 June [[O.S.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Style_and_New_Style_dates "Old Style and New Style dates") 2 June] 1856 – 20 July 1922) was a Russian mathematician celebrated for his pioneering work in [stochastic processes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stochastic_processes "Stochastic processes"). He extended foundational results—such as the [law of large numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_large_numbers "Law of large numbers") and the [central limit theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_limit_theorem "Central limit theorem")—to sequences of dependent random variables, laying the groundwork for what would become known as [Markov chains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Markov_chains "Markov chains"). To illustrate his methods, he analyzed the distribution of vowels and consonants in [Alexander Pushkin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Pushkin "Alexander Pushkin")'s *[Eugene Onegin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugene_Onegin "Eugene Onegin")*, treating letters purely as abstract categories and stripping away any poetic or semantic content.

He was also a strong chess player.

Markov and his younger brother [Vladimir Andreyevich Markov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladimir_Markov_(mathematician) "Vladimir Markov (mathematician)") (1871–1897) proved the [Markov brothers' inequality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Markov_brothers'_inequality "Markov brothers' inequality"). His son, another [Andrey Andreyevich Markov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrey_Markov_Jr. "Andrey Markov Jr.") (1903–1979), was also a notable mathematician, making contributions to [constructive mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constructive_mathematics "Constructive mathematics") and [recursive function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recursion#Functional_recursion "Recursion") theory.

## Biography

Andrey Markov was born on 14 June 1856 in [Ryazan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ryazan "Ryazan"), Russia. He attended the St. Petersburg Grammar School, where some teachers saw him as a rebellious student. In his academics he performed poorly in most subjects other than mathematics. Later in life he attended [Saint Petersburg Imperial University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Petersburg_Imperial_University "Saint Petersburg Imperial University"). Among his teachers were [Yulian Sokhotski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yulian_Sokhotski "Yulian Sokhotski") (differential calculus, higher algebra), [Konstantin Posse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Konstantin_Posse "Konstantin Posse") (analytic geometry), Yegor Zolotarev (integral calculus), [Pafnuty Chebyshev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pafnuty_Chebyshev "Pafnuty Chebyshev") ([number theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_theory "Number theory") and probability theory), [Aleksandr Korkin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aleksandr_Korkin "Aleksandr Korkin") (ordinary and partial differential equations), Mikhail Okatov (mechanism theory), [Osip Somov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osip_Somov "Osip Somov") (mechanics), and Nikolai Budajev (descriptive and higher geometry).

He completed his studies at the university and was later asked if he would like to stay and have a career as a mathematician. He later taught at high schools and continued his own mathematical studies. In this time he found a practical use for his mathematical skills.

In 1913, he conducted a study on the distribution of vowels and consonants in the first 20,000 letters of [Alexander Pushkin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Pushkin "Alexander Pushkin")'s *[Eugene Onegin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugene_Onegin "Eugene Onegin")*, treating the text as a sequence of symbols and analyzing the statistical relationships between them. By classifying each letter as either a vowel or a consonant and analyzing the probabilities of transitions between these categories, Markov demonstrated that chains of dependent events could be rigorously modeled. This was the first empirical application of what are now called [Markov chains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Markov_chains "Markov chains").

He died at age 66 on 20 July 1922.

During the following year, he passed the candidate's examinations, and he remained at the university to prepare for a lecturer's position.

In April 1880, Markov defended his [master's thesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master's_degree "Master's degree") "On the Binary Square Forms with Positive Determinant", which was directed by Aleksandr Korkin and Yegor Zolotarev. Four years later in 1884, he defended his doctoral thesis titled "On Certain Applications of the Algebraic Continuous Fractions".

His [pedagogical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pedagogy "Pedagogy") work began after the defense of his master's thesis in autumn 1880. As a [privatdozent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Privatdozent "Privatdozent") he lectured on differential and integral calculus. Later he lectured alternately on "introduction to analysis", probability theory (succeeding Chebyshev, who had left the university in 1882) and the calculus of differences. From 1895 through 1905 he also lectured in [differential calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_calculus "Differential calculus").

One year after the defense of his doctoral thesis, Markov was appointed extraordinary professor (1886) and in the same year he was elected adjunct to the Academy of Sciences. In 1890, after the death of [Viktor Bunyakovsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viktor_Bunyakovsky "Viktor Bunyakovsky"), Markov became an extraordinary member of the academy. His promotion to an ordinary professor of St. Petersburg University followed in the fall of 1894.

In 1896, Markov was elected an ordinary member of the academy as the successor of [Chebyshev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chebyshev "Chebyshev"). In 1905, he was appointed merited professor and was granted the right to retire, which he did immediately. Until 1910, however, he continued to lecture in the calculus of differences.

In connection with student riots in 1908, professors and lecturers of St. Petersburg University were ordered to monitor their students. Markov refused to accept this decree, and he wrote an explanation in which he declined to be an "agent of the governance". Markov was removed from further teaching duties at St. Petersburg University, and hence he decided to retire from the university.

Markov was an [atheist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atheist "Atheist"). In 1912, he responded to [Leo Tolstoy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leo_Tolstoy "Leo Tolstoy")'s [excommunication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Excommunication "Excommunication") from the [Russian Orthodox Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Orthodox_Church "Russian Orthodox Church") by requesting his own excommunication. The Church complied with his request.

Markov's [headstone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Headstone "Headstone")

In 1913, the council of St. Petersburg elected nine scientists honorary members of the university. Markov was among them, but his election was not affirmed by the minister of education. The affirmation only occurred four years later, after the [February Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/February_Revolution "February Revolution") in 1917. Markov then resumed his teaching activities and lectured on probability theory and the calculus of differences until his death in 1922.
