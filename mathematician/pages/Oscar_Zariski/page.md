**Oscar Zariski** (April 24, 1899 – July 4, 1986) was an American [mathematician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematician "Mathematician"). The Russian-born scientist was one of the most influential [algebraic geometers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_geometry "Algebraic geometry") of the 20th century.

## Education

Zariski was born Osher (also transliterated as Ascher or Oscher) Zaritsky ([Russian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_language "Russian language"): Ошер Зарицкий) to a Jewish family of Bezalel Zaritsky and Hanna [née](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_name#Maiden_and_married_names "Birth name") Tennenbaum in [Kobrin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kobrin "Kobrin"), Russian Empire and in 1918 studied at the [University of Kiev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Kiev "University of Kiev"). He left Kiev in 1920 to study at the [University of Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sapienza_University_of_Rome "Sapienza University of Rome") where he became a disciple of the [Italian school of algebraic geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_school_of_algebraic_geometry "Italian school of algebraic geometry"), studying with [Guido Castelnuovo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guido_Castelnuovo "Guido Castelnuovo"), [Federigo Enriques](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federigo_Enriques "Federigo Enriques") and [Francesco Severi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francesco_Severi "Francesco Severi").

Zariski wrote a doctoral dissertation in 1924 on a topic in [Galois theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galois_theory "Galois theory"), which was proposed to him by [Castelnuovo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guido_Castelnuovo "Guido Castelnuovo"). At the time of his dissertation publication, he changed his name to Oscar Zariski.

## Johns Hopkins University years

Zariski emigrated to the [United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States "United States") in 1927 supported by [Solomon Lefschetz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solomon_Lefschetz "Solomon Lefschetz"). He had a position at [Johns Hopkins University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johns_Hopkins_University "Johns Hopkins University") where he became professor in 1937. During this period, he wrote *Algebraic Surfaces* as a summation of the work of the Italian school.
The book was published in 1935 and reissued 36 years later, with detailed notes by Zariski's students that illustrated how the field of algebraic geometry had changed. It is still an important reference.

It seems to have been this work that set the seal of Zariski's discontent with the approach of the Italians to [birational geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birational_geometry "Birational geometry"). He addressed the question of rigour by recourse to [commutative algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative_algebra "Commutative algebra"). The [Zariski topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zariski_topology "Zariski topology"), as it was later known, is adequate for *biregular geometry*, where varieties are mapped by polynomial functions. That theory is too limited for algebraic surfaces, and even for curves with singular points. A rational map is to a regular map as a [rational function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_function "Rational function") is to a polynomial: it may be indeterminate at some points. In geometric terms, one has to work with functions defined on some open, [dense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dense_set "Dense set") set of a given variety. The description of the behaviour on the complement may require [infinitely near points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinitely_near_points "Infinitely near points") to be introduced to account for limiting behaviour *along different directions*. This introduces a need, in the surface case, to use also [valuation theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valuation_(algebra) "Valuation (algebra)") to describe the phenomena such as [blowing up](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blowing_up "Blowing up") (balloon-style, rather than explosively).

## Harvard University years

After spending a year 1946–1947 at the [University of Illinois at Urbana–Champaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Illinois_at_Urbana–Champaign "University of Illinois at Urbana–Champaign"), Zariski became professor at [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University "Harvard University") in 1947 where he remained until his retirement in 1969. In 1945, he fruitfully discussed foundational matters for algebraic geometry with [André Weil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/André_Weil "André Weil"). Weil's interest was in putting an abstract variety theory in place, to support the use of the [Jacobian variety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobian_variety "Jacobian variety") in his proof of the [Riemann hypothesis for curves over finite fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann_hypothesis_for_curves_over_finite_fields "Riemann hypothesis for curves over finite fields"), a direction rather oblique to Zariski's interests. The two sets of foundations weren't reconciled at that point.

At Harvard, Zariski's students included [Shreeram Abhyankar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shreeram_Abhyankar "Shreeram Abhyankar"), [Heisuke Hironaka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heisuke_Hironaka "Heisuke Hironaka"), [David Mumford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Mumford "David Mumford"), [Michael Artin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Artin "Michael Artin") and [Steven Kleiman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steven_Kleiman "Steven Kleiman")—thus spanning the main areas of advance in [singularity theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singularity_theory "Singularity theory"), [moduli theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moduli_theory "Moduli theory") and [cohomology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cohomology "Cohomology") in the next generation. Zariski himself worked on equisingularity theory. Some of his major results, [Zariski's main theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zariski's_main_theorem "Zariski's main theorem") and the Zariski theorem on holomorphic functions, were amongst the results generalized and included in the programme of [Alexander Grothendieck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Grothendieck "Alexander Grothendieck") that ultimately unified algebraic geometry.

Zariski proposed the first example of a [Zariski surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zariski_surface "Zariski surface") in 1958.

He wrote *Commutative Algebra* in two volumes, with [Pierre Samuel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Samuel "Pierre Samuel").

## Views

Zariski was a [Jewish atheist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_atheist "Jewish atheist").

## Awards and recognition

Zariski was elected to the United States [National Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Academy_of_Sciences "National Academy of Sciences") in 1944, the [American Academy of Arts and Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Academy_of_Arts_and_Sciences "American Academy of Arts and Sciences") in 1948, and the [American Philosophical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Philosophical_Society "American Philosophical Society") in 1951.

He was offered [Guggenheim Fellowship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guggenheim_Fellowship "Guggenheim Fellowship") in 1939. U.S. [National Medal of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Medal_of_Science "National Medal of Science"): 1965.

Zariski was awarded [Cole Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cole_Prize "Cole Prize") in 1944, the [Steele Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steele_Prize "Steele Prize") in 1981, and the [Wolf Prize in Mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolf_Prize_in_Mathematics "Wolf Prize in Mathematics") with [Lars Ahlfors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lars_Ahlfors "Lars Ahlfors") in 1981.

During 1969–1970 he was President of the [American Mathematical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Mathematical_Society "American Mathematical Society").

His papers have been published by [MIT Press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MIT_Press "MIT Press"), in four volumes. In 1997 a conference was held in his honor in [Obergurgl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obergurgl "Obergurgl"), Austria.
