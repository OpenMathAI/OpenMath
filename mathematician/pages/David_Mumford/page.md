**David Bryant Mumford** (born 11 June 1937) is an American [mathematician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematician "Mathematician") known for his work in [algebraic geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_geometry "Algebraic geometry") and then for research into [vision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_vision "Computer vision") and [pattern theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pattern_theory "Pattern theory"). He won the [Fields Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fields_Medal "Fields Medal") and was a [MacArthur Fellow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MacArthur_Fellow "MacArthur Fellow"). In 2010 he was awarded the [National Medal of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Medal_of_Science "National Medal of Science"). He is currently a University Professor Emeritus in the Division of Applied Mathematics at [Brown University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brown_University "Brown University").

## Life

Mumford was born in [Worth, West Sussex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worth,_West_Sussex "Worth, West Sussex") in [England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/England "England"), of an English father and American mother. His father [William Bryant Mumford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Bryant_Mumford "William Bryant Mumford") was educated at [Manchester Grammar School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manchester_Grammar_School "Manchester Grammar School") and took the [Mathematical Tripos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_Tripos "Mathematical Tripos") at [St John's College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_John's_College,_Cambridge "St John's College, Cambridge"); as his mother Edith Emily Read had done at [Girton College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Girton_College "Girton College"). He started an experimental school in the colonial [Tanganyika Territory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tanganyika_Territory "Tanganyika Territory") and at the time of his death in 1951 worked in the [United Nations Department of Public Information](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_Department_of_Public_Information "United Nations Department of Public Information"). He married in 1933 Grace Schiott of [Southport, Connecticut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southport,_Connecticut "Southport, Connecticut"), and the couple had five children.

Mumford first went to [Unquowa School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unquowa_School "Unquowa School"). He attended [Phillips Exeter Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phillips_Exeter_Academy "Phillips Exeter Academy"), where he received a [Westinghouse Science Talent Search](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westinghouse_Science_Talent_Search "Westinghouse Science Talent Search") prize for his relay-based computer project. Mumford then went to [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University "Harvard University"), where he became a student of [Oscar Zariski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oscar_Zariski "Oscar Zariski"). At Harvard, he became a [Putnam Fellow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Putnam_Fellow "Putnam Fellow") in 1955 and 1956. He completed his [PhD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PhD "PhD") in 1961, with a thesis entitled *Existence of the moduli scheme for curves of any genus*.

After a career at Harvard, Mumford moved to [Brown University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brown_University "Brown University") in 1996, concentrating on [applied mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Applied_mathematics "Applied mathematics").

## Research

### Algebraic geometry

Mumford's work in geometry combined traditional geometric insights with contemporary algebraic techniques.

#### Moduli spaces

Mumford published on [moduli spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moduli_space "Moduli space"), with a theory summed up in his book *Geometric Invariant Theory*.

At the [International Congress of Mathematicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Congress_of_Mathematicians "International Congress of Mathematicians") in 1974, [John Tate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Tate_(mathematician) "John Tate (mathematician)") said:

> Mumford's major work has been a tremendously successful multi-pronged attack on problems of the existence and structure of varieties of moduli, that is, varieties whose points parametrize isomorphism classes of some type of geometric object.

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a8/ModularGroup-FundamentalDomain.svg/250px-ModularGroup-FundamentalDomain.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:ModularGroup-FundamentalDomain.svg)

Diagram of a [fundamental domain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_domain "Fundamental domain") (in grey) for the [modular group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modular_group "Modular group") acting on the [upper half-plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_half-plane "Upper half-plane"), the classical picture of moduli for curves of genus 1

The [moduli space of curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moduli_space_of_curves "Moduli space of curves") of a given [genus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genus_of_a_curve "Genus of a curve") *g* was already considered in [complex algebraic geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_algebraic_geometry "Complex algebraic geometry") during the 19th century. When *g* = 1 the number of moduli is 1 also, the term coming from the [elliptic modulus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptic_modulus "Elliptic modulus") function of the theory of [Jacobi elliptic functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobi_elliptic_functions "Jacobi elliptic functions"). [Bernhard Riemann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernhard_Riemann "Bernhard Riemann") contributed the formula, when *g* ≥ 2, for the number of moduli, namely 3*g*−3, a consequence, in [deformation theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deformation_theory "Deformation theory"), of the [Riemann-Roch theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann-Roch_theorem "Riemann-Roch theorem").

The theory of [abstract varieties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstract_varieties "Abstract varieties"), as Mumford showed, can be applied to provide an [existence theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Existence_theorem "Existence theorem") for moduli spaces of curves, over any [algebraically closed field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraically_closed_field "Algebraically closed field"). It therefore answers the question of in what sense there is a geometric object, with an algebraic definition, that parametrizes algebraic curves, so generalizing the [modular curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modular_curve "Modular curve"), having the predicted dimension. In a summary provided by [Jean Dieudonné](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Dieudonné "Jean Dieudonné"), making use of [scheme theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scheme_theory "Scheme theory"), the steps are:

1. Construction of a certain [closed subscheme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_subscheme "Closed subscheme") *H* of the [Hilbert scheme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilbert_scheme "Hilbert scheme") of the projective space of dimension *n* over the [ring of integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_of_integers "Ring of integers").
2. Construction of an "orbit scheme" *M* of the [projective linear group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_linear_group "Projective linear group") *PGLn* acting on *H*. This step relies on the more tractable theory of [moduli of abelian varieties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moduli_of_abelian_varieties "Moduli of abelian varieties").

In the introduction to his 1965 book *Geometric Invariant Theory*, Mumford described the construction of "moduli schemes of various types of objects" as essentially "a special and highly non-trivial case" of the problem "when does an [orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit_(group_theory) "Orbit (group theory)") space of an [algebraic scheme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_scheme "Algebraic scheme") acted on by an [algebraic group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_group "Algebraic group") exist?". For this area, see [algebraic invariant theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_invariant_theory "Algebraic invariant theory"). [David Gieseker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Gieseker "David Gieseker") later distinguished, for step #2, going via [Torelli's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torelli's_theorem "Torelli's theorem"), which was Mumford's original method, and the alternative approach via the [Chow point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chow_point "Chow point") of a curve *C*, in its projective [canonical embedding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canonical_bundle#General_case "Canonical bundle"), which proves the [stability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stability_(algebraic_geometry) "Stability (algebraic geometry)") of this point.

The concept of [stable vector bundle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stable_vector_bundle "Stable vector bundle") from moduli theory has been consequential in mathematical physics: "This is a mathematical notion of stability, but it also corresponds to physical stability, at least in a regime in which quantum corrections are small."

#### Moduli stacks

Before the mathematical concept of [stack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stack_(mathematics) "Stack (mathematics)") had been defined, Mumford gave a detailed treatment of the [moduli stack of elliptic curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moduli_stack_of_elliptic_curves "Moduli stack of elliptic curves"), in his paper *Picard Groups of Moduli Problems*. In it, he made a calculation of (the analogue of) the stack's [Picard group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Picard_group "Picard group"). Hartshorne wrote that, in the paper, Mumford

> [...] makes the point that to investigate the more subtle properties of curves, the [coarse moduli space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coarse_moduli_space "Coarse moduli space") may not carry enough information, and so one should really work with stacks.

### Classification of surfaces

In three papers written between 1969 and 1976 (the last two in collaboration with [Enrico Bombieri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enrico_Bombieri "Enrico Bombieri")), Mumford extended the [Enriques–Kodaira classification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enriques–Kodaira_classification "Enriques–Kodaira classification") of smooth [projective surfaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_surface "Projective surface") from the case of the complex [ground field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ground_field "Ground field") to the case of an [algebraically closed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraically_closed "Algebraically closed") ground field of characteristic *p*. The four classes are:

1. Kodaira dimension minus infinity. These are the [ruled surfaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruled_surface "Ruled surface").
2. Kodaira dimension 0. These are the [K3 surfaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K3_surface "K3 surface"), [abelian surfaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abelian_surface "Abelian surface"), hyperelliptic and [quasi-hyperelliptic surfaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasi-hyperelliptic_surface "Quasi-hyperelliptic surface"), and [Enriques surfaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enriques_surface "Enriques surface"). There are classical and non-classical examples in the last two Kodaira dimension zero cases.
3. Kodaira dimension 1. These are the elliptic and [quasi-elliptic surfaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasi-elliptic_surface "Quasi-elliptic surface") not contained in the last two groups.
4. Kodaira dimension 2. These are the [surfaces of general type](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surfaces_of_general_type "Surfaces of general type").

### Explicit equations and algebraic techniques

Mumford wrote on an algebraic approach to the classical theory of [theta functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theta_function "Theta function"), the [theta representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theta_representation "Theta representation"), showing that its algebraic content was large, with resulting finite analogues of the [Heisenberg group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heisenberg_group "Heisenberg group"). This work on the [equations defining abelian varieties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equations_defining_abelian_varieties "Equations defining abelian varieties") appeared in 1966–7. He also is one of the founders of the [toroidal embedding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toric_geometry "Toric geometry") theory, the geometric approach to varieties defined by [monomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monomial "Monomial"). With [Dave Bayer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dave_Bayer "Dave Bayer") he published a paper "What can be computed in algebraic geometry?" in 1993 on computation and algebraic geometry, in which [Gröbner basis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gröbner_basis "Gröbner basis") techniques are fundamental. The concept of [Castelnuovo–Mumford regularity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Castelnuovo–Mumford_regularity "Castelnuovo–Mumford regularity") has been employed as a complexity measure for Gröbner bases.

### Vision

In a paper published in 1989, Mumford and Jayant Shah showed that a numerical method could tackle [image segmentation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Image_segmentation "Image segmentation"), using what became known as the [Mumford–Shah functional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mumford–Shah_functional "Mumford–Shah functional"). In the *Handbook of Mathematical Methods in Imaging* (2011) the functional was called "classical", and the method described as an [energy minimization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_minimization "Energy minimization") approach allowing optimal computation for [piecewise function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piecewise_function "Piecewise function") solutions. Another assessment in 2024 was "a foundational approach for partitioning an image into meaningful regions while preserving edges."

Following work of [Shimon Ullman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shimon_Ullman "Shimon Ullman") who brought in variational methods, and [Berthold K.P. Horn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berthold_K.P._Horn "Berthold K.P. Horn"), Mumford in 1992 addressed the [amodal completion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amodal_completion "Amodal completion") problem with concepts from [elastica theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elastica_theory "Elastica theory"). In the 1990s, [Song-Chun Zhu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Song-Chun_Zhu "Song-Chun Zhu") at the [University of Science and Technology of China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Science_and_Technology_of_China "University of Science and Technology of China"), attracted into the field by the book *Vision* (1982) by [David Marr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Marr_(scientist) "David Marr (scientist)"), studied computer science under Mumford.

In 2002, Mumford and Tai Sing Lee proposed a model for the [visual cortex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visual_cortex "Visual cortex"), influenced by work on [pattern theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pattern_theory "Pattern theory") by [Ulf Grenander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulf_Grenander "Ulf Grenander"). The book chapter "The Evolving Concept of Cortical Hierarchy" from 2023 by Vezoli, Hou and Kennedy states that Mumford's "[Bayesian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayesian "Bayesian") computational approach to cortical hierarchy was revolutionary". The 2023 book by Zhu and Wu, *Computer Vision: Statistical Models for Marr's Paradigm*, in its Preface lists as "pioneers of vision" [Béla Julesz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Béla_Julesz "Béla Julesz") and [King-Sun Fu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King-Sun_Fu "King-Sun Fu"), with Grenander, Maar and Mumford.

## Promulgation of the ideas of Alexander Grothendieck

In 1962 Mumford was appointed an assistant professor at Harvard. He decided to give a lecture course on a topic in the area of curves on an [algebraic surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_surface "Algebraic surface"), to illustrate the importance of the innovative approach in algebraic geometry of [Alexander Grothendieck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Grothendieck "Alexander Grothendieck"), based at [IHES](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IHES "IHES") near Paris. The class notes of this course, given 1963–4, were published in 1966 as *Lectures on Curves on an Algebraic Surface*, and in the Introduction Mumford wrote that the goal was to give an exposition of a "[corollary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corollary "Corollary")" in [Bourbaki seminar #221](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Séminaire_Nicolas_Bourbaki_(1960–1969) "Séminaire Nicolas Bourbaki (1960–1969)") given by Grothendieck in early 1961, *Techniques de construction et théorèmes d'existence en géométrie algébrique. IV : Les schémas de Hilbert*, in the [TDTE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TDTE "TDTE") series. It related to a vexed question in the legacy of the [Italian school of algebraic geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_school_of_algebraic_geometry "Italian school of algebraic geometry"), called the "completeness of the characteristic system" of a suitable algebraic system of curves. The issue was of a possible "[pathology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pathological_(mathematics) "Pathological (mathematics)")", and the burden of the course was to show that a [necessary and sufficient condition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Necessary_and_sufficient_condition "Necessary and sufficient condition") for the validity of the theorem was now available. In parallel, [Robin Hartshorne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robin_Hartshorne "Robin Hartshorne") was leading a seminar at Harvard, "Residues and Duality", on [coherent duality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coherent_duality "Coherent duality") in Grothendieck's [derived category](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derived_category "Derived category") setting, in which Mumford, Tate, [Stephen Lichtenbaum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Lichtenbaum "Stephen Lichtenbaum") and others participated.

### *The Red Book*

Mumford's lecture notes on [scheme theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scheme_theory "Scheme theory") circulated for years in unpublished form. A geometric motivation for the theory brought forward by Mumford was "[infinitesimals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinitesimals "Infinitesimals") well adapted to algebraic and geometric structures." In 1979 in the *[Bulletin of the American Mathematical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bulletin_of_the_American_Mathematical_Society "Bulletin of the American Mathematical Society")* a reviewer wrote of this work that it "initiated a generation of students to the subject when nothing else was available." At the time, they were, beside the treatise *[Éléments de géométrie algébrique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Éléments_de_géométrie_algébrique "Éléments de géométrie algébrique")*, the only accessible introduction.

Starting in 1967, the notes were mimeographed, bound in red cardboard, and distributed by Harvard's mathematics department under the title *Introduction to Algebraic Geometry (Preliminary version of first 3 Chapters)*. Later (1988; 1999, 2nd ed., [ISBN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISBN_(identifier) "ISBN (identifier)") [3-540-63293-X](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special:BookSources/3-540-63293-X "Special:BookSources/3-540-63293-X")), they were published by Springer under the [Lecture Notes in Mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lecture_Notes_in_Mathematics "Lecture Notes in Mathematics") series as *The Red Book of Varieties and Schemes*. In an introduction to the 1988 edition, Mumford wrote

> [...] Grothendieck came along and turned a confused world of researchers upside down, overwhelming them with the new terminology of schemes as well as with a huge production of new and very exciting results. These notes attempted to show something that was still very controversial at that time: that schemes really were the most natural language for algebraic geometry [...]

### Grothendieck's relative point of view

In *Lectures on Curves on an Algebraic Surface*, Mumford wrote "by far the most important categorical notion for algebraic geometry is that of [fibre product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibre_product "Fibre product")." Lectures 3 and 4 (with its Appendix on the [Zariski tangent space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zariski_tangent_space "Zariski tangent space")) were a concise introduction to the circle of ideas, based on scheme theory (the book uses the older terminology of pre-schemes) known as [Grothendieck's relative point of view](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grothendieck's_relative_point_of_view "Grothendieck's relative point of view"), with geometric illustrations. The [representable functor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Representable_functor "Representable functor") point of view is central: Mumford illustrates it by citing contemporary research: [Brown's representability theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brown's_representability_theorem "Brown's representability theorem") in [homotopy theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homotopy_theory "Homotopy theory"), where representable functors are clearly distinguished; Tate's [rigid analytic spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rigid_analytic_space "Rigid analytic space"); [Jaap Murre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jaap_Murre "Jaap Murre")'s work on functors to the [category of groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category_of_groups "Category of groups"); and [Teruhisa Matsusaka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teruhisa_Matsusaka "Teruhisa Matsusaka")'s theory of Q-varieties as an extension of the functors considered.

Grothendieck made a case for the "representable functor" approach to replace the [universal object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_object "Universal object") conceptualization then prevalent in [category theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category_theory "Category theory"). For moduli spaces, the classical idea of a "universal family of curves" would therefore be modified to a functor "of curves", with a proof that it was representable. The satisfactory picture from homotopy theory, of a [universal bundle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_bundle "Universal bundle") with base space a [classifying space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classifying_space "Classifying space") that represents bundles does not, however, apply directly to moduli. Hence the distinction between a "coarse moduli space", via its [geometric points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric_point "Geometric point") a universal object, and a [fine moduli space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fine_moduli_space "Fine moduli space") which represents a functor of all parametrized families of curves. The latter is in line with the "relative point of view". In *Geometric Invariant Theory* Mumford laid out the issues for moduli theory, stating that currently "one knows very few ways in which to pose a plausible fine moduli problem." He went on to state that in technical terms the fine moduli problem was not "essentially harder", given "descent machinery", a reference to the role [descent morphisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Descent_morphism?action=edit&redlink=1 "Descent morphism") play.

This background explains why [faithfully flat descent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faithfully_flat_descent "Faithfully flat descent") is introduced in Lecture 4 of *Lectures on Curves on an Algebraic Surface*: as a necessary condition for representability of a functor. Mumford names the representability issue for schemes "Grothendieck's existence problem", a nod to [Riemann's existence theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann's_existence_theorem "Riemann's existence theorem") and to Grothendieck's existence theorem for [formal schemes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_scheme "Formal scheme"). Mumford's remark about fibre products may be unpacked in later terms of [Grothendieck fibrations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grothendieck_fibration "Grothendieck fibration") and [descent theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Descent_theory "Descent theory"). He went much further into the relative theory, in defining "modular families" of curves via [Grothendieck topologies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grothendieck_topologies "Grothendieck topologies"), in *Picard Groups of Moduli Problems*.

The lacuna of the mid-1960s theory in terms of sufficient conditions for representability of functors, as would apply to moduli problems, was filled through work of [Michael Artin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Artin "Michael Artin") and others by 1969. There were two sides to this advance. (1) Further innovation in the foundations came with [algebraic spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_space "Algebraic space") (more general than schemes but more restricted than Matsusaka's Q-varieties or Mumford's stacks). Algebraic spaces allow more quotients by equivalence relations than schemes. (2) This definition was combined with a thorough use of algebraic infinitesimal techniques around [artinian rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artinian_ring "Artinian ring") (commutative and [local](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_ring "Local ring")). See [Artin's criterion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artin's_criterion "Artin's criterion"), [formal moduli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_moduli "Formal moduli"), [Schlessinger's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schlessinger's_theorem "Schlessinger's theorem").

## Awards and honors

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/8/83/Mumford2.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail_unscaled)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Mumford2.jpg)

David Mumford in 1975

[MacArthur Fellow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MacArthur_Fellow "MacArthur Fellow") from 1987 to 1992. He won the [Shaw Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shaw_Prize "Shaw Prize") in 2006. In 2007 he was awarded the [Steele Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steele_Prize "Steele Prize") for Mathematical Exposition by the [American Mathematical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Mathematical_Society "American Mathematical Society"). In 2010 he was awarded the [National Medal of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Medal_of_Science "National Medal of Science"). In 2012 he became a fellow of the [American Mathematical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Mathematical_Society "American Mathematical Society").
