**Albert William Tucker** (28 November 1905 – 25 January 1995) was a Canadian [mathematician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematician "Mathematician") who made important contributions in [topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topology "Topology"), [game theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game_theory "Game theory"), and [non-linear programming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-linear_programming "Non-linear programming").

## Early life and education

Albert Tucker was born in [Oshawa, Ontario](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oshawa,_Ontario "Oshawa, Ontario"), Canada, and earned his [B.A.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_Arts "Bachelor of Arts") at the [University of Toronto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Toronto "University of Toronto") in 1928 and his [M.A.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master_of_Arts "Master of Arts") at the same institution in 1929. In 1932, he earned his [Ph.D.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Philosophy "Doctor of Philosophy") at [Princeton University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University "Princeton University") under the supervision of [Solomon Lefschetz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solomon_Lefschetz "Solomon Lefschetz"), with a dissertation entitled *An Abstract Approach to Manifolds*. In 1932–33 he was a National Research Fellow at [Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge "Cambridge"), [Harvard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard "Harvard"), and then [University of Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Chicago "University of Chicago").

## Career

Tucker then returned to Princeton to join the faculty in 1933, where he stayed until 1974. He chaired the mathematics department for about twenty years, one of the longest tenures. His extensive relationships within the field made him a great source for oral histories of the mathematics community.

In 1950, Albert Tucker gave the name and interpretation "[prisoner's dilemma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prisoner's_dilemma "Prisoner's dilemma")" to [Merrill M. Flood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merrill_M._Flood "Merrill M. Flood") and [Melvin Dresher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melvin_Dresher "Melvin Dresher")'s model of cooperation and conflict, resulting in the most well-known game theoretic paradox. He is also well known for the [Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karush–Kuhn–Tucker_conditions "Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions"), a basic result in non-linear programming, which was published in conference proceedings, rather than in a journal.

In the 1960s, he was heavily involved in mathematics education, as chair of the [AP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advanced_Placement_Program "Advanced Placement Program") Calculus committee for the College Board (1960–1963), through work with the Committee on the Undergraduate Program in Mathematics (CUPM) of the [MAA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_Association_of_America "Mathematical Association of America") (he was president of the MAA in 1961–1962), and through many [NSF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Science_Foundation "National Science Foundation") summer workshops for high school and college teachers. [George B. Thomas Jr.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_B._Thomas "George B. Thomas") acknowledged Tucker's contribution of many exercises to Thomas's classic textbook, *Calculus and Analytic Geometry.*

In the early 1980s, Tucker recruited Princeton history professor [Charles Coulston Gillispie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Coulston_Gillispie "Charles Coulston Gillispie") to help him set up an oral history project to preserve stories about the Princeton mathematical community in the 1930s. With funding from the [Sloan Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sloan_Foundation "Sloan Foundation"), this project later expanded its scope. Among those who shared their memories of such figures as [Einstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Einstein "Albert Einstein"), [von Neumann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_von_Neumann "John von Neumann"), and [Gödel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurt_Gödel "Kurt Gödel") were computer pioneer [Herman Goldstine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herman_Goldstine "Herman Goldstine") and Nobel laureates [John Bardeen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Bardeen "John Bardeen") and [Eugene Wigner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugene_Wigner "Eugene Wigner").

## Students and legacy

Tucker's Ph.D. students include [Michel Balinski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michel_Balinski "Michel Balinski"), [David Gale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Gale "David Gale"), [Alan J. Goldman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_J._Goldman "Alan J. Goldman"), [John Isbell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Isbell "John Isbell"), [Stephen Maurer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Maurer?action=edit&redlink=1 "Stephen Maurer"), Turing Award winner [Marvin Minsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marvin_Minsky "Marvin Minsky"), Nobel Prize winner [John Nash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Forbes_Nash "John Forbes Nash"), [Torrence Parsons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torrence_Parsons "Torrence Parsons"), Nobel Prize winner [Lloyd Shapley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lloyd_Shapley "Lloyd Shapley"), Robert Singleton, and [Marjorie Stein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marjorie_Stein?action=edit&redlink=1 "Marjorie Stein"). Tucker advised and collaborated with [Harold W. Kuhn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_W._Kuhn "Harold W. Kuhn") on a number of papers and mathematical models.

Tucker noticed the leadership ability and talent of a young mathematics graduate student named [John G. Kemeny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_George_Kemeny "John George Kemeny"), whose hiring Tucker suggested to [Dartmouth College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dartmouth_College "Dartmouth College"). Following Tucker's advice, Dartmouth recruited Kemeny, who became Chair of the Mathematics Department and later College President. Years later, Dartmouth College recognized Albert Tucker with an honorary degree.

Tucker died in [Hightstown, N.J.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hightstown,_New_Jersey "Hightstown, New Jersey") in 1995 at age 89. His sons, [Alan Tucker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_Tucker "Alan Tucker") and [Thomas W. Tucker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_W._Tucker "Thomas W. Tucker"), and his grandson [Thomas J. Tucker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_J._Tucker?action=edit&redlink=1 "Thomas J. Tucker") are all professional mathematicians.

### Tucker Prize

At each (triennial) International Symposium of the [Mathematical Optimization Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_Optimization_Society "Mathematical Optimization Society") (MOS) the [Tucker Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tucker_Prize "Tucker Prize"), in honour of A. W. Tucker, is given for outstanding thesis in the area of [discrete mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_mathematics "Discrete mathematics").
