**Sir Erik Christopher Zeeman** [FRS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow_of_the_Royal_Society "Fellow of the Royal Society") (4 February 1925 – 13 February 2016) was a British mathematician known for his work in [geometric topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric_topology "Geometric topology") and [singularity theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singularity_theory "Singularity theory").

## Overview

Zeeman's main contributions to mathematics were in [topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topology "Topology"), particularly in [knot theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knot_theory "Knot theory"), the [piecewise linear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piecewise_linear_manifold "Piecewise linear manifold") category, and [dynamical systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamical_systems "Dynamical systems").

His 1955 thesis at the [University of Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Cambridge "University of Cambridge") described a new theory termed "dihomology", an algebraic structure associated to a [topological space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_space "Topological space"), containing both [homology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homology_(mathematics) "Homology (mathematics)") and [cohomology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cohomology "Cohomology"), introducing what is now known as the Zeeman [spectral sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectral_sequence "Spectral sequence"). This was studied by Clint McCrory in his 1972 Brandeis thesis following a suggestion of [Dennis Sullivan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dennis_Sullivan "Dennis Sullivan") that one make "a general study of the Zeeman [spectral sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectral_sequence "Spectral sequence") to see how singularities in a space perturb [Poincaré duality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poincaré_duality "Poincaré duality")". This in turn led to the discovery of [intersection homology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intersection_homology "Intersection homology") by [Robert MacPherson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_MacPherson_(mathematician) "Robert MacPherson (mathematician)") and [Mark Goresky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Goresky "Mark Goresky") at [Brown University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brown_University "Brown University") where McCrory was appointed in 1974.

From 1976 to 1977 he was the [Donegall Lecturer in Mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donegall_Lecturer_in_Mathematics "Donegall Lecturer in Mathematics") at [Trinity College Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College_Dublin "Trinity College Dublin").

Zeeman is known among the wider scientific public for his contribution to, and spreading awareness of [catastrophe theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catastrophe_theory "Catastrophe theory"), which was due initially to another topologist, [René Thom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/René_Thom "René Thom"), and for his Christmas lectures about mathematics on television in 1978. He was especially active in encouraging the application of mathematics, and catastrophe theory in particular, to biology and behavioural sciences.

## Early life

Zeeman was born in Japan to a Danish father, Christian Zeeman, and a British mother. They moved to England one year after his birth. After being educated at [Christ's Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christ's_Hospital "Christ's Hospital") in [Horsham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horsham "Horsham"), West Sussex, he served as a [Flying Officer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flying_Officer "Flying Officer") with the [Royal Air Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Air_Force "Royal Air Force") from 1943 to 1947. He studied mathematics at [Christ's College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christ's_College,_Cambridge "Christ's College, Cambridge"), but had forgotten much of his school mathematics while serving in the Air Force. He received an [MA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master_of_Arts_(Oxbridge) "Master of Arts (Oxbridge)") and PhD (the latter under the supervision of [Shaun Wylie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shaun_Wylie "Shaun Wylie")) from the University of Cambridge, and became a Fellow of [Gonville and Caius College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gonville_and_Caius_College "Gonville and Caius College") where he tutored [David Fowler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Fowler_(mathematician) "David Fowler (mathematician)") and [John Horton Conway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Horton_Conway "John Horton Conway").

## Academic career

Zeeman is one of the founders of engulfing theory in piecewise linear topology and is credited with working out the engulfing theorem (independently also worked out by John Stallings), which can be used to prove the piecewise linear version of the [Poincaré conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poincaré_conjecture "Poincaré conjecture") for all dimensions above four.

After working at [Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Cambridge "University of Cambridge") (during which he spent a year abroad at [University of Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Chicago "University of Chicago") and [Princeton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University "Princeton University") as a [Harkness Fellow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harkness_Fellowship "Harkness Fellowship")) and the [Institut des Hautes Études Scientifiques](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institut_des_Hautes_Études_Scientifiques "Institut des Hautes Études Scientifiques"), he founded the Mathematics Department and Mathematics Research Centre at the new [University of Warwick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Warwick "University of Warwick") in 1964. In his own words

:   *I was 38 and had developed some fairly strong ideas on how to run a department and create a Mathematics Institute: I wanted to combine the flexibility of options that are common in most American universities, with the kind of tutorial care to be found in Oxford and Cambridge.*

Zeeman's style of leadership was informal, but inspirational, and he rapidly took Warwick to international recognition for the quality of its mathematical research. The first six appointments he made were all in topology, enabling the department to immediately become internationally competitive, followed by six in algebra, and finally six in analysis and six in applied mathematics. He was able to trade four academic appointments for funding that enabled PhD students to give undergraduate supervision in groups of two for the first two years, in a manner similar to the tutorial system at Oxford and Cambridge. He remained at Warwick until 1988, but from 1966 to 1967 he was a visiting professor at the [University of California at Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Berkeley "University of California, Berkeley"), after which his research turned to dynamical systems, inspired by many of the world leaders in this field, including [Stephen Smale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Smale "Stephen Smale") and [René Thom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/René_Thom "René Thom"), who both spent time at Warwick. In 1963, Zeeman showed that causality in special relativity expressed by the preservation of partial ordering is given exactly and only by the [Lorentz transforms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentz_transforms "Lorentz transforms"). Zeeman subsequently spent a sabbatical with Thom at the Institut des Hautes Études Scientifiques in Bures-sur-Yvette near Paris, where he became interested in [catastrophe theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catastrophe_theory "Catastrophe theory"). On his return to Warwick, he taught an undergraduate course in Catastrophe Theory that became immensely popular with students; his lectures generally were "standing room only". In 1973 he gave an MSc course at Warwick giving a complete detailed proof of Thom's classification of elementary catastrophes, mainly following an unpublished manuscript, "Right-equivalence" written by [John Mather](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Mather_(mathematician) "John Mather (mathematician)") at Warwick in 1969. [David Trotman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Trotman "David Trotman") wrote up his notes of the course as an MSc thesis. These were then distributed in thousands of copies throughout the world and published both in the proceedings of a 1975 Seattle conference on catastrophe theory and its applications, and in a 1977 collection of papers on catastrophe theory by Zeeman. In 1974 Zeeman gave an [invited address at the International Congress of Mathematicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_International_Congresses_of_Mathematicians_Plenary_and_Invited_Speakers "List of International Congresses of Mathematicians Plenary and Invited Speakers") in Vancouver, about applications of catastrophe theory.

Zeeman at the Warwick Mathematics Institute in December 2009

Zeeman was elected as a [Fellow of the Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow_of_the_Royal_Society "Fellow of the Royal Society") in 1975, and was awarded the Society's Faraday Medal in 1988. He was the 63rd President of the [London Mathematical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Mathematical_Society "London Mathematical Society") in 1986–88 giving his Presidential Address on 18 November 1988 *On the classification of dynamical systems*. He was awarded the [Senior Whitehead Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senior_Whitehead_Prize "Senior Whitehead Prize") of the Society in 1982. He was the Society's first Forder lecturer, involving a lecture tour in New Zealand, in 1987. Between 1988 and 1994 he was the [Professor of Geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gresham_Professor_of_Geometry "Gresham Professor of Geometry") at [Gresham College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gresham_College "Gresham College").

In 1978, Zeeman gave the televised series of [Christmas Lectures at the Royal Institution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Institution_Christmas_Lectures "Royal Institution Christmas Lectures"). From these grew the Mathematics and Engineering Masterclasses for both primary and secondary school children that now flourish in forty centers in the United Kingdom.

In 1988, Zeeman became Principal of [Hertford College, Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hertford_College,_Oxford "Hertford College, Oxford"). The following year he was appointed an honorary fellow of [Christ's College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christ's_College,_Cambridge "Christ's College, Cambridge"). He received a [knighthood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knight_Bachelor "Knight Bachelor") in the [1991 Birthday Honours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1991_Birthday_Honours "1991 Birthday Honours") for "mathematical excellence and service to British mathematics and mathematics education". He was invited to become President of [The Mathematical Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_Association "Mathematical Association") in 2003 and based his book *Three-dimensional Theorems for Schools* on his 2004 Presidential Address. On Friday 6 May 2005, the University of Warwick's new Mathematics and Statistics building was named the Zeeman Building in his honour. He became an Honorary Member of [The Mathematical Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Mathematical_Association "The Mathematical Association") in 2006. In September 2006, the [London Mathematical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Mathematical_Society "London Mathematical Society") and the [Institute of Mathematics and its Applications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_of_Mathematics_and_its_Applications "Institute of Mathematics and its Applications") awarded him the [David Crighton medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category:David_Crighton_medalists "Category:David Crighton medalists") in recognition of his long and distinguished service to mathematics and the mathematical community. The medal is awarded triennially, and Zeeman was the second-ever recipient of the award. He died on 13 February 2016.

## The Zeeman Medal

The *[Christopher Zeeman Medal for Communication of Mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Zeeman_Medal_for_Communication_of_Mathematics "Christopher Zeeman Medal for Communication of Mathematics")* of the [London Mathematical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Mathematical_Society "London Mathematical Society") and the [Institute of Mathematics and its Applications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_of_Mathematics_and_its_Applications "Institute of Mathematics and its Applications") is named in Zeeman's honour. The award aims "to honour mathematicians who have excelled in promoting mathematics and engaging with the general public. They may be academic mathematicians based in universities, mathematics school teachers, industrial mathematicians, those working in the financial sector or indeed mathematicians from any number of other fields".
