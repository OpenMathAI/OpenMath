**Gorō Shimura** (志村 五郎, *Shimura Gorō*; 23 February 1930 – 3 May 2019) was a Japanese [mathematician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematician "Mathematician") and Michael Henry Strater [Professor Emeritus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Professor_Emeritus "Professor Emeritus") of [Mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics "Mathematics") at [Princeton University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University "Princeton University") who worked in [number theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_theory "Number theory"), [automorphic forms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automorphic_form "Automorphic form"), and [arithmetic geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arithmetic_geometry "Arithmetic geometry"). He was known for developing the theory of [complex multiplication of abelian varieties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abelian_variety_of_CM-type "Abelian variety of CM-type") and [Shimura varieties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shimura_variety "Shimura variety"), as well as posing the [Taniyama–Shimura conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taniyama–Shimura_conjecture "Taniyama–Shimura conjecture") which ultimately led to the [proof](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wiles's_proof_of_Fermat's_Last_Theorem "Wiles's proof of Fermat's Last Theorem") of [Fermat's Last Theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermat's_Last_Theorem "Fermat's Last Theorem").

## Biography

Gorō Shimura was born in [Hamamatsu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamamatsu "Hamamatsu"), [Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan "Japan"), on 23 February 1930. Shimura graduated with a B.A. in mathematics and a D.Sc. in mathematics from the [University of Tokyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Tokyo "University of Tokyo") in 1952 and 1958, respectively.

After graduating, Shimura became a lecturer at the University of Tokyo, then worked abroad — including ten months in Paris and a seven-month stint at Princeton's [Institute for Advanced Study](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_for_Advanced_Study "Institute for Advanced Study") — before returning to Tokyo, where he married Chikako Ishiguro. He then moved from Tokyo to join the faculty of [Osaka University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osaka_University "Osaka University"), but growing unhappy with his funding situation, he decided to seek employment in the United States. Through [André Weil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/André_Weil "André Weil") he obtained a position at Princeton University. Shimura joined the Princeton faculty in 1964 and retired in 1999, during which time he advised over 28 doctoral students and received the [Guggenheim Fellowship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guggenheim_Fellowship "Guggenheim Fellowship") in 1970, the [Cole Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cole_Prize "Cole Prize") for number theory in 1977, the [Asahi Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asahi_Prize "Asahi Prize") in 1991, and the [Steele Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steele_Prize "Steele Prize") for lifetime achievement in 1996.

Shimura described his approach to mathematics as "phenomenological": his interest was in finding new types of interesting behavior in the theory of automorphic forms. He also argued for a "romantic" approach, something he found lacking in the younger generation of mathematicians. Shimura used a two-part process for research, using one desk in his home dedicated to working on new research in the mornings and a second desk for perfecting papers in the afternoon.

Shimura had two children, Tomoko and Haru, with his wife Chikako. Shimura died on 3 May 2019 in [Princeton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton,_New_Jersey "Princeton, New Jersey"), [New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Jersey "New Jersey") at the age of 89.

## Research

Shimura was a colleague and a friend of [Yutaka Taniyama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yutaka_Taniyama "Yutaka Taniyama"), with whom he wrote the first book on the [complex multiplication of abelian varieties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abelian_variety_of_CM-type "Abelian variety of CM-type") and formulated the Taniyama–Shimura conjecture. Shimura then wrote a long series of major papers, extending the phenomena found in the theory of [complex multiplication of elliptic curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_multiplication "Complex multiplication") and the theory of [modular forms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modular_form "Modular form") to higher dimensions (e.g. Shimura varieties). This work provided examples for which the equivalence between [motivic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motivic_L-function "Motivic L-function") and [automorphic *L*-functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automorphic_L-function "Automorphic L-function") postulated in the [Langlands program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Langlands_program "Langlands program") could be tested: [automorphic forms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automorphic_form "Automorphic form") realized in the [cohomology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cohomology "Cohomology") of a Shimura variety have a construction that attaches [Galois representations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galois_representation "Galois representation") to them.

In 1958, Shimura generalized the initial work of [Martin Eichler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Eichler "Martin Eichler") on the [Eichler–Shimura congruence relation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eichler–Shimura_congruence_relation "Eichler–Shimura congruence relation") between the [local *L*-function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_zeta_function "Local zeta function") of a [modular curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modular_curve "Modular curve") and the eigenvalues of [Hecke operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hecke_operators "Hecke operators"). In 1959, Shimura extended the work of Eichler on the [Eichler–Shimura isomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eichler–Shimura_isomorphism "Eichler–Shimura isomorphism") between Eichler cohomology groups and spaces of [cusp forms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cusp_form "Cusp form") which would be used in [Pierre Deligne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Deligne "Pierre Deligne")'s proof of the [Weil conjectures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weil_conjectures "Weil conjectures").

In 1971, Shimura's work on explicit [class field theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Class_field_theory "Class field theory") in the spirit of [Kronecker's Jugendtraum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilbert's_twelfth_problem "Hilbert's twelfth problem") resulted in his proof of [Shimura's reciprocity law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shimura's_reciprocity_law "Shimura's reciprocity law"). In 1973, Shimura established the [Shimura correspondence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shimura_correspondence "Shimura correspondence") between modular forms of half integral weight *k*+1/2, and modular forms of even weight 2*k*.

Shimura's formulation of the Taniyama–Shimura conjecture (later known as the modularity theorem) in the 1950s played a key role in the proof of Fermat's Last Theorem by [Andrew Wiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Wiles "Andrew Wiles") in 1995. In 1990, [Kenneth Ribet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenneth_Ribet "Kenneth Ribet") proved [Ribet's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ribet's_theorem "Ribet's theorem") which demonstrated that Fermat's Last Theorem followed from the semistable case of this conjecture. Shimura dryly commented that his first reaction on hearing of [Andrew Wiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Wiles "Andrew Wiles")'s proof of the semistable case was 'I told you so'.

## Other interests

His hobbies were [shogi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shogi "Shogi") problems of extreme length and collecting [Imari porcelain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imari_porcelain "Imari porcelain"). *The Story of Imari: The Symbols and Mysteries of Antique Japanese Porcelain* is a non-fiction work about the Imari porcelain that he collected over 30 years that was published by [Ten Speed Press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ten_Speed_Press "Ten Speed Press") in 2008.
