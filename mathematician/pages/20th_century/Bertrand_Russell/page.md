**Bertrand Arthur William Russell, 3rd Earl Russell** (18 May 1872 – 2 February 1970), was an English [philosopher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy "Philosophy"), [logician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logic "Logic"), and [social reformer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reformism "Reformism").

Russell was born into the prominent [Russell family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clan_Russell "Clan Russell"), to the [Viscount](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Russell,_Viscount_Amberley "John Russell, Viscount Amberley") and [Viscountess Amberley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Katharine_Russell,_Viscountess_Amberley "Katharine Russell, Viscountess Amberley"). He was [orphaned](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orphan "Orphan") at the age of four and moved into [Pembroke Lodge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pembroke_Lodge "Pembroke Lodge") to live with his grandparents, the [Earl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Russell,_1st_Earl_Russell "John Russell, 1st Earl Russell") and [Countess Russell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frances_Russell,_Countess_Russell "Frances Russell, Countess Russell"). The Earl Russell had twice been [Prime Minister of the United Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_Minister_of_the_United_Kingdom "Prime Minister of the United Kingdom") (1846–1852; 1865–1866) and died in 1878, leaving Russell in the care of his grandmother. He won a scholarship to [Trinity College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College,_Cambridge "Trinity College, Cambridge"), in 1890 and graduated in 1893 with a [Bachelor of Arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_Arts "Bachelor of Arts"). At Cambridge, Russell was introduced to philosophy through figures such as [G. E. Moore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G._E._Moore "G. E. Moore") and [Alfred North Whitehead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_North_Whitehead "Alfred North Whitehead"). Russell also came to oppose [British idealism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_idealism "British idealism") and led the "revolt against idealism" with Moore.

He was one of the early 20th century's prominent [logicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logic "Logic") and a founder of [analytic philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytic_philosophy "Analytic philosophy"), along with his predecessor [Gottlob Frege](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gottlob_Frege "Gottlob Frege"), his friend and colleague [G. E. Moore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G._E._Moore "G. E. Moore"), and his student and protégé [Ludwig Wittgenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludwig_Wittgenstein "Ludwig Wittgenstein"). Russell with Moore led the British "revolt against [idealism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_idealism "British idealism")". Together with his former teacher [Alfred North Whitehead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_North_Whitehead "Alfred North Whitehead"), Russell wrote *[Principia Mathematica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principia_Mathematica "Principia Mathematica")*, a milestone in the development of [classical logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_logic "Classical logic") and a major attempt to reduce the whole of mathematics to [logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logic "Logic") (see [logicism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logicism "Logicism")). Russell's article "[On Denoting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_Denoting "On Denoting")" has been considered a "paradigm of philosophy".

Russell was educated at [Trinity College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College,_Cambridge "Trinity College, Cambridge") at the [University of Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Cambridge "University of Cambridge"), where he graduated in 1893. He was a [pacifist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pacifism "Pacifism") who championed [anti-imperialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-imperialism "Anti-imperialism") and chaired the England-based [India League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/India_League "India League"). He went to prison for his pacifism during the [First World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_World_War "First World War"), and he initially supported appeasing [Adolf Hitler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adolf_Hitler "Adolf Hitler")'s [Nazi Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi_Germany "Nazi Germany"), before changing his view in 1943, describing war as a necessary "[lesser of two evils](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lesser_of_two_evils_principle "Lesser of two evils principle")". In the wake of the [Second World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_World_War "Second World War"), he welcomed American global [hegemony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hegemony "Hegemony") in preference to either [Soviet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union "Soviet Union") hegemony or no (or ineffective) world leadership, even if it were to come at the cost of using their [nuclear weapons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_weapon "Nuclear weapon"). He later criticised [Stalinist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stalinism "Stalinism") [totalitarianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Totalitarianism "Totalitarianism"), condemned the United States' involvement in the [Vietnam War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnam_War "Vietnam War"), and became an outspoken proponent of [nuclear disarmament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_disarmament "Nuclear disarmament").

In 1950, Russell was awarded the [Nobel Prize in Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1950_Nobel_Prize_in_Literature "1950 Nobel Prize in Literature") "in recognition of his varied and significant writings in which he champions humanitarian ideals and [freedom of thought](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_of_thought "Freedom of thought")". He was also the recipient of the [De Morgan Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Morgan_Medal "De Morgan Medal") (1932), [Sylvester Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sylvester_Medal "Sylvester Medal") (1934), [Kalinga Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalinga_Prize "Kalinga Prize") (1957), and [Jerusalem Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerusalem_Prize "Jerusalem Prize") (1963).

## Biography

### Early life and background

Bertrand Arthur William Russell was born at [Ravenscroft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cleddon_Hall "Cleddon Hall"), a country house in [Trellech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trellech "Trellech"), Monmouthshire, on 18 May 1872, into an influential and liberal family of the [British aristocracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_aristocracy "British aristocracy"). His parents were [Viscount](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Russell,_Viscount_Amberley "John Russell, Viscount Amberley") and [Viscountess Amberley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Katharine_Russell,_Viscountess_Amberley "Katharine Russell, Viscountess Amberley"). Both were early advocates of [birth control](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_control "Birth control") at a time when this was considered scandalous. Lord Amberley consented to his wife's relationship with their children's tutor, the biologist [Douglas Spalding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Douglas_Spalding "Douglas Spalding"). Lord Amberley, a [deist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deist "Deist"), asked the philosopher [John Stuart Mill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Stuart_Mill "John Stuart Mill") to act as Russell's secular godfather. Mill died the year after Russell's birth, but his writings later influenced Russell's life.

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/fd/Bertrand_Russell_in_1876.jpg/250px-Bertrand_Russell_in_1876.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Bertrand_Russell_in_1876.jpg)

Russell as a 4-year-old

Russell's paternal grandfather, Lord John Russell, later [1st Earl Russell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Russell,_1st_Earl_Russell "John Russell, 1st Earl Russell"), had twice been [Prime Minister of the United Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_Minister_of_the_United_Kingdom "Prime Minister of the United Kingdom") in the 1840s and 1860s. A member of Parliament since the early 1810s, he met [Napoleon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleon "Napoleon") in [Elba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elba "Elba"). The Russells had been prominent in England for several centuries before this, coming to power and the [peerage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peerage "Peerage") with the rise of the [Tudor dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tudor_dynasty "Tudor dynasty") (see: [Duke of Bedford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duke_of_Bedford "Duke of Bedford")). They established themselves as one of the leading [Whig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Whig_Party "British Whig Party") families and participated in political events from the [dissolution of the monasteries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissolution_of_the_monasteries "Dissolution of the monasteries") in 1536–1540 to the [Glorious Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glorious_Revolution "Glorious Revolution") in 1688–1689 and the [Great Reform Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Reform_Act "Great Reform Act") in 1832.

Lady Amberley was the daughter of [Lord](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Stanley,_2nd_Baron_Stanley_of_Alderley "Edward Stanley, 2nd Baron Stanley of Alderley") and [Lady Stanley of Alderley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henrietta_Stanley,_Baroness_Stanley_of_Alderley "Henrietta Stanley, Baroness Stanley of Alderley"). Russell often feared the ridicule of his maternal grandmother, one of the campaigners for [education of women](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education_of_women "Education of women").

### Childhood and adolescence

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/23/Pembroke_Lodge%2C_Richmond_Park.jpg/250px-Pembroke_Lodge%2C_Richmond_Park.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Pembroke_Lodge,_Richmond_Park.jpg)

Childhood home, [Pembroke Lodge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pembroke_Lodge,_Richmond_Park "Pembroke Lodge, Richmond Park"), Richmond Park, London

Russell had two siblings: brother [Frank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Russell,_2nd_Earl_Russell "Frank Russell, 2nd Earl Russell") (seven years older), and sister Rachel (four years older). In June 1874 Russell's mother died of [diphtheria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diphtheria "Diphtheria"), followed shortly by Rachel's death. In January 1876, his father died of [bronchitis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronchitis "Bronchitis") after a long period of [depression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Major_depressive_disorder "Major depressive disorder"). Frank and Bertrand were placed in the care of paternal grandparents, who lived at [Pembroke Lodge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pembroke_Lodge,_Richmond_Park "Pembroke Lodge, Richmond Park") in [Richmond Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richmond_Park "Richmond Park"). His grandfather, former Prime Minister [Earl Russell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Russell,_1st_Earl_Russell "John Russell, 1st Earl Russell"), died in 1878, and was remembered by Russell as a kind old man in a wheelchair. His grandmother, the [Countess Russell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frances_Russell,_Countess_Russell "Frances Russell, Countess Russell") (née Lady Frances Elliot), was the central family figure for the rest of Russell's childhood and youth. The Countess was from a Scottish [Presbyterian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presbyterian "Presbyterian") family and petitioned the [Court of Chancery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Court_of_Chancery "Court of Chancery") to set aside a provision in Amberley's will requiring the children to be raised as agnostics. Despite her religious conservatism, she held progressive views in other areas (accepting [Darwinism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darwinism "Darwinism") and supporting [Irish Home Rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Home_Rule "Home Rule")), and her influence on Bertrand Russell's outlook on [social justice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_justice "Social justice") and standing up for principle remained with him throughout his life.

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/71/Young_Bertrand_Russell%2C_circa_late_1880s.jpg/250px-Young_Bertrand_Russell%2C_circa_late_1880s.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Young_Bertrand_Russell,_circa_late_1880s.jpg)

Russell at [Trinity College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College,_Cambridge "Trinity College, Cambridge"), in 1891

Russell's adolescence was lonely and he contemplated suicide. He remarked in his autobiography that his interests in "nature and books and (later) mathematics saved me from complete despondency;" only his wish to know more mathematics kept him from suicide. He was educated at home by a series of tutors. When Russell was eleven, his brother Frank introduced him to the work of [Euclid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclid "Euclid"), which he described in his autobiography as "one of the great events of my life, as dazzling as first love".

During these formative years, he also discovered the works of [Percy Bysshe Shelley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Percy_Bysshe_Shelley "Percy Bysshe Shelley"). Russell wrote: "I spent all my spare time reading him, and learning him by heart, knowing no one to whom I could speak of what I thought or felt, I used to reflect how wonderful it would have been to know Shelley, and to wonder whether I should meet any live human being with whom I should feel so much sympathy." Russell said that beginning at age 15, he spent considerable time thinking about the validity of [Christian religious dogma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dogma#In_religion "Dogma"), which he found unconvincing. At this age, he came to the conclusion that there is no [free will](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_will "Free will") and, two years later, that there is no life after death. Finally, at the age of 18, after reading Mill's *Autobiography*, he abandoned the "[First Cause](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Cause "First Cause")" argument and became an [atheist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atheist "Atheist").

He travelled to the continent in 1890 with an American friend, [Edward FitzGerald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_FitzGerald_(mountaineer) "Edward FitzGerald (mountaineer)"), and with FitzGerald's family he visited the [Paris Exhibition of 1889](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paris_Exhibition_of_1889 "Paris Exhibition of 1889") and climbed the [Eiffel Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eiffel_Tower "Eiffel Tower") soon after it was completed.

### Education

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/1e/Portrait_of_Bertrand_Russell_in_1893.jpg/250px-Portrait_of_Bertrand_Russell_in_1893.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Portrait_of_Bertrand_Russell_in_1893.jpg)

Russell at [Trinity College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College,_Cambridge "Trinity College, Cambridge"), in 1893

Russell won a scholarship to read for the [Mathematical Tripos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_Tripos "Mathematical Tripos") at [Trinity College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College,_Cambridge "Trinity College, Cambridge"), and began his studies there in 1890, taking as coach [Robert Rumsey Webb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Rumsey_Webb "Robert Rumsey Webb"). He became acquainted with the younger [George Edward Moore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Edward_Moore "George Edward Moore") and came under the influence of [Alfred North Whitehead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_North_Whitehead "Alfred North Whitehead"), who recommended him to the [Cambridge Apostles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_Apostles "Cambridge Apostles"). He distinguished himself in mathematics and philosophy, graduating as seventh [Wrangler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wrangler_(University_of_Cambridge) "Wrangler (University of Cambridge)") in the former in 1893 and becoming a Fellow in the latter in 1895. During his time at Cambridge, Russell increasingly turned from mathematics toward philosophy, influenced by contemporary debates about idealism and the foundations of geometry. Although initially sympathetic to [British Idealism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_idealism "British idealism"), he later rejected it, developing the realist approach that would become central to analytic philosophy. His early academic work focused on the logical foundations of mathematics, culminating in his first major publication, *[An Essay on the Foundations of Geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/An_Essay_on_the_Foundations_of_Geometry?action=edit&redlink=1 "An Essay on the Foundations of Geometry")* (1897).

Russell's election to a Fellowship at Trinity College in 1895 provided him with financial independence and the freedom to pursue research. During these formative years, he began the investigations into logic and set theory that would eventually lead to his work on logicism and his collaboration with Alfred North Whitehead on *Principia Mathematica*.

### Early career

Russell began his published work in 1896 with *German Social Democracy*, a study in politics that was an early indication of his interest in political and social theory. In 1896 he taught German social democracy at the [London School of Economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_School_of_Economics "London School of Economics"). He was a member of the [Coefficients dining club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coefficients_(dining_club) "Coefficients (dining club)") of social reformers set up in 1902 by the [Fabian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fabian_Society "Fabian Society") campaigners [Sidney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidney_Webb "Sidney Webb") and [Beatrice Webb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beatrice_Webb "Beatrice Webb").

He now started a study of the [foundations of mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foundations_of_mathematics "Foundations of mathematics") at Trinity. In 1897, he wrote *[An Essay on the Foundations of Geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/An_Essay_on_the_Foundations_of_Geometry?action=edit&redlink=1 "An Essay on the Foundations of Geometry")* (submitted at the [Fellowship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow "Fellow") Examination of Trinity College) which discussed the [Cayley–Klein metrics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cayley–Klein_metric "Cayley–Klein metric") used for [non-Euclidean geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-Euclidean_geometry "Non-Euclidean geometry"). He attended the first [International Congress of Philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Congress_of_Philosophy "International Congress of Philosophy") in Paris in 1900 where he met [Giuseppe Peano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giuseppe_Peano "Giuseppe Peano") and [Alessandro Padoa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alessandro_Padoa "Alessandro Padoa"). The Italians had responded to [Georg Cantor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georg_Cantor "Georg Cantor"), making a science of [set theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Set_theory "Set theory"); they gave Russell their literature including the *[Formulario mathematico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formulario_mathematico "Formulario mathematico")*. Russell was impressed by the precision of Peano's arguments at the Congress, read the literature upon returning to England, and came upon [Russell's paradox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russell's_paradox "Russell's paradox"). In 1903 he published *[The Principles of Mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Principles_of_Mathematics "The Principles of Mathematics")*, a work on the foundations of mathematics. It advanced a thesis of [logicism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logicism "Logicism"), that mathematics and logic are one and the same.

In February 1901, at the age of 29, Russell underwent what he called a "sort of mystic illumination", after witnessing [Whitehead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_North_Whitehead "Alfred North Whitehead")'s wife's suffering in an [angina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angina_pectoris "Angina pectoris") attack. "I found myself filled with semi-mystical feelings about beauty and with a desire almost as profound as that of the [Buddha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddha "Buddha") to find some philosophy which should make human life endurable", Russell would later recall. "At the end of those five minutes, I had become a completely different person."

In 1905, he wrote the essay "[On Denoting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_Denoting "On Denoting")", which was published in the philosophical journal *[Mind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mind_(journal) "Mind (journal)")*. Russell was elected a [Fellow of the Royal Society (FRS) in 1908](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_fellows_of_the_Royal_Society_elected_in_1908 "List of fellows of the Royal Society elected in 1908"). The three-volume *[Principia Mathematica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principia_Mathematica "Principia Mathematica")*, written with Whitehead, was published between 1910 and 1913. This, along with the earlier *The Principles of Mathematics*, soon made Russell world-famous in his field. Russell's first political activity was as the [Independent Liberal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_Liberal "Independent Liberal") candidate in the [1907 by-election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1907_Wimbledon_by-election "1907 Wimbledon by-election") for the [Wimbledon constituency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wimbledon_(UK_Parliament_constituency) "Wimbledon (UK Parliament constituency)"), where he was not elected.

In 1910, he became a lecturer at the [University of Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Cambridge "University of Cambridge"), Trinity College, where he had studied. He was considered for a fellowship, which would give him a vote in the college government and protect him from being fired for his opinions, but was passed over because he was "anti-clerical", because he was agnostic. He was approached by the Austrian engineering student [Ludwig Wittgenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludwig_Wittgenstein "Ludwig Wittgenstein"), who started undergraduate study with him. Russell viewed Wittgenstein as a successor who would continue his work on logic. He spent hours dealing with Wittgenstein's various [phobias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phobia "Phobia") and his bouts of despair. This was a drain on Russell's energy, but Russell continued to be fascinated by him and encouraged his academic development, including the publication of Wittgenstein's *[Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tractatus_Logico-Philosophicus "Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus")* in 1922. Russell delivered his lectures on [logical atomism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_atomism "Logical atomism"), his version of these ideas, in 1918, before the end of [World War I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I "World War I").

### First World War

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/thumb/e/e4/National_Committee_of_the_No-Conscription_Fellowship_May_1916.gif/250px-National_Committee_of_the_No-Conscription_Fellowship_May_1916.gif?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:National_Committee_of_the_No-Conscription_Fellowship_May_1916.gif)

Russell served on the National Committee of the [No-Conscription Fellowship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/No-Conscription_Fellowship "No-Conscription Fellowship"), shown here in May 1916 (*back right*).

During [World War I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I "World War I"), Russell was one of the few people to engage in active [pacifist activities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opposition_to_World_War_I "Opposition to World War I"). In 1916, due to his absence of allegiance to the war effort, he was dismissed from Trinity College following his conviction under the [Defence of the Realm Act 1914](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Defence_of_the_Realm_Act_1914 "Defence of the Realm Act 1914"). He later described this, in *[Free Thought and Official Propaganda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_Thought_and_Official_Propaganda "Free Thought and Official Propaganda")*, as an illegitimate means the state used to violate freedom of expression. Russell championed the case of [Eric Chappelow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eric_Chappelow "Eric Chappelow"), a poet jailed and abused as a conscientious objector. Russell played a part in the Leeds Convention in June 1917, a historic event which saw well over a thousand "anti-war socialists" gather; many being delegates from the [Independent Labour Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_Labour_Party "Independent Labour Party") and the [British Socialist Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Socialist_Party "British Socialist Party"), united in their pacifist beliefs and advocating a peace settlement. The international press reported that Russell appeared with a number of Labour [Members of Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Member_of_Parliament_(United_Kingdom) "Member of Parliament (United Kingdom)") (MPs), including [Ramsay MacDonald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramsay_MacDonald "Ramsay MacDonald") and [Philip Snowden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_Snowden "Philip Snowden"), as well as former [Liberal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberal_Party_(UK) "Liberal Party (UK)") MP and anti-conscription campaigner, Professor [Arnold Lupton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arnold_Lupton "Arnold Lupton"). After the event, Russell told [Lady Ottoline Morrell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lady_Ottoline_Morrell "Lady Ottoline Morrell") that, "to my surprise, when I got up to speak, I was given the greatest ovation that was possible to give anybody".

His conviction in 1916 resulted in Russell being fined £100 (equivalent to £6,000 in 2025), which he refused to pay in the hope that he would be sent to prison, but his books were sold at auction to raise the money. The books were bought by friends; he later treasured his copy of the [King James Bible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_James_Version "King James Version") that was stamped "Confiscated by Cambridge Police".

A later conviction for publicly lecturing against inviting the United States to enter the war on the United Kingdom's side resulted in six months' imprisonment in [Brixton Prison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HM_Prison_Brixton "HM Prison Brixton") (see *[Bertrand Russell's political views](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bertrand_Russell's_political_views "Bertrand Russell's political views")*) in 1918 (he was prosecuted under the [Defence of the Realm Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Defence_of_the_Realm_Act "Defence of the Realm Act")). He later said of his imprisonment:

> I found prison in many ways quite agreeable. I had no engagements, no difficult decisions to make, no fear of callers, no interruptions to my work. I read enormously; I wrote a book, "Introduction to Mathematical Philosophy"... and began the work for "[The Analysis of Mind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Analysis_of_Mind "The Analysis of Mind")". I was rather interested in my fellow-prisoners, who seemed to me in no way morally inferior to the rest of the population, though they were on the whole slightly below the usual level of intelligence as was shown by their having been caught.

While he was reading [Strachey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lytton_Strachey "Lytton Strachey")'s *[Eminent Victorians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eminent_Victorians "Eminent Victorians")* chapter about [Gordon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_George_Gordon "Charles George Gordon") he laughed out loud in his cell prompting the warder to intervene and reminding him that "prison was a place of punishment".

Russell was reinstated to Trinity in 1919, resigned in 1920, was Tarner Lecturer in 1926 and became a Fellow again in 1944 until 1949.

In 1924, Russell again gained press attention when attending a "banquet" in the [House of Commons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Commons_of_the_United_Kingdom "House of Commons of the United Kingdom") with well-known campaigners, including [Arnold Lupton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arnold_Lupton "Arnold Lupton"), who had been an [MP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Member_of_Parliament_(United_Kingdom) "Member of Parliament (United Kingdom)") and had also endured imprisonment for "passive resistance to military or naval service".

### G. H. Hardy on the Trinity controversy

In 1941, [G. H. Hardy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G._H._Hardy "G. H. Hardy") wrote a 61-page pamphlet titled *Bertrand Russell and Trinity* (published later as a book by [Cambridge University Press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_University_Press "Cambridge University Press") with a foreword by [C. D. Broad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C._D._Broad "C. D. Broad")) in which he gave an authoritative account of Russell's 1916 dismissal from Trinity College, explaining that a reconciliation between the college and Russell had later taken place and gave details about Russell's personal life. Hardy writes that Russell's dismissal had created a scandal since the vast majority of the Fellows of the College opposed the decision. The ensuing pressure from the Fellows induced the Council to reinstate Russell. In January 1920, it was announced that Russell had accepted the reinstatement offer from Trinity and would begin lecturing in October. In July 1920, Russell applied for a one-year leave of absence; this was approved. He spent the year giving lectures in China and Japan. In January 1921, it was announced by Trinity that Russell had resigned and his resignation had been accepted. This resignation, Hardy explains, was voluntary and was not the result of another altercation.

The reason for the resignation, according to Hardy, was that Russell was going through a tumultuous time in his personal life with a divorce and subsequent remarriage. Russell contemplated asking Trinity for another one-year leave of absence but decided against it since this would have been an "unusual application" and the situation had the potential to snowball into another controversy. Although Russell did the right thing, in Hardy's opinion, the reputation of the College suffered with Russell's resignation since the 'world of learning' knew about Russell's altercation with Trinity but not that the rift had healed. In 1925, Russell was asked by the Council of Trinity College to give the *Tarner Lectures* on the Philosophy of the Sciences; these would later be the basis for one of Russell's best-received books according to Hardy: *The Analysis of Matter*, published in 1927. In the preface to the Trinity pamphlet, Hardy wrote:

> I wish to make it plain that Russell himself is not responsible, directly or indirectly, for the writing of the pamphlet.... I wrote it without his knowledge and, when I sent him the typescript and asked for his permission to print it, I suggested that, unless it contained misstatement of fact, he should make no comment on it. He agreed to this... no word has been changed as the result of any suggestion from him.

### Between the wars

In August 1920, Russell travelled to [Soviet Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Soviet_Federative_Socialist_Republic "Russian Soviet Federative Socialist Republic") as part of an official delegation sent by the British government to investigate the effects of the [Russian Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Revolution_of_1917 "Russian Revolution of 1917"). He wrote a four-part series of articles, titled "Soviet Russia—1920", for the magazine *[The Nation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Nation "The Nation")*. He met [Vladimir Lenin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladimir_Lenin "Vladimir Lenin") and had an hour-long conversation with him. In his autobiography, he mentions that he found Lenin disappointing, sensing an "impish cruelty" in him and comparing him to "an opinionated professor". He cruised down the [Volga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volga "Volga") on a steamship. His experiences destroyed his previous tentative support for the revolution. He subsequently wrote a book, *The Practice and Theory of Bolshevism*, about his experiences on this trip,

> The Russian authorities only admitted me on the express condition that I should travel with the British Labour Delegation ... I, not being a member of the Delegation, felt less obligation than my companions did to attend at propaganda meetings where one knew the speeches by heart beforehand. In this way, I was able, by the help of neutral interpreters, mostly English or American, to have many conversations with casual people whom I met in the streets or on village greens, and to find out how the whole system appears to the ordinary non-political man and woman.

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d9/Russell_with_John_and_Kate.jpg/250px-Russell_with_John_and_Kate.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Russell_with_John_and_Kate.jpg)

Russell with his children, [John](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Russell,_4th_Earl_Russell "John Russell, 4th Earl Russell") and [Kate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lady_Katharine_Tait "Lady Katharine Tait")

Russell's lover [Dora Black](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dora_Russell "Dora Russell"), a British author, [feminist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feminist "Feminist") and socialist campaigner, visited Soviet Russia independently at the same time; in contrast to his reaction, she was enthusiastic about the [Bolshevik revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/October_Revolution "October Revolution").

The following year, Russell, accompanied by Dora, visited [Peking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peking "Peking") ([Beijing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beijing "Beijing")) to lecture on philosophy for a year. He went with optimism and hope, seeing China as [then being](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Republic_of_China#Fight_against_warlordism_and_the_First_United_Front "History of the Republic of China") on a new path. Other scholars present in China at the time included [John Dewey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Dewey "John Dewey") and [Rabindranath Tagore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rabindranath_Tagore "Rabindranath Tagore"), the Indian Nobel-laureate poet. Before leaving China, Russell became gravely ill with [pneumonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pneumonia "Pneumonia"), and [incorrect reports](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_premature_obituaries "List of premature obituaries") of his death were published in the Japanese press. When the couple visited Japan on their return journey, Dora took on the role of spurning the local press by handing out notices reading "Mr. Bertrand Russell, having died according to the Japanese press, is unable to give interviews to Japanese journalists". Apparently they found this harsh and reacted resentfully. Russell supported his family during this time by writing popular books explaining matters of [physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics "Physics"), ethics, and education to the layman.

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b8/Bertrand_Russell_in_1924.jpg/120px-Bertrand_Russell_in_1924.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Bertrand_Russell_in_1924.jpg)

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4c/Russell_in_1924_01.jpg/120px-Russell_in_1924_01.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Russell_in_1924_01.jpg)

Bertrand Russell in 1924

From 1922 to 1927, the Russells divided their time between London and [Cornwall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornwall "Cornwall"), spending summers in [Porthcurno](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porthcurno "Porthcurno"). In the [1922](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1922_United_Kingdom_general_election "1922 United Kingdom general election") and [1923 general elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1923_United_Kingdom_general_election "1923 United Kingdom general election") Russell stood as a [Labour Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labour_Party_(UK) "Labour Party (UK)") candidate in the [Chelsea constituency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chelsea_(UK_Parliament_constituency) "Chelsea (UK Parliament constituency)"), but only on the basis that he knew he was unlikely to be elected in such a safe Conservative seat, and he was unsuccessful on both occasions.

After the birth of his two children, he became interested in education, especially [early childhood education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_childhood_education "Early childhood education"). He was not satisfied with the old [traditional education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_education "Traditional education") and thought that [progressive education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Progressive_education "Progressive education") also had some flaws; as a result, together with Dora, Russell founded the experimental Beacon Hill School in 1927. The school was run from a succession of different locations, including its original premises at the Russells' residence, Telegraph House, near [Harting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harting "Harting"), West Sussex. During this time, he published *On Education, Especially in Early Childhood*. On 8 July 1930, Dora gave birth to her third child Harriet Ruth. After he left the school in 1932, Dora continued it until 1943.

In 1927, Russell met [Barry Fox (later Barry Stevens)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barry_Stevens_(therapist) "Barry Stevens (therapist)"), who became a known [Gestalt therapist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gestalt_therapy "Gestalt therapy") and writer in her later years. They developed an intense relationship, and in Fox's words: "... for three years we were very close." Fox sent her daughter Judith to Beacon Hill School. From 1927 to 1932 Russell wrote 34 letters to Fox. Upon the death of his elder brother Frank, in 1931, Russell became the 3rd [Earl Russell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earl_Russell "Earl Russell").

Russell's marriage to Dora grew tenuous, and it reached a breaking point over her having two children with an American journalist, Griffin Barry. They separated in 1932 and finally divorced. On 18 January 1936, Russell married his third wife, an [Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Oxford "University of Oxford") undergraduate named [Patricia ("Peter") Spence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patricia_Russell_(nee_Spence) "Patricia Russell (nee Spence)"), who had been his children's governess since 1930. Russell and Peter had one son, [Conrad Sebastian Robert Russell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conrad_Russell,_5th_Earl_Russell "Conrad Russell, 5th Earl Russell"), 5th Earl Russell, who became a historian and one of the leading figures in the [Liberal Democrat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberal_Democrats_(UK) "Liberal Democrats (UK)") party.

In 1937, Russell returned to the [London School of Economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_School_of_Economics "London School of Economics") to lecture on the science of power. During the 1930s, Russell became a friend and collaborator of [V. K. Krishna Menon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V._K._Krishna_Menon "V. K. Krishna Menon"), then President of the [India League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/India_League "India League"), the foremost lobby in the United Kingdom for Indian independence. Russell chaired the India League from 1932 to 1939.

### Second World War

[Russell's political views](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bertrand_Russell's_political_views "Bertrand Russell's political views") changed over time, mostly about war. He opposed rearmament against [Nazi Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi_Germany "Nazi Germany"). In 1937, he wrote in a personal letter: "If the Germans succeed in sending an invading army to England we should do best to treat them as visitors, give them quarters and invite the commander and chief to dine with the prime minister." In 1940, he changed his [appeasement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appeasement "Appeasement") view that avoiding a full-scale world war was more important than defeating Hitler. He concluded that Adolf Hitler taking over all of Europe would be a permanent threat to democracy. In 1943, he adopted a stance toward large-scale warfare called "relative political pacifism": "War was always a great evil, but in some particularly extreme circumstances, it may be the lesser of two evils."

Before World War II, Russell taught at the [University of Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Chicago "University of Chicago"), later moving on to Los Angeles to lecture at the [UCLA Department of Philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UCLA_Department_of_Philosophy "UCLA Department of Philosophy"). He was appointed professor at the [City College of New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_College_of_New_York "City College of New York") (CCNY) in 1940, but after a public outcry the appointment was annulled by a court judgment that pronounced him "morally unfit" to teach at the college because of his opinions, especially those relating to [sexual morality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sexual_morality "Sexual morality"), detailed in *[Marriage and Morals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marriage_and_Morals "Marriage and Morals")* (1929). The matter was taken to the [New York Supreme Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_Supreme_Court "New York Supreme Court") by [Jean Kay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Kay?action=edit&redlink=1 "Jean Kay") who was afraid that her daughter would be harmed by the appointment, though her daughter was not a student at CCNY. Many intellectuals, led by [John Dewey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Dewey "John Dewey"), protested at his treatment. [Albert Einstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Einstein "Albert Einstein")'s oft-quoted aphorism that "great spirits have always encountered violent opposition from mediocre minds" originated in his open letter, dated 19 March 1940, to [Morris Raphael Cohen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morris_Raphael_Cohen "Morris Raphael Cohen"), a professor emeritus at CCNY, supporting Russell's appointment. Dewey and [Horace M. Kallen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horace_M._Kallen "Horace M. Kallen") edited a collection of articles on the CCNY affair in *[The Bertrand Russell Case](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Bertrand_Russell_Case "The Bertrand Russell Case")*. Russell soon joined the [Barnes Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnes_Foundation "Barnes Foundation"), lecturing to a varied audience on the [history of philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_philosophy "History of philosophy"); these lectures formed the basis of *[A History of Western Philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_History_of_Western_Philosophy "A History of Western Philosophy")*. His relationship with the eccentric [Albert C. Barnes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_C._Barnes "Albert C. Barnes") soon soured, and he returned to the UK in 1944 to rejoin the faculty of Trinity College.

### Later life

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/1a/Bertrand_Russell_1954.jpg/250px-Bertrand_Russell_1954.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Bertrand_Russell_1954.jpg)

Russell in 1954

Russell participated in many broadcasts over the [BBC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC "BBC"), particularly *[The Brains Trust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Brains_Trust "The Brains Trust")* and for the [Third Programme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Third_Programme "BBC Third Programme"), on various topical and philosophical subjects. By this time Russell was known outside academic circles, frequently the subject or author of magazine and newspaper articles, and was called upon to offer opinions on a variety of subjects, even mundane ones. En route to one of his lectures in [Trondheim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trondheim "Trondheim"), Russell was one of 24 survivors (out of 43 passengers) of an [aeroplane crash in Hommelvik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bukken_Bruse_disaster "Bukken Bruse disaster") in October 1948. He said he owed his life to smoking since the people who drowned were in the non-smoking part of the plane. *[A History of Western Philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_History_of_Western_Philosophy "A History of Western Philosophy")* (1945) became a best-seller and provided Russell with a steady income for the remainder of his life.

In 1942, Russell argued in favour of a moderate [socialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialism "Socialism"), capable of overcoming its metaphysical principles. In an inquiry on [dialectical materialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dialectical_materialism "Dialectical materialism"), launched by the Austrian artist and philosopher [Wolfgang Paalen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolfgang_Paalen "Wolfgang Paalen") in his journal *[DYN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DYN_(magazine) "DYN (magazine)")*, Russell said: "I think the metaphysics of both [Hegel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georg_Wilhelm_Friedrich_Hegel "Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel") and [Marx](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Marx "Karl Marx") plain nonsense—Marx's claim to be 'science' is no more justified than [Mary Baker Eddy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Baker_Eddy "Mary Baker Eddy")'s. This does not mean that I am opposed to socialism."

In 1943, Russell expressed support for [Zionism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zionism "Zionism"): "I have come gradually to see that, in a dangerous and largely hostile world, it is essential to Jews to have some country which is theirs, some region where they are not suspected aliens, some state which embodies what is distinctive in their culture".

In a speech in 1948, Russell said that if the [USSR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USSR "USSR")'s aggression continued, it would be morally worse to go to war after the USSR possessed an [atomic bomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_weapon "Nuclear weapon") than before it possessed one, because if the USSR had no bomb the West's victory would come more swiftly and with fewer casualties than if there were atomic bombs on both sides. At that time, only the United States possessed an atomic bomb, and the USSR was pursuing an aggressive policy towards the countries in Eastern Europe which were being absorbed into the Soviet Union's [sphere of influence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sphere_of_influence "Sphere of influence"). Many understood Russell's comments to mean that Russell approved of a [first strike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-emptive_nuclear_strike "Pre-emptive nuclear strike") in a war with the USSR, including [Nigel Lawson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nigel_Lawson "Nigel Lawson"), who was present when Russell spoke of such matters. Others, including [Griffin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicholas_Griffin_(philosopher) "Nicholas Griffin (philosopher)"), who obtained a transcript of the speech, have argued that he was explaining the usefulness of America's atomic arsenal in deterring the USSR from continuing its domination of Eastern Europe.

Just after the [atomic bombs exploded over Hiroshima and Nagasaki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_bombings_of_Hiroshima_and_Nagasaki "Atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki"), Russell wrote letters, and published articles in newspapers from 1945 to 1948, stating clearly that it was morally justified and better to go to war against the USSR using atomic bombs while the United States possessed them and before the USSR did. In September 1949, one week after the USSR tested its first A-bomb, but before this became known, Russell wrote that the USSR would be unable to develop nuclear weapons because following Stalin's purges only science based on Marxist principles would be practised in the Soviet Union. After it became known that the USSR had carried out [its nuclear bomb tests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_nuclear_weapons_tests_of_the_Soviet_Union "List of nuclear weapons tests of the Soviet Union"), Russell declared his position advocating the total abolition of atomic weapons.

In 1948, Russell was invited by the BBC to deliver the inaugural [Reith Lectures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reith_Lectures "Reith Lectures")—what was to become an annual series of lectures, still broadcast by the BBC. His series of six broadcasts, titled *Authority and the Individual*, explored themes such as the role of individual initiative in the development of a community and the role of state control in a progressive society. Russell continued to write about philosophy. He wrote a foreword to *Words and Things* by [Ernest Gellner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_Gellner "Ernest Gellner"), which was highly critical of the [later thought](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophical_Investigations "Philosophical Investigations") of [Ludwig Wittgenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludwig_Wittgenstein "Ludwig Wittgenstein") and of [ordinary language philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordinary_language_philosophy "Ordinary language philosophy"). [Gilbert Ryle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gilbert_Ryle "Gilbert Ryle") refused to have the book reviewed in the philosophical journal *[Mind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mind_(journal) "Mind (journal)")*, which caused Russell to respond via *[The Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Times "The Times")*. The result was a month-long correspondence in *The Times* between the supporters and detractors of ordinary language philosophy, which was ended when the paper published an editorial critical of both sides but agreeing with the opponents of ordinary language philosophy.

In the King's [Birthday Honours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birthday_Honours "Birthday Honours") of 9 June 1949, Russell was awarded the [Order of Merit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_Merit_(Commonwealth) "Order of Merit (Commonwealth)"), and the following year he was awarded the [Nobel Prize in Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Literature "Nobel Prize in Literature"). When he was given the Order of Merit, [George VI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_VI "George VI") was affable but embarrassed at decorating a former jailbird, saying, "You have sometimes behaved in a manner that would not do if generally adopted". Russell merely smiled, but afterwards claimed that the reply "That's right, just like your [brother](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_VIII "Edward VIII")" immediately came to mind.

In 1950, Russell attended the inaugural conference for the [Congress for Cultural Freedom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congress_for_Cultural_Freedom "Congress for Cultural Freedom"), a [CIA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CIA "CIA")-funded anti-communist organisation committed to the deployment of culture as a weapon during the [Cold War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cold_War "Cold War"). Russell was one of the known patrons of the Congress until he resigned in 1956.

In 1952, Russell was divorced by Spence, with whom he had been very unhappy. Conrad, Russell's son by Spence, did not see his father between the time of the divorce and 1968 (at which time his decision to meet his father caused a permanent breach with his mother). Russell married his fourth wife, [Edith Finch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edith_Finch_Russell "Edith Finch Russell"), soon after the divorce, on 15 December 1952. They had known each other since 1925, and Edith had taught English at [Bryn Mawr College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bryn_Mawr_College "Bryn Mawr College") near Philadelphia, sharing a house for 20 years with Russell's old friend [Lucy Donnelly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucy_Donnelly "Lucy Donnelly"). Edith remained with him until his death, and, by all accounts, their marriage was a happy, close, and loving one. Russell's eldest son John suffered from [mental illness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mental_illness "Mental illness"), which was the source of ongoing disputes between Russell and his former wife Dora.

In 1962 Russell played a public role in the [Cuban Missile Crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuban_Missile_Crisis#Crisis_deepens "Cuban Missile Crisis"): in an exchange of telegrams with Soviet leader [Nikita Khrushchev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nikita_Khrushchev "Nikita Khrushchev"), Khrushchev assured him that the Soviet government would not be reckless. Russell sent this telegram to [President Kennedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_F._Kennedy "John F. Kennedy"):

> YOUR ACTION DESPERATE. THREAT TO HUMAN SURVIVAL. NO CONCEIVABLE JUSTIFICATION. CIVILIZED MAN CONDEMNS IT. WE WILL NOT HAVE MASS MURDER. ULTIMATUM MEANS WAR... END THIS MADNESS.

According to historian Peter Knight, after JFK's [assassination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_F._Kennedy_assassination "John F. Kennedy assassination"), Russell, "prompted by the emerging work of the lawyer [Mark Lane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Lane_(author) "Mark Lane (author)") in the US ... rallied support from other noteworthy and left-leaning compatriots to form a 'Who Killed Kennedy committee' in June 1964, members of which included [Michael Foot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Foot "Michael Foot") MP, [Caroline Benn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caroline_Benn "Caroline Benn")
(wife of [Tony Benn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tony_Benn "Tony Benn") MP), the publisher [Victor Gollancz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victor_Gollancz "Victor Gollancz"), the writers [John Arden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Arden "John Arden") and [J. B. Priestley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._B._Priestley "J. B. Priestley"), and the Oxford history professor [Hugh Trevor-Roper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugh_Trevor-Roper "Hugh Trevor-Roper")." Russell published a highly critical article in *[The Minority of One](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M._S._Arnoni "M. S. Arnoni")* weeks before the [Warren Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warren_Commission "Warren Commission") report was published, setting forth *16 Questions on the Assassination.* Russell equated the [Oswald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lee_Harvey_Oswald "Lee Harvey Oswald") case with the [Dreyfus affair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dreyfus_affair "Dreyfus affair") of late 19th-century France, in which the state convicted an innocent man. Russell also criticised the American press for failing to heed any voices critical of the official version.

### Political causes

Bertrand Russell was opposed to war from a young age; his opposition to World War I was used as grounds for his dismissal from Trinity College at Cambridge. This incident fused two of his controversial causes, as he had failed to be granted fellow status which would have protected him from firing, because he was not willing to either pretend to be a devout Christian, or at least avoid admitting he was agnostic.

He later described the resolution of these issues as essential to freedom of thought and expression, citing the incident in [Free Thought and Official Propaganda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_Thought_and_Official_Propaganda "Free Thought and Official Propaganda"), where he explained that the expression of any idea, even the most obviously "bad", must be protected not only from direct State intervention but also economic leveraging and other means of being silenced:

> The opinions which are still persecuted strike the majority as so monstrous and immoral that the general principle of toleration cannot be held to apply to them. But this is exactly the same view as that which made possible the tortures of the [Inquisition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_Inquisition "Spanish Inquisition").

Russell spent the 1950s and 1960s engaged in political causes primarily related to [nuclear disarmament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_disarmament "Nuclear disarmament") and opposing the [Vietnam War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnam_War "Vietnam War"). The 1955 [Russell–Einstein Manifesto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russell–Einstein_Manifesto "Russell–Einstein Manifesto") was a document calling for nuclear disarmament and was signed by eleven of the most prominent nuclear physicists and intellectuals of the time. In October 1960 "[The Committee of 100](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Committee_of_100_(United_Kingdom) "Committee of 100 (United Kingdom)")" was formed with a declaration by Russell and [Michael Scott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Scott_(priest) "Michael Scott (priest)"), entitled "Act or Perish", which called for a "movement of nonviolent resistance to nuclear war and weapons of mass destruction". In September 1961, at the age of 89, Russell was jailed for seven days in [Brixton Prison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HM_Prison_Brixton "HM Prison Brixton") for a "breach of the peace" after taking part in [an anti-nuclear demonstration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Committee_of_100_(United_Kingdom)#1961 "Committee of 100 (United Kingdom)") in London. The magistrate offered to exempt him from jail if he pledged himself to "good behaviour", to which Russell replied: "No, I won't."

From 1966 to 1967, Russell worked with [Jean-Paul Sartre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Paul_Sartre "Jean-Paul Sartre") and many other intellectual figures to form the [Russell Vietnam War Crimes Tribunal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russell_Tribunal "Russell Tribunal") to investigate the conduct of the United States in Vietnam. He wrote many letters to world leaders during this period.

Early in his life, Russell supported [eugenicist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugenics "Eugenics") policies. In 1894, he proposed that the state issue certificates of health to prospective parents and withhold public benefits from those considered unfit. In 1929, he wrote that people deemed "mentally defective" and "feebleminded" should be sexually sterilised because they "are apt to have enormous numbers of illegitimate children, all, as a rule, wholly useless to the community." Russell was also an advocate of [population control](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_control "Population control"):

> The nations which at present increase rapidly should be encouraged to adopt the methods by which, in the West, the increase of population has been checked. Educational propaganda, with government help, could achieve this result in a generation. There are, however, two powerful forces opposed to such a policy: one is religion, the other is nationalism. I think it is the duty of all to proclaim that opposition to the spread of birth is appalling depth of misery and degradation, and that within another fifty years or so. I do not pretend that [birth control](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_control "Birth control") is the only way in which population can be kept from increasing. There are others, which, one must suppose, opponents of birth control would prefer. War, as I remarked a moment ago, has hitherto been disappointing in this respect, but perhaps bacteriological war may prove more effective. If a [Black Death](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Death "Black Death") could be spread throughout the whole world once in every generation survivors could procreate freely without making the world too full. There would be nothing in this to offend the consciences of the devout or to restrain the ambitions of nationalists. The state of affairs might be somewhat unpleasant, but what of that? Really high-minded people are indifferent to happiness, especially other people's.

— Bertrand Russell, *The Impact of Science on Society*

On 20 November 1948, in a public speech at [Westminster School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westminster_School "Westminster School"), addressing a gathering arranged by the New Commonwealth, Russell shocked some observers by suggesting that a preemptive nuclear strike on the [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union "Soviet Union") was justified. Russell argued that war between the United States and the Soviet Union seemed inevitable, so it would be a humanitarian gesture to get it over with quickly and have the United States in the dominant position. Currently, Russell argued, humanity could survive such a war, whereas a full [nuclear war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_warfare "Nuclear warfare") after both sides had manufactured large stockpiles of more destructive weapons was likely to result in the [extinction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extinction "Extinction") of the [human race](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human "Human"). Russell later relented from this stance, instead arguing for mutual disarmament by the nuclear powers.

In 1956, before and during the [Suez Crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suez_Crisis "Suez Crisis"), Russell expressed his opposition to European imperialism in the Middle East. He viewed the crisis as another reminder of the pressing need for an effective mechanism for international governance, and to restrict national sovereignty in places such as the [Suez Canal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suez_Canal "Suez Canal") area "where general interest is involved". At the same time the Suez Crisis was taking place, the world was also captivated by the [Hungarian Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungarian_Revolution_of_1956 "Hungarian Revolution of 1956") and the subsequent crushing of the revolt by intervening Soviet forces. Russell attracted criticism for speaking out fervently against the Suez war while ignoring Soviet repression in Hungary, to which he responded that he did not criticise the Soviets "because there was no need. Most of the so-called Western World was fulminating". Although he later feigned a lack of concern, at the time he was disgusted by the brutal Soviet response, and on 16 November 1956, he expressed approval for a declaration of support for Hungarian scholars which [Michael Polanyi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Polanyi "Michael Polanyi") had cabled to the Soviet embassy in London twelve days previously, shortly after Soviet troops had entered [Budapest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Budapest "Budapest").

In November 1957 Russell wrote an article addressing US President [Dwight D. Eisenhower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dwight_D._Eisenhower "Dwight D. Eisenhower") and Soviet Premier [Nikita Khrushchev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nikita_Khrushchev "Nikita Khrushchev"), urging a summit to consider "the conditions of co-existence". Khrushchev responded that peace could be served by such a meeting. In January 1958 Russell elaborated his views in *[The Observer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Observer "The Observer")*, proposing a cessation of all nuclear weapons production, with the UK taking the first step by unilaterally suspending its own nuclear weapons programme if necessary, and with Germany "freed from all alien armed forces and pledged to neutrality in any conflict between East and West". US Secretary of State [John Foster Dulles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Foster_Dulles "John Foster Dulles") replied for Eisenhower. The exchange of letters was published as *The Vital Letters of Russell, Khrushchev, and Dulles*.

Russell was asked by *[The New Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_Republic "The New Republic")*, a liberal American magazine, to elaborate his views on world peace. He urged that all nuclear weapons testing and flights by planes armed with nuclear weapons be halted immediately, and negotiations be opened for the destruction of all [hydrogen bombs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_bombs "Hydrogen bombs"), with the number of conventional nuclear devices limited to ensure a balance of power. He proposed that Germany be reunified and accept the [Oder-Neisse line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oder-Neisse_line "Oder-Neisse line") as its border, and that a neutral zone be established in Central Europe, consisting at the minimum of Germany, Poland, Hungary, and [Czechoslovakia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Czechoslovakia "Czechoslovakia"), with each of these countries being free of foreign troops and influence, and prohibited from forming alliances with countries outside the zone. In the Middle East, Russell suggested that the West avoid opposing [Arab nationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_nationalism "Arab nationalism"), and proposed the creation of a United Nations peacekeeping force to guard Israel's frontiers to ensure that Israel was prevented from committing aggression and protected from it. He also suggested Western recognition of the [People's Republic of China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/People's_Republic_of_China "People's Republic of China"), and that it be admitted to the UN with a permanent seat on the [UN Security Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UN_Security_Council "UN Security Council").

He was in contact with [Lionel Rogosin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lionel_Rogosin "Lionel Rogosin") while the latter was filming his anti-war film *[Good Times, Wonderful Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Good_Times,_Wonderful_Times "Good Times, Wonderful Times")* in the 1960s. He became a hero to many of the youthful members of the [New Left](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Left "New Left"). In early 1963, Russell became increasingly vocal in his disapproval of the Vietnam War, and felt that the US government's policies there were near-[genocidal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genocidal "Genocidal"). In 1963, he became the inaugural recipient of the [Jerusalem Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerusalem_Prize "Jerusalem Prize"), an award for writers concerned with the freedom of the individual in society. In 1964, he was one of eleven world figures who issued an appeal to Israel and the [Arab countries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_world "Arab world") to accept an [arms embargo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arms_embargo "Arms embargo") and international supervision of [nuclear plants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_plant "Nuclear plant") and rocket weaponry. In October 1965, he tore up his [Labour Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labour_Party_(UK) "Labour Party (UK)") card because he suspected [Harold Wilson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Wilson "Harold Wilson")'s Labour government was going to send troops to support the United States in Vietnam.

### Final years, death and legacy

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/48/Plas_Penrhyn_%28geograph_6365767%29.jpg/250px-Plas_Penrhyn_%28geograph_6365767%29.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Plas_Penrhyn_(geograph_6365767).jpg)

Plas Penrhyn in [Penrhyndeudraeth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penrhyndeudraeth "Penrhyndeudraeth")

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/9d/Bertrand_Russell_1972_stamp_of_India.jpg/250px-Bertrand_Russell_1972_stamp_of_India.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Bertrand_Russell_1972_stamp_of_India.jpg)

Russell on a 1972 stamp of India

In June 1955, Russell had leased Plas Penrhyn in [Penrhyndeudraeth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penrhyndeudraeth "Penrhyndeudraeth"), Merionethshire, Wales and on 5 July of the following year it became his and Edith's principal residence.

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/ff/Bust_Of_Bertrand_Russell-Red_Lion_Square-London.jpg/250px-Bust_Of_Bertrand_Russell-Red_Lion_Square-London.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Bust_Of_Bertrand_Russell-Red_Lion_Square-London.jpg)

Bust of Russell in [Red Lion Square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Lion_Square "Red Lion Square")

Russell published his three-volume autobiography in 1967, 1968, and 1969. He made a [cameo appearance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cameo_appearance "Cameo appearance") playing himself in the anti-war [Hindi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindi "Hindi") film [*Aman*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aman_(film) "Aman (film)"), by [Mohan Kumar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mohan_Kumar_(director) "Mohan Kumar (director)"), which was released in India in 1967. This was Russell's only appearance in a feature film.

On 23 November 1969, he wrote to *The Times* newspaper saying that the preparation for show trials in Czechoslovakia was "highly alarming". The same month, he appealed to Secretary General [U Thant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U_Thant "U Thant") of the United Nations to support an international war crimes commission to investigate alleged torture and genocide by the United States in [South Vietnam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Vietnam "South Vietnam") during the Vietnam War. The following month, he protested to [Alexei Kosygin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexei_Kosygin "Alexei Kosygin") over the expulsion of [Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aleksandr_Solzhenitsyn "Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn") from the [Soviet Union of Writers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union_of_Writers "Soviet Union of Writers").

On 31 January 1970, Russell issued a statement condemning "Israel's aggression in the Middle East", and in particular, Israeli bombing raids being carried out deep in Egyptian territory as part of the [War of Attrition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_of_Attrition "War of Attrition"), which he compared to German bombing raids in the [Battle of Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Britain "Battle of Britain") and the US bombing of Vietnam. He called for an Israeli withdrawal to the pre-[Six-Day War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Six-Day_War "Six-Day War") borders, stating "The aggression committed by Israel must be condemned, not only because no state has the right to annexe foreign territory, but because every expansion is an experiment to discover how much more aggression the world will tolerate." This was Russell's final political statement or act. It was read out at the International Conference of Parliamentarians in [Cairo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cairo "Cairo") on 3 February 1970, the day after his death.

Russell died of [influenza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Influenza "Influenza"), just after 8 pm on 2 February 1970 at his home in Penrhyndeudraeth, aged 97. His body was cremated in [Colwyn Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colwyn_Bay "Colwyn Bay") on 5 February 1970 with five people present. In accordance with his will, there was no religious ceremony but one minute's silence; his ashes were later scattered over the Welsh mountains. Although he was born in [Monmouthshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monmouthshire_(historic) "Monmouthshire (historic)"), and died in [Penrhyndeudraeth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penrhyndeudraeth "Penrhyndeudraeth") in Wales, Russell identified as English. Later in 1970, on 23 October, his will was published showing he had left an estate valued at £69,423 (equivalent to £1 million in 2025). In 1980, a memorial to Russell was commissioned by a committee including the philosopher [A. J. Ayer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A._J._Ayer "A. J. Ayer"). It consists of a bust of Russell in [Red Lion Square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Lion_Square "Red Lion Square") in London sculpted by Marcelle Quinton.

Lady Katharine Jane Tait, Russell's daughter, founded the Bertrand Russell Society in 1974 to preserve and understand his work. It publishes the *Bertrand Russell Society Bulletin*, holds meetings and awards prizes for scholarship, including the Bertrand Russell Society Award. She also authored several essays about her father; as well as a book, *My Father, Bertrand Russell*, which was published in 1975. All members receive *Russell: The Journal of Bertrand Russell Studies*.

For the [sesquicentennial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sesquicentennial "Sesquicentennial") of his birth, in May 2022, [McMaster University's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McMaster_University's "McMaster University's") Bertrand Russell Archive, the university's largest and most heavily used research collection, organised both a physical and virtual exhibition on Russell's anti-nuclear stance in the post-war era, [*Scientists* *for Peace: the Russell-Einstein Manifesto and the Pugwash Conference*](https://expo.mcmaster.ca/s/scientists-for-peace/page/scientists-for-peace-introduction), which included the earliest version of the [Russell–Einstein Manifesto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russell–Einstein_Manifesto "Russell–Einstein Manifesto"). The [Bertrand Russell Peace Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bertrand_Russell_Peace_Foundation "Bertrand Russell Peace Foundation") held a commemoration at [Conway Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conway_Hall "Conway Hall") in Red Lion Square, London, on 18 May, the anniversary of his birth.

Bangladesh's first leader, [Mujibur Rahman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mujibur_Rahman "Mujibur Rahman"), named his youngest son [Sheikh Russel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheikh_Russel "Sheikh Russel") in honour of Bertrand Russell.

## Marriages and issue

In 1889, Russell, aged 17, met the family of [Alys Pearsall Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alys_Pearsall_Smith "Alys Pearsall Smith"), an American [Quaker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_Society_of_Friends "Religious Society of Friends") five years his senior and a graduate of [Bryn Mawr College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bryn_Mawr_College "Bryn Mawr College") near [Philadelphia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philadelphia "Philadelphia"). He became a friend of the Pearsall Smith family. They knew him as "Lord John's grandson" and enjoyed showing him off.

He fell in love with Alys, and contrary to his grandmother's wishes, married her on 13 December 1894. Their marriage began to fall apart in 1901 when it occurred to Russell, while cycling, that he no longer loved her. She asked him if he loved her, and he factually replied that he did not. Russell also disliked Alys's mother, finding her controlling and cruel. A lengthy period of separation began in 1911 with Russell's affair with [Lady Ottoline Morrell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lady_Ottoline_Morrell "Lady Ottoline Morrell"), and he and Alys finally divorced in 1921 to enable Russell to remarry. During his years of separation from Alys, Russell had affairs (often simultaneous) with a number of women, including Morrell and the actress [Lady Constance Malleson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lady_Constance_Malleson "Lady Constance Malleson"). Some have suggested that at this point he had an affair with [Vivienne Haigh-Wood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vivienne_Haigh-Wood "Vivienne Haigh-Wood"), the English governess and writer, and first wife of [T. S. Eliot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T._S._Eliot "T. S. Eliot").

In 1921, his second marriage was to [Dora Winifred Black](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dora_Russell "Dora Russell") (died 1986), daughter of Sir Frederick Black. Dora was six months pregnant when the couple returned to England. The marriage was dissolved in 1935, having produced two children:

- [John Conrad Russell, 4th Earl Russell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Conrad_Russell,_4th_Earl_Russell "John Conrad Russell, 4th Earl Russell") (1921–1987)
- Lady Katharine Jane Russell (1923–2021), who married Rev. Charles Tait in 1948 and [had issue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Issue_(genealogy) "Issue (genealogy)")

Additionally, Russell was temporarily registered as the birth father of Dora Russell's daughter, Harriett Ruth Barry (1930-2024), but this was reversed at some point and Harriett's father was correctly registered as American journalist Griffin Barry. Dora Russell also gave birth to a son by Griffin Barry in 1932, Roderick Barry (1932-1983). Though the Russell marriage was an open one, the Barry children's births led to a dissolution of the marriage and Russell divorced Dora. Russell did continue to be involved in both Barry children's lives, though not closely.

Russell's third marriage was to his student [Patricia Helen Spence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patricia_Russell "Patricia Russell") (d. 2004) in 1936, with the marriage producing one child:

- [Conrad Sebastian Robert Russell, 5th Earl Russell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conrad_Sebastian_Robert_Russell,_5th_Earl_Russell "Conrad Sebastian Robert Russell, 5th Earl Russell") (1937–2004), who became a historian and one of the leading figures in the [Liberal Democrat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberal_Democrats_(UK) "Liberal Democrats (UK)") party.

Russell's third marriage ended in divorce in 1952. He married Edith Finch in the same year. They remained married at the time of his death in 1970; Finch died in 1978.

## Titles, awards and honours

Upon his brother's death in 1931, Russell became the 3rd [Earl Russell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earl_Russell "Earl Russell") of [Kingston Russell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingston_Russell "Kingston Russell"), and the subsidiary title of Viscount Amberley of Amberley and of Ardsalla. He held both titles, and the accompanying seat in the House of Lords, until his death in 1970.

### Philosophy

Russell is credited with being one of the founders of [analytic philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytic_philosophy "Analytic philosophy"). He was impressed by [Gottfried Leibniz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gottfried_Leibniz "Gottfried Leibniz") (1646–1716), and wrote on major areas of philosophy except [aesthetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aesthetics "Aesthetics"). He was prolific in the fields of [metaphysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphysics "Metaphysics"), [logic and the philosophy of mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bertrand_Russell's_views_on_philosophy#Logic_and_philosophy_of_mathematics "Bertrand Russell's views on philosophy"), the [philosophy of language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bertrand_Russell's_views_on_philosophy#Philosophy_of_language "Bertrand Russell's views on philosophy"), [ethics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bertrand_Russell's_views_on_philosophy#Ethics "Bertrand Russell's views on philosophy") and [epistemology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bertrand_Russell's_views_on_philosophy#Epistemology "Bertrand Russell's views on philosophy"). When [Brand Blanshard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brand_Blanshard "Brand Blanshard") asked Russell why he did not write on aesthetics, Russell replied that he did not know anything about it, though he hastened to add "but that is not a very good excuse, for my friends tell me it has not deterred me from writing on other subjects".

On ethics, Russell wrote that he was a [utilitarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utilitarian "Utilitarian") in his youth, yet he later distanced himself from this view.

For the advancement of science and protection of liberty of expression, Russell advocated [Alfred Henry Lloyd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Henry_Lloyd "Alfred Henry Lloyd")'s concept of *The Will to Doubt*, the recognition, expressed in his book of the same name, that all human knowledge is at most a best guess. He writes that that one should always remember:

> None of our beliefs are quite true; all have at least a penumbra of vagueness and error. The methods of increasing the degree of truth in our beliefs are well known; they consist in hearing all sides, trying to ascertain all the relevant facts, controlling our own bias by discussion with people who have the opposite bias, and cultivating a readiness to discard any hypothesis which has proved inadequate. These methods are practised in science, and have built up the body of scientific knowledge.
> Every man of science whose outlook is truly scientific is ready to admit that what passes for scientific knowledge at the moment is sure to require correction with the progress of discovery; nevertheless, it is near enough to the truth to serve for most practical purposes, though not for all. In science, where alone something approximating to genuine knowledge is to be found, men's attitude is tentative and full of doubt.

### Religion

Russell described himself in 1947 as an [agnostic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agnosticism "Agnosticism") or an [atheist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atheism "Atheism"): he found it difficult to determine which term to adopt, saying:

> Therefore, in regard to the [Olympic gods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twelve_Olympians "Twelve Olympians"), speaking to a purely philosophical audience, I would say that I am an Agnostic. But speaking popularly, I think that all of us would say in regard to those gods that we were Atheists. In regard to the [Christian God](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/God_in_Christianity "God in Christianity"), I should, I think, take exactly the same line.

For most of his adult life, Russell maintained religion to be little more than [superstition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superstition "Superstition") and, despite any positive effects, largely harmful to people. He believed that religion and the religious outlook serve to impede knowledge and foster fear and dependency, and to be responsible for much of our world's wars, oppression, and misery. He was a member of the advisory council of the [British Humanist Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Humanist_Association "British Humanist Association") and the president of Cardiff Humanists until his death.

### Society

Political and social activism occupied much of Russell's time for most of his life. Russell remained politically active almost to the end of his life, writing to and exhorting world leaders and lending his name to various causes. He was a prominent campaigner against Western intervention into the [Vietnam War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnam_War "Vietnam War") in the 1960s, writing essays and books, attending demonstrations, and even organising the [Russell Tribunal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russell_Tribunal "Russell Tribunal") in 1966 alongside other prominent philosophers such as [Jean-Paul Sartre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Paul_Sartre "Jean-Paul Sartre") and [Simone de Beauvoir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simone_de_Beauvoir "Simone de Beauvoir"), which fed into his 1967 book *War Crimes in Vietnam.*

Russell argued for a "scientific society", where war would be abolished, population growth would be limited, and prosperity would be shared. He suggested the establishment of a "single supreme world government" able to enforce peace, claiming that "the only thing that will redeem mankind is co-operation". He was one of the signatories of the agreement to convene a convention for drafting a [world constitution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_constitution "World constitution"). As a result, for the first time in human history, a [World Constituent Assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Constituent_Assembly "World Constituent Assembly") convened to draft and adopt the [Constitution for the Federation of Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitution_for_the_Federation_of_Earth "Constitution for the Federation of Earth").

Russell also expressed support for [guild socialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guild_socialism "Guild socialism"), and commented positively on several socialist thinkers and activists. According to [Jean Bricmont](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Bricmont "Jean Bricmont") and Normand Baillargeon, "Russell was both a [liberal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberalism "Liberalism") and a [socialist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialism "Socialism"), a combination that was perfectly comprehensible in his time, but which has become almost unthinkable today. He was a liberal in that he opposed concentrations of power in all its manifestations, military, governmental, or religious, as well as the superstitious or nationalist ideas that usually serve as its justification. But he was also a socialist, even as an extension of his liberalism, because he was equally opposed to the concentrations of power stemming from the [private ownership](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Private_ownership "Private ownership") of the major [means of production](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Means_of_production "Means of production"), which therefore needed to be put under social control (which does not mean state control)."

Russell was an active supporter of the [Homosexual Law Reform Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homosexual_Law_Reform_Society "Homosexual Law Reform Society"), being one of the signatories of [A. E. Dyson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A._E._Dyson "A. E. Dyson")'s 1958 letter to *The Times* calling for a change in the law regarding male homosexual practices, which were partly legalised in 1967, when Russell was still alive.

He expressed sympathy and support for the [Palestinian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palestinians "Palestinians") people and was critical of [Israel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Israel "Israel")'s actions. He wrote in 1960 that, "I think it was a mistake to establish a Jewish State in Palestine, but it would be a still greater mistake to try to get rid of it now that it exists." In his final written document, read aloud in [Cairo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cairo "Cairo") three days after his death on 31 January 1970, he condemned Israel as an aggressive [imperialist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperialism "Imperialism") power, which "wishes to consolidate with the least difficulty what it has already taken by violence. Every new conquest becomes the new basis of the proposed negotiation from strength, which ignores the injustice of the previous aggression." In regards to the Palestinian people and [refugees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palestinian_refugees "Palestinian refugees"), he wrote that, "No people anywhere in the world would accept being expelled en masse from their own country; how can anyone require the people of Palestine to accept a punishment which nobody else would tolerate? A permanent just settlement of the refugees in their homeland is an essential ingredient of any genuine settlement in the [Middle East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_East "Middle East")."

Russell advocated a [universal basic income](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_basic_income "Universal basic income"). In his 1918 book *Roads to Freedom*, Russell wrote that "[Anarchism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anarchism "Anarchism") has the advantage as regards liberty, Socialism as regards the inducement to work. Can we not find a method of combining these two advantages? It seems to me that we can. [...] Stated in more familiar terms, the plan we are advocating amounts essentially to this: that a certain small income, sufficient for necessaries, should be secured to all, whether they work or not, and that a larger income – as much larger as might be warranted by the total amount of commodities produced – should be given to those who are willing to engage in some work which the community recognizes as useful...When education is finished, no one should be compelled to work, and those who choose not to work should receive a bare livelihood and be left completely free."

In "Reflections on My Eightieth Birthday" ("Postscript" in his *Autobiography*), Russell wrote: "I have lived in the pursuit of a vision, both personal and social. Personal: to care for what is noble, for what is beautiful, for what is gentle; to allow moments of insight to give wisdom at more mundane times. Social: to see in imagination the society that is to be created, where individuals grow freely, and where hate and greed and envy die because there is nothing to nourish them. These things I believe, and the world, for all its horrors, has left me unshaken".

### Freedom of opinion and expression

Russell supported freedom of opinion and was an opponent of both censorship and indoctrination. In 1928, he wrote:

> The fundamental argument for freedom of opinion is the doubtfulness of all our belief... when the State intervenes to ensure the indoctrination of some doctrine, it does so because there is no conclusive evidence in favour of that doctrine ... It is clear that thought is not free if the profession of certain opinions make it impossible to make a living.

In 1957, he wrote: "'Free thought' means thinking freely ... to be worthy of the name freethinker he must be free of two things: the force of tradition and the tyranny of his own passions."

### Education

Russell has presented ideas on the possible means of control of education in case of scientific dictatorship governments, of the kind of this excerpt taken from Chapter II "General Effects of Scientific Technique" of "The Impact of Science on society":

> This subject will make great strides when it is taken up by scientists under a scientific dictatorship. Anaxagoras maintained that snow is black, but no one believed him. The social psychologists of the future will have a number of classes of school children on whom they will try different methods of producing an unshakable conviction that snow is black. Various results will soon be arrived at. First, that the influence of home is obstructive. Second, that not much can be done unless indoctrination begins before the age of ten. Third, that verses set to music and repeatedly intoned are very effective. Fourth, that the opinion that snow is white must be held to show a morbid taste for eccentricity. But I anticipate. It is for future scientists to make these maxims precise and discover exactly how much it costs per head to make children believe that snow is black, and how much less it would cost to make them believe it is dark grey. Although this science will be diligently studied, it will be rigidly confined to the governing class. The populace will not be allowed to know how its convictions were generated. When the technique has been perfected, every government that has been in charge of education for a generation will be able to control its subjects securely without the need of armies or policemen. As yet there is only one country which has succeeded in creating this politician's paradise. The social effects of scientific technique have already been many and important, and are likely to be even more noteworthy in the future. Some of these effects depend upon the political and economic character of the country concerned; others are inevitable, whatever this character may be.

He pushed his visionary scenarios even further into details, in Chapter III "Scientific Technique in an Oligarchy" of the same book, stating as an example:

> In future such failures are not likely to occur where there is dictatorship. Diet, injections, and injunctions will combine, from a very early age, to produce the sort of character and the sort of beliefs that the authorities consider desirable, and any serious criticism of the powers that be will become psychologically impossible. Even if all are miserable, all will believe themselves happy, because the government will tell them that they are so.

Russell was the author of more than sixty books and over two thousand articles. Additionally, he wrote many pamphlets, introductions, and letters to the editor. One pamphlet, titled *"I Appeal unto Caesar": The Case of the Conscientious Objectors*, ghostwritten for Margaret Hobhouse, the mother of imprisoned peace activist [Stephen Hobhouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Hobhouse "Stephen Hobhouse"), allegedly helped secure the release from prison of hundreds of [conscientious objectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conscientious_objector "Conscientious objector").

His works can be found in anthologies and collections, including *The Collected Papers of Bertrand Russell*, which [McMaster University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McMaster_University "McMaster University") began publishing in 1983. By January 2026, this collection of his shorter and previously unpublished works included 22 volumes, and 13 more are in progress for a total of 35 planned volumes. A bibliography in three additional volumes catalogues his publications. The Russell Archives held by McMaster's [William Ready Division of Archives and Research Collections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Ready_Division_of_Archives_and_Research_Collections "William Ready Division of Archives and Research Collections") possess over 40,000 of his letters.
