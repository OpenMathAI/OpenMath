**René Frédéric Thom** (French: [[ʁəne tɔm]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/French "Help:IPA/French"); 2 September 1923 – 25 October 2002) was a French [mathematician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematician "Mathematician"), who received the [Fields Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fields_Medal "Fields Medal") in 1958.

He made his reputation as a [topologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topologist "Topologist"), moving on to aspects of what would be called [singularity theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singularity_theory "Singularity theory"); he became world-famous among the wider academic community and the educated general public for one aspect of this latter interest, his work as the founder of [catastrophe theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catastrophe_theory "Catastrophe theory") (later developed by [Christopher Zeeman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Zeeman "Christopher Zeeman")).

## Life and career

René Thom grew up in a modest family in [Montbéliard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montbéliard "Montbéliard"), [Doubs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doubs "Doubs") and obtained a [Baccalauréat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baccalauréat "Baccalauréat") in 1940. After the [German invasion of France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_invasion_of_France_(1940) "German invasion of France (1940)"), his family took refuge in [Switzerland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Switzerland "Switzerland") and then in [Lyon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyon "Lyon"). In 1941 he moved to Paris to attend [Lycée Saint-Louis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lycée_Saint-Louis "Lycée Saint-Louis") and in 1943 he began studying mathematics at [École Normale Supérieure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/École_Normale_Supérieure "École Normale Supérieure"), becoming [agrégé](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agrégé "Agrégé") in 1946.

He received his PhD in 1951 from the [University of Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Paris "University of Paris"). His thesis, titled *Espaces fibrés en sphères et carrés de Steenrod* (*Sphere bundles and Steenrod squares*), was written under the direction of [Henri Cartan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_Cartan "Henri Cartan").

After a fellowship at [Princeton University Graduate College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University_Graduate_College "Princeton University Graduate College") (1951–1952), he became [Maître de conférences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maître_de_conférences "Maître de conférences") at the Universities of [Grenoble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Université_Joseph_Fourier "Université Joseph Fourier") (1953–1954) and [Strasbourg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Strasbourg "University of Strasbourg") (1954–1963), where he was appointed Professor in 1957. In 1964 he moved to the [Institut des Hautes Études Scientifiques](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institut_des_Hautes_Études_Scientifiques "Institut des Hautes Études Scientifiques"), in [Bures-sur-Yvette](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bures-sur-Yvette "Bures-sur-Yvette"), where he worked until 1990.

In 1958, Thom received the [Fields Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fields_Medal "Fields Medal") at the [International Congress of Mathematicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Congress_of_Mathematicians "International Congress of Mathematicians") in [Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edinburgh "Edinburgh") for the foundations of [cobordism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cobordism "Cobordism") theory, which were already present in his thesis. He was [invited speaker at the International Congress of Mathematicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invited_speaker_at_the_International_Congress_of_Mathematicians "Invited speaker at the International Congress of Mathematicians") two more times: in 1970 in [Nice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nice "Nice") and 1983 in [Warsaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warsaw "Warsaw") (which he did not attend).

He was awarded the [Brouwer Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brouwer_Medal "Brouwer Medal") in 1970, the [Grand Prix Scientifique de la Ville de Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Prix_Scientifique_de_la_Ville_de_Paris?action=edit&redlink=1 "Grand Prix Scientifique de la Ville de Paris") in 1974, and the [John von Neumann Lecture Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_von_Neumann_Lecture_Prize "John von Neumann Lecture Prize") in 1976. He became the first president, together with [Louis Néel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Néel "Louis Néel"), of the newly established [Fondation Louis-de-Broglie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fondation_Louis-de-Broglie "Fondation Louis-de-Broglie") In 1973 and was elected Member of the [Académie des Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Académie_des_Sciences "Académie des Sciences") of Paris in 1976.

[Salvador Dalí](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salvador_Dalí "Salvador Dalí") paid homage to René Thom with the paintings *[The Swallow's Tail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Swallow's_Tail "The Swallow's Tail")* and *Topological Abduction of Europe*.

## Research

While René Thom is most known to the public for his development of [catastrophe theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catastrophe_theory "Catastrophe theory") between 1968 and 1972, his academic achievements concern mostly his mathematical work on topology.

In the early 1950s, it concerned what are now called [Thom spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thom_space "Thom space"), [characteristic classes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Characteristic_class "Characteristic class"), [cobordism theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cobordism_theory "Cobordism theory"), and the [Thom transversality theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thom_transversality_theorem "Thom transversality theorem"). Another example of this line of work is the [Thom conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thom_conjecture "Thom conjecture"), versions of which have been investigated using [gauge theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauge_theory "Gauge theory"). From the mid 1950s he moved into [singularity theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singularity_theory "Singularity theory"), of which catastrophe theory is just one aspect, and in a series of deep (and at the time obscure) papers between 1960 and 1969 developed the theory of [stratified sets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topologically_stratified_space "Topologically stratified space") and stratified maps, proving a basic stratified isotopy theorem describing the local conical structure of [Whitney stratified sets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whitney_conditions "Whitney conditions"), now known as the [Thom–Mather isotopy theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thom–Mather_isotopy_theorem "Thom–Mather isotopy theorem"). Much of his work on stratified sets was developed so as to understand the notion of topologically [stable maps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stable_map "Stable map"), and to eventually prove the result that the set of topologically stable mappings between two smooth manifolds is a [dense set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dense_set "Dense set").

Thom's lectures on the stability of differentiable mappings, given at the [University of Bonn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Bonn "University of Bonn") in 1960, were written up by [Harold Levine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Levine "Harold Levine") and published in the proceedings of a year long symposium on singularities at [Liverpool University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liverpool_University "Liverpool University") during 1969–70, edited by [C. T. C. Wall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C._T._C._Wall "C. T. C. Wall"). The proof of the density of topologically stable mappings was completed by [John Mather](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Mather_(mathematician) "John Mather (mathematician)") in 1970, based on the ideas developed by Thom in the previous ten years. A coherent detailed account was published in 1976 by Christopher Gibson, Klaus Wirthmüller, Andrew du Plessis, and [Eduard Looijenga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eduard_Looijenga "Eduard Looijenga").

During the last twenty years of his life Thom's published work was mainly in philosophy and epistemology, and he undertook a reevaluation of [Aristotle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotle "Aristotle")'s writings on science. In 1992, he was one of eighteen academics who sent a letter to [Cambridge University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_University "Cambridge University") protesting against plans to award [Jacques Derrida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques_Derrida "Jacques Derrida") an honorary doctorate.

Beyond Thom's contributions to algebraic topology, he studied differentiable mappings, through the study of [generic properties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generic_property "Generic property"). In his final years, he turned his attention to an effort to apply his ideas about structural topography to the questions of thought, language, and meaning in the form of a "semiophysics".
