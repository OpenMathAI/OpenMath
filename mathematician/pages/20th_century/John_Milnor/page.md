**John Willard Milnor** (born February 20, 1931) is an American mathematician known for his work in [differential topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_topology "Differential topology"), [algebraic K-theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_K-theory "Algebraic K-theory") and low-dimensional holomorphic [dynamical systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamical_systems "Dynamical systems"). Milnor is a distinguished professor at [Stony Brook University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stony_Brook_University "Stony Brook University") and the only mathematician to have won the [Fields Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fields_Medal "Fields Medal"), the [Wolf Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolf_Prize "Wolf Prize"), the [Abel Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abel_Prize "Abel Prize") and all three [Steele prizes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leroy_P._Steele_Prize "Leroy P. Steele Prize").

## Early life and career

Milnor was born on February 20, 1931, in [Orange, New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orange,_New_Jersey "Orange, New Jersey"). His father was J. Willard Milnor, an engineer, and his mother was Emily Cox Milnor. As an undergraduate at [Princeton University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University "Princeton University") he was named a [Putnam Fellow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Lowell_Putnam_Mathematical_Competition "William Lowell Putnam Mathematical Competition") in 1949 and 1950 and also proved the [Fáry–Milnor theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fáry–Milnor_theorem "Fáry–Milnor theorem") when he was only 19 years old. Milnor graduated with an A.B. in mathematics in 1951 after completing a senior thesis, titled "Link groups", under the supervision of [Ralph Fox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ralph_Fox "Ralph Fox"). He remained at Princeton to pursue graduate studies and received his Ph.D. in mathematics in 1954 after completing a doctoral dissertation, titled "Isotopy of links", also under the supervision of Fox. His dissertation concerned [link groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Link_group "Link group") (a generalization of the classical knot group) and their associated link structure, classifying [Brunnian links](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brunnian_link "Brunnian link") up to link-homotopy and introduced new invariants of it, called [Milnor invariants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milnor_invariants "Milnor invariants"). Upon completing his doctorate, he went on to work at Princeton. He was a professor at the [Institute for Advanced Study](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_for_Advanced_Study "Institute for Advanced Study") from 1970 to 1990.

Milnor was an editor of the *[Annals of Mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annals_of_Mathematics "Annals of Mathematics")* for a number of years after 1962. He has written a number of books, like *[Characteristic Classes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Characteristic_Classes "Characteristic Classes")*, which are famous for their clarity and presentation, providing inspiration for the research of many mathematicians in their areas, even many decades after their publication. He served as Vice President of the [AMS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Mathematical_Society "American Mathematical Society") in 1976–77 period.

Milnor's students have included [Tadatoshi Akiba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tadatoshi_Akiba "Tadatoshi Akiba"), [Jon Folkman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jon_Folkman "Jon Folkman"), [John Mather](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Mather_(mathematician) "John Mather (mathematician)"), [Laurent C. Siebenmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laurent_C._Siebenmann "Laurent C. Siebenmann"), [Michael Spivak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Spivak "Michael Spivak"), and Jonathan Sondow. His wife, [Dusa McDuff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dusa_McDuff "Dusa McDuff"), is a professor of mathematics at [Barnard College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnard_College "Barnard College") and is known for her work in [symplectic topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symplectic_topology "Symplectic topology").

## Research

One of Milnor's best-known works is his proof in 1956 of the existence of [7-dimensional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seven-dimensional_space "Seven-dimensional space") [spheres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypersphere "Hypersphere") with nonstandard differentiable structure, which marked the beginning of a new field – differential topology. He coined the term [exotic sphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exotic_sphere "Exotic sphere"), referring to any *n*-sphere with nonstandard differential structure. Kervaire and Milnor initiated the systematic study of exotic spheres by [Kervaire–Milnor groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kervaire–Milnor_group "Kervaire–Milnor group"), showing in particular that the 7-sphere has 15 distinct [differentiable structures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differentiable_structure "Differentiable structure") (28 if one considers orientation).

[Egbert Brieskorn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egbert_Brieskorn "Egbert Brieskorn") found simple algebraic equations for 28 complex hypersurfaces in complex 5-space such that their intersection with a small sphere of dimension 9 around a [singular point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singular_point_of_a_curve "Singular point of a curve") is diffeomorphic to these exotic spheres. Subsequently, Milnor worked on the [topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topology "Topology") of isolated [singular points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singular_point_of_a_curve "Singular point of a curve") of complex hypersurfaces in general, developing the theory of the [Milnor fibration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milnor_fibration "Milnor fibration") whose fiber has the [homotopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homotopy "Homotopy") type of a bouquet of *μ* spheres where *μ* is known as the [Milnor number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milnor_number "Milnor number"). Milnor's 1968 book on his theory, *Singular Points of Complex Hypersurfaces*, inspired the growth of a huge and rich research area that continues to mature to this day.

In 1961, Milnor disproved the [Hauptvermutung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hauptvermutung "Hauptvermutung") by illustrating two [simplicial complexes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simplicial_complex "Simplicial complex") that are [homeomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeomorphism "Homeomorphism") but [combinatorially](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combinatorial_topology "Combinatorial topology") distinct, using the concept of [Reidemeister torsion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytic_torsion "Analytic torsion").

In 1984, Milnor introduced a definition of [attractor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attractor "Attractor"). The objects generalize standard attractors, include so-called unstable attractors, and are now known as Milnor attractors.

Milnor's current interest is dynamics, especially holomorphic dynamics. His work in dynamics is summarized by Peter Makienko in his review of *Topological Methods in Modern Mathematics*:

> It is evident now that low-dimensional dynamics, to a large extent initiated by Milnor's work, is a fundamental part of general dynamical systems theory. Milnor cast his eye on dynamical systems theory in the mid-1970s. By that time the Smale program in dynamics had been completed. Milnor's approach was to start over from the very beginning, looking at the simplest nontrivial families of maps. The first choice, one-dimensional dynamics, became the subject of his joint paper with [Thurston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Thurston "William Thurston"). Even the case of a unimodal map, that is, one with a single critical point, turns out to be extremely rich. This work may be compared with [Poincaré's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_Poincaré "Henri Poincaré") work on [circle diffeomorphisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffeomorphism#Topology "Diffeomorphism"), which 100 years before had inaugurated the qualitative theory of dynamical systems. Milnor's work has opened several new directions in this field, and has given us many basic concepts, challenging problems and nice theorems.

His other significant contributions include [microbundles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microbundle "Microbundle"), influencing the usage of [Hopf algebras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hopf_algebra "Hopf algebra"), theory of [quadratic forms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_forms "Quadratic forms") and the related area of [symmetric bilinear forms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bilinear_form "Bilinear form"), higher [algebraic K-theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_K-theory "Algebraic K-theory"), [game theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game_theory "Game theory"), and three-dimensional [Lie groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lie_group "Lie group").

## Awards and honors

Milnor was elected as a member of the [American Academy of Arts and Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Academy_of_Arts_and_Sciences "American Academy of Arts and Sciences") in 1961. In 1962 Milnor was awarded the [Fields Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fields_Medal "Fields Medal") for his work in differential topology. He was elected to the United States [National Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Academy_of_Sciences "National Academy of Sciences") in 1963 and the [American Philosophical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Philosophical_Society "American Philosophical Society") 1965. He later went on to win the [National Medal of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Medal_of_Science "National Medal of Science") (1967), the [Lester R. Ford Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lester_R._Ford_Award "Lester R. Ford Award") in 1970 and again in 1984, the [Leroy P. Steele Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leroy_P._Steele_Prize "Leroy P. Steele Prize") for "Seminal Contribution to Research" (1982), the [Wolf Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolf_Prize "Wolf Prize") in Mathematics (1989), the [Leroy P. Steele Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leroy_P._Steele_Prize "Leroy P. Steele Prize") for Mathematical Exposition (2004), and the [Leroy P. Steele Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leroy_P._Steele_Prize "Leroy P. Steele Prize") for Lifetime Achievement (2011). In 1991 a symposium was held at Stony Brook University in celebration of his 60th birthday.

Milnor was awarded the 2011 [Abel Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abel_Prize "Abel Prize"), for his "pioneering discoveries in topology, geometry and algebra." Reacting to the award, Milnor told the *[New Scientist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Scientist "New Scientist")* "It feels very good," adding that "[o]ne is always surprised by a call at 6 o'clock in the morning."

In 2013, he became a [fellow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow "Fellow") of the [American Mathematical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Mathematical_Society "American Mathematical Society"), for "contributions to differential topology, geometric topology, algebraic topology, algebra, and dynamical systems".

In 2020, he received the [Lomonosov Gold Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lomonosov_Gold_Medal "Lomonosov Gold Medal") of the Russian Academy of Sciences.
