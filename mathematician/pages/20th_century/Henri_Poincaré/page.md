**Jules Henri Poincaré** ([UK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_English "British English"): [/ˈpwæ̃kɑːreɪ/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English"), [US](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_English "American English"): [/ˌpwæ̃kɑːˈreɪ/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English"); French: [[ɑ̃ʁi pwɛ̃kaʁe]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/French "Help:IPA/French") [ⓘ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Fr-Henri_Poincar%C3%A9.ogg "File:Fr-Henri Poincaré.ogg"); 29 April 1854 – 17 July 1912) was a French [mathematician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematician "Mathematician"), [theoretical physicist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theoretical_physics "Theoretical physics"), engineer, and [philosopher of science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_science "Philosophy of science"). He is often described as a [polymath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polymath "Polymath"), and in mathematics as "The Last Universalist", since he excelled in all fields of the discipline as it existed during his lifetime. He has further been called "the [Gauss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Friedrich_Gauss "Carl Friedrich Gauss") of [modern mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_mathematics "History of mathematics")". Due to his success in science, along with his influence in philosophy, he has also been called "the philosopher par excellence of modern science".

As a mathematician and [physicist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physicist "Physicist"), he made many original fundamental contributions to [pure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pure_mathematics "Pure mathematics") and [applied mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Applied_mathematics "Applied mathematics"), [mathematical physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_physics "Mathematical physics"), and [celestial mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celestial_mechanics "Celestial mechanics"). In his research on the [three-body problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three-body_problem "Three-body problem"), Poincaré became the first person to discover a chaotic [deterministic system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deterministic_system "Deterministic system") which laid the foundations of modern [chaos theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chaos_theory "Chaos theory"). Poincaré is regarded as the creator of the field of [algebraic topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_topology "Algebraic topology"), and is further credited with introducing [automorphic forms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automorphic_form "Automorphic form"). He also made important contributions to [algebraic geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_geometry "Algebraic geometry"), [number theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_theory "Number theory"), [complex analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_analysis "Complex analysis") and [Lie theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lie_theory "Lie theory"). He famously introduced the concept of the [Poincaré recurrence theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poincaré_recurrence_theorem "Poincaré recurrence theorem"), which states that a state will eventually return arbitrarily close to its initial state after a sufficiently long time, which has far-reaching consequences. Early in the 20th century he formulated the [Poincaré conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poincaré_conjecture "Poincaré conjecture"), which became, over time, one of the famous [unsolved problems in mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_unsolved_problems_in_mathematics "List of unsolved problems in mathematics"). It was eventually solved in 2002–2003 by [Grigori Perelman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grigori_Perelman "Grigori Perelman"). Poincaré popularized the use of [non-Euclidean geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-Euclidean_geometry "Non-Euclidean geometry") in [mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics "Mathematics") as well.

Poincaré made clear the importance of paying attention to the [invariance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invariant_(mathematics) "Invariant (mathematics)") of laws of physics under different transformations, and was the first to present the [Lorentz transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentz_transformation "Lorentz transformation") in their modern symmetrical form. Poincaré discovered the remaining [relativistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relativistic_mechanics "Relativistic mechanics") velocity transformations and recorded them in a letter to [Hendrik Lorentz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hendrik_Lorentz "Hendrik Lorentz") in 1905. Thus he obtained perfect invariance of all of [Maxwell's equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxwell's_equations "Maxwell's equations"), an important step in the formulation of the theory of [special relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_relativity "Special relativity"), for which he is also credited with laying down the foundations, further writing foundational papers in 1905. He first proposed [gravitational waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_wave "Gravitational wave") (*ondes gravifiques*) emanating from a body and propagating at the speed of light as being required by the Lorentz transformations, doing so in 1905. In 1912, he wrote an influential paper which provided a mathematical argument for [quantum mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_mechanics "Quantum mechanics"). Poincaré also laid the seeds of the discovery of [radioactivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radioactive_decay "Radioactive decay") through his interest and study of [X-rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray "X-ray"), which influenced physicist [Henri Becquerel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_Becquerel "Henri Becquerel"), who then discovered the phenomena. The [Poincaré group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poincaré_group "Poincaré group") used in physics and [mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics "Mathematics") was named after him, after he introduced the notion of the group.

Poincaré was considered the dominant figure in mathematics and [theoretical physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theoretical_physics "Theoretical physics") during his time, and was the most respected mathematician of his time, being described as "the living brain of the rational sciences" by mathematician [Paul Painlevé](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Painlevé "Paul Painlevé"). Philosopher [Karl Popper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Popper "Karl Popper") regarded Poincaré as the greatest philosopher of science of all time, with Poincaré also originating the [conventionalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conventionalism "Conventionalism") view in science. Poincaré was a [public intellectual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intellectual#Public_intellectual "Intellectual") in his time, and personally, he believed in [political equality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_egalitarianism "Political egalitarianism") for all, while wary of the influence of anti-intellectual positions that the [Catholic Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Church "Catholic Church") held at the time. He served as the president of the [French Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Academy_of_Sciences#Presidents "French Academy of Sciences") (1906), the president of [Société astronomique de France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Société_astronomique_de_France#Presidents "Société astronomique de France") (1901–1903), and twice the president of [Société mathématique de France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Société_mathématique_de_France "Société mathématique de France") (1886, 1900).

## Life

Poincaré was born on 29 April 1854 in Cité Ducale neighborhood, [Nancy, Meurthe-et-Moselle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nancy,_Meurthe-et-Moselle "Nancy, Meurthe-et-Moselle"), into an influential French family. His father [Léon Poincaré](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Léon_Poincaré?action=edit&redlink=1 "Léon Poincaré") (1828–1892) was a professor of medicine at the [University of Nancy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Nancy "University of Nancy"). His younger sister Aline married the spiritual philosopher [Émile Boutroux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Émile_Boutroux "Émile Boutroux"). Another notable member of Henri's family was his cousin, [Raymond Poincaré](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raymond_Poincaré "Raymond Poincaré"), a fellow member of the [Académie française](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Académie_française "Académie française"), who was [President of France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_of_France "President of France") from 1913 to 1920, and three-time [Prime Minister of France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_Minister_of_France "Prime Minister of France") between 1913 and 1929.

### Education

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/1b/Henri_Poincar%C3%A9_maison_natale_Nancy_plaque.jpg/250px-Henri_Poincar%C3%A9_maison_natale_Nancy_plaque.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Henri_Poincaré_maison_natale_Nancy_plaque.jpg)

Plaque on the birthplace of Henri Poincaré at house number 117 on the Grande Rue in the city of Nancy

During his childhood he was seriously ill for a time with [diphtheria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diphtheria "Diphtheria") and received special instruction from his mother, Eugénie Launois (1830–1897).

In 1862, Henri entered the Lycée in [Nancy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nancy,_Meurthe-et-Moselle "Nancy, Meurthe-et-Moselle") (now renamed the [Lycée Henri-Poincaré](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lycée_Henri-Poincaré?action=edit&redlink=1 "Lycée Henri-Poincaré") in his honour, along with [Henri Poincaré University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_Poincaré_University "Henri Poincaré University"), also in Nancy). He spent eleven years at the Lycée and during this time he proved to be one of the top students in every topic he studied. He excelled in written composition. His mathematics teacher described him as a "monster of mathematics" and he won first prizes in the [concours général](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concours_général "Concours général"), a competition between the top pupils from all the Lycées across France. His poorest subjects were music and physical education, where he was described as "average at best". Poor eyesight and a tendency towards absentmindedness may explain these difficulties. He graduated from the Lycée in 1871 with a [baccalauréat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baccalauréat "Baccalauréat") in both letters and sciences.

During the [Franco-Prussian War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franco-Prussian_War "Franco-Prussian War") of 1870, he served alongside his father in the [Ambulance Corps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ambulance_Corps "Ambulance Corps").

Poincaré entered the [École Polytechnique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/École_Polytechnique "École Polytechnique") as the top qualifier in 1873 and graduated in 1875. There he studied mathematics as a student of [Charles Hermite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Hermite "Charles Hermite"), continuing to excel and publishing his first paper (*Démonstration nouvelle des propriétés de l'indicatrice d'une surface*) in 1874. From November 1875 to June 1878 he studied at the [École des Mines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/École_des_Mines "École des Mines"), while continuing the study of mathematics in addition to the [mining engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mining_engineering "Mining engineering") syllabus, and received the degree of ordinary mining engineer in March 1879.

As a graduate of the École des Mines, he joined the [Corps des Mines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corps_des_Mines "Corps des Mines") as an inspector for the [Vesoul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vesoul "Vesoul") region in northeast France. He was on the scene of a mining disaster at [Magny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magny-lès-Jussey "Magny-lès-Jussey") in August 1879 in which 18 miners died. He carried out the official investigation into the accident.

At the same time, Poincaré was preparing for his [Doctorate in Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctorate_in_Science "Doctorate in Science") in mathematics under the supervision of Charles Hermite. His doctoral thesis was in the field of [differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_equations "Differential equations"). It was named *Sur les propriétés des fonctions définies par les équations aux différences partielles*. Poincaré devised a new way of studying the properties of these equations. He not only faced the question of determining the integral of such equations, but also was the first person to study their general geometric properties. He realised that they could be used to model the behaviour of multiple bodies in free motion within the [Solar System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_System "Solar System"). He graduated from the [University of Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Paris "University of Paris") in 1879.

### First scientific achievements

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/db/Young_Poincare.jpg/250px-Young_Poincare.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Young_Poincare.jpg)

The young Henri Poincaré in 1887 at the age of 33

After receiving his degree, Poincaré began teaching as junior [lecturer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lecturer "Lecturer") in mathematics at the [University of Caen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caen_University "Caen University") in Normandy (in December 1879). At the same time he published his first major article concerning the treatment of a class of [automorphic functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automorphic_function "Automorphic function").

There, in [Caen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caen "Caen"), he met his future wife, Louise Poulain d'Andecy (1857–1934), granddaughter of [Isidore Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isidore_Geoffroy_Saint-Hilaire "Isidore Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire") and great-granddaughter of [Étienne Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Étienne_Geoffroy_Saint-Hilaire "Étienne Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire") and on 20 April 1881, they married. Together they had four children: Jeanne (born 1887), Yvonne (born 1889), Henriette (born 1891), and Léon (born 1893).

Poincaré immediately established himself among the greatest mathematicians of Europe, attracting the attention of many prominent mathematicians. In 1881 Poincaré was invited to take a teaching position at the Faculty of Sciences of the [University of Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Paris "University of Paris"); he accepted the invitation. During the years 1883 to 1897, he taught [mathematical analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_analysis "Mathematical analysis") in the [École Polytechnique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/École_Polytechnique "École Polytechnique").

In 1881–1882, Poincaré created a new branch of mathematics: [qualitative theory of differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qualitative_theory_of_differential_equations "Qualitative theory of differential equations"). He showed how it is possible to derive the most important information about the behavior of a family of solutions without having to solve the equation (since this may not always be possible). He successfully used this approach to problems in [celestial mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celestial_mechanics "Celestial mechanics") and [mathematical physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_physics "Mathematical physics").

### Career

He never fully abandoned his career in the mining administration for mathematics. He worked at the [Ministry of Public Services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Public_Services?action=edit&redlink=1 "Ministry of Public Services") as an engineer in charge of northern railway development from 1881 to 1885. He eventually became chief engineer of the [Corps des Mines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corps_des_Mines "Corps des Mines") in 1893 and inspector general in 1910.

Beginning in 1881 and for the rest of his career, he taught at the [University of Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Paris "University of Paris") (the [Sorbonne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Paris "University of Paris")). He was initially appointed as the *maître de conférences d'analyse* (associate professor of analysis). Eventually, he held the chairs of Physical and Experimental Mechanics, Mathematical Physics and Theory of Probability, and Celestial Mechanics and Astronomy.

In 1887, at the young age of 32, Poincaré was elected to the [French Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Academy_of_Sciences "French Academy of Sciences") he became its president in 1906, to honorary membership of the [Manchester Literary and Philosophical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manchester_Literary_and_Philosophical_Society "Manchester Literary and Philosophical Society"), in 1892, and was elected to the [Académie française](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Académie_française "Académie française") on 5 March 1908.

In 1887, he won [Oscar II, King of Sweden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oscar_II_of_Sweden "Oscar II of Sweden")'s mathematical competition for a resolution of the [three-body problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three-body_problem "Three-body problem") concerning the free motion of multiple orbiting bodies. (See [three-body problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_Poincaré#Three-body_problem) section below.)

In 1893, Poincaré joined the French [Bureau des Longitudes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bureau_des_Longitudes "Bureau des Longitudes"), which engaged him in the [synchronisation of time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clock_synchronization "Clock synchronization") around the world. In 1897 Poincaré backed an unsuccessful proposal for the [decimalisation of circular measure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decimal_degrees "Decimal degrees"), and hence time and [longitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longitude "Longitude"). It was this post which led him to consider the question of establishing international time zones and the synchronisation of time between bodies in relative motion. (See [work on relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_Poincaré#Work_on_relativity) section below.)

In 1904, he intervened in the [trials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dreyfus_affair "Dreyfus affair") of [Alfred Dreyfus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Dreyfus "Alfred Dreyfus"), attacking the spurious scientific claims regarding evidence brought against Dreyfus.

Poincaré was the President of the [Société Astronomique de France (SAF)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Société_astronomique_de_France "Société astronomique de France"), the French astronomical society, from 1901 to 1903.

#### Students

Poincaré had two notable doctoral students at the University of Paris, [Louis Bachelier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Bachelier "Louis Bachelier") (1900) and [Dimitrie Pompeiu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimitrie_Pompeiu "Dimitrie Pompeiu") (1905).

### Death

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/32/Poincar%C3%A9_gravestone.jpg/250px-Poincar%C3%A9_gravestone.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Poincaré_gravestone.jpg)

The Poincaré family grave at the [Cimetière du Montparnasse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cimetière_du_Montparnasse "Cimetière du Montparnasse")

In 1912, Poincaré underwent surgery for a [prostate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prostate "Prostate") problem and subsequently died from an [embolism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embolism "Embolism") on 17 July 1912, in Paris. He was 58 years of age. He is buried in the Poincaré family vault in the [Cemetery of Montparnasse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cimetière_du_Montparnasse "Cimetière du Montparnasse"), Paris, in section 16, close to the Rue Émile-Richard.

A former French Minister of Education, [Claude Allègre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claude_Allègre "Claude Allègre"), proposed in 2004 that Poincaré be reburied in the [Panthéon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panthéon,_Paris "Panthéon, Paris") in Paris, which is reserved for French citizens of the highest honour.

### Three-body problem

The problem of finding the general solution to the motion of more than two orbiting bodies in the [Solar System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_System "Solar System") had eluded mathematicians since [Newton's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Newton "Isaac Newton") time. This was known originally as the three-body problem and later the [*n*-body problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/N-body_problem "N-body problem"), where *n* is any number of more than two orbiting bodies. The *n*-body solution was considered very important and challenging at the close of the 19th century. Indeed, in 1887, in honour of his 60th birthday, [Oscar II, King of Sweden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oscar_II_of_Sweden "Oscar II of Sweden"), advised by [Gösta Mittag-Leffler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gösta_Mittag-Leffler "Gösta Mittag-Leffler"), established a prize for anyone who could find the solution to the problem. The announcement was quite specific:

> Given a system of arbitrarily many mass points that attract each according to [Newton's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton's_law_of_universal_gravitation "Newton's law of universal gravitation"), under the assumption that no two points ever collide, try to find a representation of the coordinates of each point as a series in a variable that is some known function of time and for all of whose values the series [converges uniformly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_convergence "Uniform convergence").

In case the problem could not be solved, any other important contribution to classical mechanics would then be considered to be prizeworthy. The prize was finally awarded to Poincaré, even though he did not solve the original problem. One of the judges, the distinguished [Karl Weierstrass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Weierstrass "Karl Weierstrass"), said, *"This work cannot indeed be considered as furnishing the complete solution of the question proposed, but that it is nevertheless of such importance that its publication will inaugurate a new era in the history of celestial mechanics."* (The first version of his contribution even contained a serious error; for details see the article by Diacu and the book by [Barrow-Green](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/June_Barrow-Green "June Barrow-Green")). The version finally printed contained many important ideas which led to the [theory of chaos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chaos_theory "Chaos theory"). The problem as stated originally was finally solved by [Karl F. Sundman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_F._Sundman "Karl F. Sundman") for *n* = 3 in 1912 and was generalised to the case of *n* > 3 bodies by [Qiudong Wang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qiudong_Wang "Qiudong Wang") in the 1990s. The series solutions have very slow convergence. It would take millions of terms to determine the motion of the particles for even very short intervals of time, so they are unusable in numerical work.

At the same time Dutch theorist [Hendrik Lorentz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hendrik_Lorentz "Hendrik Lorentz") was developing Maxwell's theory into a theory of the motion of charged particles ("electrons" or "ions"), and their interaction with radiation. #### Mass–energy relation

Like [others](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass–energy_equivalence#Electromagnetic_mass "Mass–energy equivalence") before, Poincaré (1900) discovered a relation between [mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass "Mass") and [electromagnetic energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_energy "Electromagnetic energy"). While studying the conflict between the [action/reaction principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton's_laws_of_motion "Newton's laws of motion") and [Lorentz ether theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentz_ether_theory "Lorentz ether theory"), he tried to determine whether the [center of gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_of_gravity "Center of gravity") still moves with a uniform velocity when electromagnetic fields are included. He noticed that the action/reaction principle does not hold for matter alone, but that the electromagnetic field has its own momentum. Poincaré concluded that the electromagnetic field energy of an electromagnetic wave behaves like a fictitious [fluid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluid "Fluid") (*fluide fictif*) with a mass density of *E*/*c*2. If the [center of mass frame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_of_mass_frame "Center of mass frame") is defined by both the mass of matter *and* the mass of the fictitious fluid, and if the fictitious fluid is indestructible—[it's neither created or destroyed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_law_of_thermodynamics "First law of thermodynamics")—then the motion of the center of mass frame remains uniform. But electromagnetic energy can be converted into other forms of energy. So Poincaré assumed that there exists a non-electric energy fluid at each point of space, into which electromagnetic energy can be transformed and which also carries a mass proportional to the energy. In this way, the motion of the center of mass remains uniform. Poincaré said that one should not be too surprised by these assumptions, since they are only mathematical fictions.

However, Poincaré's resolution led to a [paradox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paradox "Paradox") when changing frames: if a Hertzian oscillator radiates in a certain direction, it will suffer a [recoil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recoil "Recoil") from the inertia of the fictitious fluid. Poincaré performed a [Lorentz boost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentz_boost "Lorentz boost") (to order *v*/*c*) to the frame of the moving source. He noted that energy conservation holds in both frames, but that the [law of conservation of momentum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Momentum#Conservation "Momentum") is violated. This would allow [perpetual motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perpetual_motion "Perpetual motion"), a notion which he abhorred. The laws of nature would have to be different in the [frames of reference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frames_of_reference "Frames of reference"), and the relativity principle would not hold. Therefore, he argued that also in this case there has to be another compensating mechanism in the [ether](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ether "Ether").

Poincaré himself came back to this topic in his St. Louis lecture (1904). He rejected the possibility that energy carries mass and criticized his own solution to compensate the above-mentioned problems:

> The apparatus will recoil as if it were a cannon and the projected energy a ball, and that contradicts the principle of Newton, since our present projectile has no mass; it is not matter, it is energy. [..] Shall we say that the space which separates the oscillator from the receiver and which the disturbance must traverse in passing from one to the other, is not empty, but is filled not only with ether, but with air, or even in inter-planetary space with some subtile, yet ponderable fluid; that this matter receives the shock, as does the receiver, at the moment the energy reaches it, and recoils, when the disturbance leaves it? That would save Newton's principle, but it is not true. If the energy during its propagation remained always attached to some material substratum, this matter would carry the light along with it and Fizeau has shown, at least for the air, that there is nothing of the kind. Michelson and Morley have since confirmed this. We might also suppose that the motions of matter proper were exactly compensated by those of the ether; but that would lead us to the same considerations as those made a moment ago. The principle, if thus interpreted, could explain anything, since whatever the visible motions we could imagine hypothetical motions to compensate them. But if it can explain anything, it will allow us to foretell nothing; it will not allow us to choose between the various possible hypotheses, since it explains everything in advance. It therefore becomes useless.

In the above quote he refers to the Hertz assumption of total aether entrainment that was falsified by the [Fizeau experiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fizeau_experiment "Fizeau experiment") but that experiment does indeed show that light is partially "carried along" with a substance. Finally in 1908 he revisits the problem and ends with abandoning the principle of reaction altogether in favor of supporting a solution based in the inertia of aether itself.

> But we have seen above that Fizeau's experiment does not permit of our retaining the theory of Hertz; it is necessary therefore to adopt the theory of Lorentz, and consequently to renounce the principle of reaction.

It was [Albert Einstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Einstein "Albert Einstein")'s concept of [mass–energy equivalence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass–energy_equivalence "Mass–energy equivalence") (1905) that a body losing energy as radiation or heat was losing mass of amount *m* = *E*/*c*2 that resolved Poincaré's paradox, without using any compensating mechanism within the ether. The Hertzian oscillator loses mass in the emission process, and momentum is conserved in any frame. However, concerning Poincaré's solution of the Center of Gravity problem, Einstein noted that Poincaré's formulation and his own from 1906 were mathematically equivalent.

#### Gravitational waves

In 1905 Poincaré first proposed [gravitational waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_waves "Gravitational waves") (*ondes gravifiques*) emanating from a body and propagating at the speed of light. He wrote:

> It has become important to examine this hypothesis more closely and in particular to ask in what ways it would require us to modify the laws of gravitation. That is what I have tried to determine; at first I was led to assume that the propagation of gravitation is not instantaneous, but happens with the speed of light.

#### Poincaré and Einstein

Einstein's first paper on relativity was published three months after Poincaré's short paper, but before Poincaré's longer version. Einstein relied on the principle of relativity to derive the Lorentz transformations and used a similar clock synchronisation procedure ([Einstein synchronisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Einstein_synchronisation "Einstein synchronisation")) to the one that Poincaré (1900) had described, but Einstein's paper was remarkable in that it contained no references at all. Poincaré never acknowledged Einstein's work on [special relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_relativity "Special relativity"). However, Einstein expressed sympathy with Poincaré's outlook obliquely in a letter to [Hans Vaihinger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Vaihinger "Hans Vaihinger") on 3 May 1919, when Einstein considered Vaihinger's general outlook to be close to his own and Poincaré's to be close to Vaihinger's. In public, Einstein acknowledged Poincaré posthumously in the text of a lecture in 1921 titled "*Geometrie und Erfahrung* (Geometry and Experience)" in connection with [non-Euclidean geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-Euclidean_geometry "Non-Euclidean geometry"), but not in connection with special relativity. A few years before his death, Einstein commented on Poincaré as being one of the pioneers of relativity, saying "Lorentz had already recognized that the transformation named after him is essential for the analysis of Maxwell's equations, and Poincaré deepened this insight still further ....".

#### Assessments on Poincaré and relativity

Poincaré's work in the development of special relativity is well recognised, though most historians stress that despite many similarities with Einstein's work, the two had very different research agendas and interpretations of the work. Poincaré developed a similar physical interpretation of local time and noticed the connection to signal velocity, but contrary to Einstein he continued to use the ether-concept in his papers and argued that clocks at rest in the ether show the "true" time, and moving clocks show the local time. So Poincaré tried to keep the relativity principle in accordance with classical concepts, while Einstein developed a mathematically equivalent kinematics based on the new physical concepts of the relativity of space and time.

While this is the view of most historians, a minority go much further, such as [E. T. Whittaker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E._T._Whittaker "E. T. Whittaker"), who held that Poincaré and Lorentz were the true discoverers of relativity.

### Algebra and number theory

Poincaré introduced [group theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_theory "Group theory") to physics, and was the first to study the group of [Lorentz transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentz_transformations "Lorentz transformations"). He also made major contributions to the theory of discrete groups and their representations.

### Topology

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/26/Mug_and_Torus_morph.gif)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Mug_and_Torus_morph.gif)

Topological transformation of a mug into a torus

The subject is clearly defined by [Felix Klein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Felix_Klein "Felix Klein") in his "Erlangen Program" (1872): the geometry invariants of arbitrary continuous transformation, a kind of geometry. The term "topology" was introduced, as suggested by [Johann Benedict Listing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Benedict_Listing "Johann Benedict Listing"), instead of previously used "Analysis situs". Some important concepts were introduced by [Enrico Betti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enrico_Betti "Enrico Betti") and [Bernhard Riemann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernhard_Riemann "Bernhard Riemann"). But the foundation of this science, for a space of any dimension, was created by Poincaré. His first article on this topic appeared in 1894.

His research in [geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometry "Geometry") led to the abstract topological definition of [homotopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homotopy "Homotopy") and [homology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homology_(mathematics) "Homology (mathematics)"). He also first introduced the basic concepts and invariants of combinatorial topology, such as [Betti numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Betti_number "Betti number") and the [fundamental group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_group "Fundamental group"). Poincaré proved a formula relating the number of edges, [vertices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triangulated_irregular_network "Triangulated irregular network") and faces of *n*-dimensional [polyhedron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyhedron "Polyhedron") (the [Euler–Poincaré theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler_characteristic "Euler characteristic")) and gave the first precise formulation of the intuitive notion of dimension.

### Astronomy and celestial mechanics

[![Title page to volume I of Les Méthodes Nouvelles de la Mécanique Céleste (1892)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/df/Poincar%C3%A9-7.jpg/120px-Poincar%C3%A9-7.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Poincaré-7.jpg)

Title page to volume I of *Les Méthodes Nouvelles de la Mécanique Céleste* (1892)

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/f/f9/N-body_problem_%283%29.gif)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:N-body_problem_(3).gif)

Chaotic motion in three-body problem (computer simulation)

Poincaré published two now classical monographs, "New Methods of Celestial Mechanics" (1892–1899) and "Lectures on Celestial Mechanics" (1905–1910). In them, he successfully applied the results of their research to the problem of the motion of three bodies and studied in detail the behavior of solutions (frequency, stability, asymptotic, and so on). They introduced the small parameter method, fixed points, integral invariants, variational equations, the convergence of the asymptotic expansions. Generalizing a theory of Bruns (1887), Poincaré showed that the three-body problem is not integrable. In other words, the general solution of the three-body problem can not be expressed in terms of [algebraic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebra "Algebra") and [transcendental functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transcendental_functions "Transcendental functions") through unambiguous coordinates and velocities of the bodies. His work in this area was the first major achievement in celestial mechanics since [Isaac Newton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Newton "Isaac Newton").

These monographs include an idea of Poincaré, which later became the basis for mathematical "[chaos theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chaos_theory "Chaos theory")" (see, in particular, the [Poincaré recurrence theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poincaré_recurrence_theorem "Poincaré recurrence theorem")) and the general theory of [dynamical systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamical_system "Dynamical system"). Poincaré authored important works on [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy "Astronomy") for the [equilibrium figures of a gravitating rotating fluid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrostatic_equilibrium "Hydrostatic equilibrium"). He introduced the important concept of [bifurcation points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bifurcation_theory "Bifurcation theory") and proved the existence of equilibrium figures such as the non-ellipsoids, including ring-shaped and pear-shaped figures, and their stability. For this discovery, Poincaré received the Gold Medal of the Royal Astronomical Society (1900).

### Differential equations and mathematical physics

After defending his doctoral thesis on the study of singular points of the system of [differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_equations "Differential equations"), Poincaré wrote a series of memoirs under the title "On curves defined by differential equations" (1881–1882). In these articles, he built a new branch of mathematics, called "[qualitative theory of differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qualitative_theory_of_differential_equations "Qualitative theory of differential equations")". Poincaré showed that even if the differential equation can not be solved in terms of known functions, yet from the very form of the equation, a wealth of information about the properties and behavior of the solutions can be found. In particular, Poincaré investigated the nature of the trajectories of the integral curves in the plane, gave a classification of singular points ([saddle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saddle_point "Saddle point"), [focus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Focus_(geometry) "Focus (geometry)"), [center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_(algebra) "Center (algebra)"), [node](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertex_(graph_theory) "Vertex (graph theory)")), introduced the concept of a [limit cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limit_cycle "Limit cycle") and the [loop index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Control_flow#Loop_system_cross-reference_table "Control flow"), and showed that the number of limit cycles is always finite, except for some special cases. Poincaré also developed a general theory of integral invariants and solutions of the variational equations. For the [finite-difference equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_difference "Finite difference"), he created a new direction – the [asymptotic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asymptotic "Asymptotic") analysis of the solutions. He applied all these achievements to study practical problems of [mathematical physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_physics "Mathematical physics") and [celestial mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celestial_mechanics "Celestial mechanics"), and the methods used were the basis of its topological works.

## Character

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/thumb/0/0b/Henri_Poincar%C3%A9_by_H_Manuel.jpg/250px-Henri_Poincar%C3%A9_by_H_Manuel.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Henri_Poincaré_by_H_Manuel.jpg)

Photographic portrait of H. Poincaré by Henri Manuel

Poincaré's work habits have been compared to a [bee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bee "Bee") flying from flower to flower. Poincaré was interested in the way his [mind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mind "Mind") worked; he studied his habits and gave a talk about his observations in 1908 at the Institute of General Psychology in [Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paris "Paris"). He linked his way of [thinking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thought "Thought") to how he made several discoveries.

The mathematician [Darboux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Gaston_Darboux "Jean Gaston Darboux") claimed he was *un intuitif* (an [intuitive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intuitive "Intuitive")), arguing that this is demonstrated by the fact that he worked so often by visual representation. [Jacques Hadamard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques_Hadamard "Jacques Hadamard") wrote that Poincaré's research demonstrated marvelous clarity and Poincaré himself wrote that he  believed that logic was not a way to invent but a way to structure ideas and that logic limits ideas.

### Toulouse's characterisation

Poincaré's mental organisation was interesting not only to Poincaré himself but also to [Édouard Toulouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Édouard_Toulouse "Édouard Toulouse"), a [psychologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychologist "Psychologist") of the Psychology Laboratory of the School of Higher Studies in Paris. Toulouse wrote a book entitled *Henri Poincaré* (1910). In it, he discussed Poincaré's regular schedule:

- He worked during the same times each day in short periods of time. He undertook mathematical research for four hours a day, between 10 a.m. and noon then again from 5 p.m. to 7 p.m.. He would read articles in journals later in the evening.
- His normal work habit was to solve a problem completely in his head, then commit the completed problem to paper.
- He was [ambidextrous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ambidextrous "Ambidextrous") and [nearsighted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nearsighted "Nearsighted").
- His ability to visualise what he heard proved particularly useful when he attended lectures, since his eyesight was so poor that he could not see properly what the lecturer wrote on the blackboard.

These abilities were offset to some extent by his shortcomings:

- He was physically clumsy and [artistically](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art "Art") inept.
- He was always in a rush and disliked going back for changes or corrections.
- He never spent a long time on a problem since he believed that the [subconscious](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subconscious "Subconscious") would continue working on the problem [while he consciously worked on another problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_multitasking "Human multitasking").

In addition, Toulouse stated that most mathematicians worked from principles already established while Poincaré started from basic principles each time.

His method of thinking is well summarised as:

> *Habitué à négliger les détails et à ne regarder que les cimes, il passait de l'une à l'autre avec une promptitude surprenante et les faits qu'il découvrait se groupant d'eux-mêmes autour de leur centre étaient instantanément et automatiquement classés dans sa mémoire* (accustomed to neglecting details and to looking only at mountain tops, he went from one peak to another with surprising rapidity, and the facts he discovered, clustering around their center, were instantly and automatically pigeonholed in his memory).

— Belliver (1956)

## Legacy

Poincaré is credited with laying the foundations of [special relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_relativity "Special relativity"), with some [arguing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relativity_priority_dispute "Relativity priority dispute") that he should be credited with its creation. He is said to have "dominated the mathematics and the theoretical physics of his time", and that "he was without a doubt the most admired [mathematician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematician "Mathematician") while he was alive, and he remains today one of the world's most emblematic scientific figures." Poincaré is regarded as a "universal specialist", as he refined [celestial mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celestial_mechanics "Celestial mechanics"), he progressed nearly all parts of mathematics of his time, including creating new subjects, is a father of special relativity, participated in all the great debates of his time in physics, was a major actor in the great [epistemological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epistemology "Epistemology") debates of his day in relation to [philosophy of science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_science "Philosophy of science"), and Poincaré was the one who investigated the 1879 [Magny shaft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magny_shaft "Magny shaft") firedamp explosion as an engineer. Due to the breadth of his research, Poincaré was the only member to be elected to every section of the [French Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Academy_of_Sciences "French Academy of Sciences") of the time, those being geometry, mechanics, physics, astronomy and navigation.

Physicist [Henri Becquerel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_Becquerel "Henri Becquerel") nominated Poincaré for a [Nobel Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize "Nobel Prize") in 1904, as Becquerel took note that "Poincaré's mathematical and philosophical genius surveyed all of physics and was among those that contributed most to human progress by giving researchers a solid basis for their journeys into the unknown." After his death, he was praised by many intellectual figures of his time, as the author [Marie Bonaparte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marie_Bonaparte "Marie Bonaparte") wrote to his widowed wife Louise that "He was – as you know better than anyone – not only the greatest thinker, the most powerful genius of our time – but also a deep and incomparable heart; and having been close to him remains the precious memory of a whole life."

Mathematician [E.T. Bell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eric_Temple_Bell "Eric Temple Bell") titled Poincaré as "The Last Universalist", and noted his prowess in many fields, stating that:

> Poincaré was the last man to take practically all mathematics, both pure and applied, as his province ... few mathematicians have had the breadth of philosophical vision that Poincaré had and none is his superior in the gift of clear exposition.

When philosopher and mathematician [Bertrand Russell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bertrand_Russell "Bertrand Russell") was asked who was the greatest man that [France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/France "France") had produced in modern times, he instantly replied "Poincaré". Bell noted that if Poincaré had been as strong in practical science as he was in theoretical, he might have "made a fourth with the incomparable three, [Archimedes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archimedes "Archimedes"), [Newton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Newton "Isaac Newton"), and [Gauss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Friedrich_Gauss "Carl Friedrich Gauss")."

Bell further noted his powerful memory, one that was even superior to [Leonhard Euler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonhard_Euler "Leonhard Euler")'s, stating that:

> His principal diversion was reading, where his unusual talents first showed up. A book once read - at incredible speed - became a permanent possession, and he could always state the page and line where a particular thing occurred. He retained this powerful memory all his life. This rare faculty, which Poincaré shared with Euler who had it in a lesser degree, might be called visual or spatial memory. In temporal memory - the ability to recall with uncanny precision a sequence of events long passed — he was also unusually strong.

Bell notes the terrible eyesight of Poincaré, he almost completely remembered formulas and theorems by ear, and "unable to see the board distinctly when he became a student of advanced mathematics, he sat back and listened, following and remembering perfectly without taking notes - an easy feat for him, but one incomprehensible to most mathematicians."

The nomination archive reveals that Poincaré received a total of 51 nominations between 1904 and 1912, the year of his death. Of the 58 nominations for the 1910 Nobel Prize, 34 named Poincaré. Nominators included Nobel laureates [Hendrik Lorentz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hendrik_Lorentz "Hendrik Lorentz") and [Pieter Zeeman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pieter_Zeeman "Pieter Zeeman") (both of 1902), [Marie Curie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marie_Curie "Marie Curie") (of 1903), [Albert Michelson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Michelson "Albert Michelson") (of 1907), [Gabriel Lippmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gabriel_Lippmann "Gabriel Lippmann") (of 1908) and [Guglielmo Marconi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guglielmo_Marconi "Guglielmo Marconi") (of 1909).

The fact that renowned [theoretical physicists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theoretical_physics "Theoretical physics") like Poincaré, [Boltzmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludwig_Boltzmann "Ludwig Boltzmann") or [Gibbs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josiah_Willard_Gibbs "Josiah Willard Gibbs") were not awarded the [Nobel Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics") is seen as evidence that the Nobel committee had more regard for experimentation than theory. In Poincaré's case, several of those who nominated him pointed out that the greatest problem was to name a specific discovery, invention, or technique.

## Philosophy

[![First page of Science and hypothesis (1905)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b2/Poincar%C3%A9-16.jpg/120px-Poincar%C3%A9-16.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Poincaré-16.jpg)

First page of *Science and hypothesis* (1905)

Poincaré had [philosophical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophical "Philosophical") views opposite to those of [Bertrand Russell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bertrand_Russell "Bertrand Russell") and [Gottlob Frege](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gottlob_Frege "Gottlob Frege"), who believed that mathematics was a branch of [logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logic "Logic"). Poincaré strongly disagreed, claiming that [intuition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intuition_(knowledge) "Intuition (knowledge)") was the life of mathematics. Poincaré gives an interesting point of view in his 1902 book *[Science and Hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_and_Hypothesis "Science and Hypothesis")*:

> For a superficial observer, scientific truth is beyond the possibility of doubt; the logic of science is infallible, and if the scientists are sometimes mistaken, this is only from their mistaking its rule.

Poincaré believed that [arithmetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arithmetic "Arithmetic") is [synthetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytic/synthetic_distinction "Analytic/synthetic distinction"). He argued that [Peano's axioms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peano's_axioms "Peano's axioms") cannot be proven non-circularly with the [principle of induction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inductive_reasoning "Inductive reasoning") (Murzi, 1998), therefore concluding that arithmetic is *[a priori](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_priori_and_a_posteriori "A priori and a posteriori")* [synthetic and not analytic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytic–synthetic_distinction "Analytic–synthetic distinction"). Poincaré then went on to say that mathematics cannot be deduced from logic since it is not analytic. His views were similar to those of [Immanuel Kant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immanuel_Kant "Immanuel Kant") (Kolak, 2001, Folina 1992). He strongly opposed [Cantorian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cantorian "Cantorian") [set theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Set_theory "Set theory"), objecting to its use of [impredicative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impredicativity "Impredicativity") definitions. Moreover, in *La logique de l'infini*, Poincaré extended this criticism to Ernst Zermelo's axiomatization of set theory, arguing that Zermelo had not demonstrated that the axioms in his system were free from contradiction, and still required a prior intuition of *Menge* (set).

However, Poincaré did not share [Kantian views](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kantianism "Kantianism") in all branches of philosophy and mathematics. For example, in geometry, Poincaré believed that the structure of [non-Euclidean space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-Euclidean_geometry "Non-Euclidean geometry") can be known analytically. Poincaré held that convention plays an important role in physics. His view (and some later, more extreme versions of it) came to be known as "[conventionalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conventionalism "Conventionalism")". Poincaré believed that [Newton's first law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton's_first_law "Newton's first law") was not empirical but is a conventional framework assumption for [mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanics "Mechanics") (Gargani, 2012). He also believed that the geometry of [physical space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_space "Physical space") is conventional. He considered examples in which either the geometry of the physical fields or [gradients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gradients "Gradients") of temperature can be changed, either describing a space as non-Euclidean measured by rigid rulers, or as a [Euclidean space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_space "Euclidean space") where the rulers are expanded or shrunk by a [variable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variable_(mathematics) "Variable (mathematics)") heat distribution. However, Poincaré thought that we were so accustomed to [Euclidean geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_geometry "Euclidean geometry") that we would prefer to change the physical laws to save Euclidean geometry rather than shift to non-Euclidean physical geometry.

### Free will

Poincaré's famous lectures before the Société de Psychologie in Paris (published as *[Science and Hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_and_Hypothesis "Science and Hypothesis")*, *[The Value of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Value_of_Science "The Value of Science")*, and *Science and Method*) were cited by [Jacques Hadamard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques_Hadamard "Jacques Hadamard") as the source for the idea that [creativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creativity "Creativity") and [invention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Innovation "Innovation") consist of two mental stages, first random combinations of possible solutions to a problem, followed by a [critical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critical_thinking "Critical thinking") [evaluation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evaluation "Evaluation").

Although he most often spoke of a [deterministic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deterministic "Deterministic") [universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universe "Universe"), Poincaré said that the [subconscious](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subconscious "Subconscious") generation of new possibilities involves [chance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Randomness "Randomness").

> It is certain that the combinations which present themselves to the mind in a kind of sudden illumination after a somewhat prolonged period of unconscious work are generally useful and fruitful combinations... all the combinations are formed as a result of the automatic action of the subliminal ego, but those only which are interesting find their way into the field of consciousness... A few only are harmonious, and consequently at once useful and beautiful, and they will be capable of affecting the geometrician's special sensibility I have been speaking of; which, once aroused, will direct our attention upon them, and will thus give them the opportunity of becoming conscious... In the subliminal ego, on the contrary, there reigns what I would call liberty, if one could give this name to the mere absence of discipline and to disorder born of chance.

Poincaré's two stages—random combinations followed by selection—became the basis for [Daniel Dennett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Dennett "Daniel Dennett")'s two-stage model of [free will](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_will "Free will").

### Theorems

Here is a list of theorems proved by Poincaré:

- [Poincaré's recurrence theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poincaré's_recurrence_theorem "Poincaré's recurrence theorem"): certain systems will, after a sufficiently long but finite time, return to a state very close to the initial state.
- [Poincaré–Bendixson theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poincaré–Bendixson_theorem "Poincaré–Bendixson theorem"): a statement about the long-term behaviour of orbits of continuous dynamical systems on the plane, cylinder, or two-sphere.
- [Poincaré–Hopf theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poincaré–Hopf_theorem "Poincaré–Hopf theorem"): a generalization of the hairy-ball theorem, which states that there is no smooth vector field on a sphere having no sources or sinks.
- [Poincaré–Lefschetz duality theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poincaré–Lefschetz_duality_theorem "Poincaré–Lefschetz duality theorem"): a version of Poincaré duality in geometric topology, applying to a manifold with boundary
- [Poincaré separation theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poincaré_separation_theorem "Poincaré separation theorem"): gives the upper and lower bounds of eigenvalues of a real symmetric matrix B'AB that can be considered as the orthogonal projection of a larger real symmetric matrix A onto a linear subspace spanned by the columns of B.
- [Poincaré–Birkhoff theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poincaré–Birkhoff_theorem "Poincaré–Birkhoff theorem"): every area-preserving, orientation-preserving homeomorphism of an annulus that rotates the two boundaries in opposite directions has at least two fixed points.
- [Poincaré–Birkhoff–Witt theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poincaré–Birkhoff–Witt_theorem "Poincaré–Birkhoff–Witt theorem"): an explicit description of the universal enveloping algebra of a Lie algebra.
- [Poincaré–Bjerknes circulation theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kelvin's_circulation_theorem#Poincaré–Bjerknes_circulation_theorem "Kelvin's circulation theorem"): theorem about a conservation of quantity for the rotating frame.
- [Poincaré conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poincaré_conjecture "Poincaré conjecture") (now a theorem): Every simply connected, closed 3-manifold is homeomorphic to the 3-sphere.
- [Poincaré–Miranda theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poincaré–Miranda_theorem "Poincaré–Miranda theorem"): a generalization of the [intermediate value theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intermediate_value_theorem "Intermediate value theorem") to *n* dimensions.
