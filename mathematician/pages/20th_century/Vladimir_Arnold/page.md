**Vladimir Igorevich Arnold** (or **Arnol'd**; Russian: Влади́мир И́горевич Арно́льд, IPA: [[vlɐˈdʲimʲɪr ˈiɡərʲɪvʲɪtɕ ɐrˈnolʲt]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Russian "Help:IPA/Russian"); 12 June 1937 – 3 June 2010) was a Soviet and Russian mathematician. He is best known for the [Kolmogorov–Arnold–Moser theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kolmogorov–Arnold–Moser_theorem "Kolmogorov–Arnold–Moser theorem") regarding the [stability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stability_theory "Stability theory") of [integrable systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrable_system "Integrable system"), and contributed to several areas, including geometrical theory of [dynamical systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamical_systems_theory "Dynamical systems theory"), [algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebra "Algebra"), [catastrophe theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catastrophe_theory "Catastrophe theory"), [topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topology "Topology"), [real algebraic geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_algebraic_geometry "Real algebraic geometry"), [symplectic geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symplectic_geometry "Symplectic geometry"), [differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_equation "Differential equation"), [classical mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_mechanics "Classical mechanics"), [differential-geometric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_geometry "Differential geometry") approach to [hydrodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrodynamics "Hydrodynamics"), [geometric analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric_analysis "Geometric analysis") and [singularity theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singularity_theory "Singularity theory"), including posing the [ADE classification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ADE_classification "ADE classification") problem. In his later years he shifted his research interests, investigating [discrete mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_mathematics "Discrete mathematics").

His first main result was the solution of [Hilbert's thirteenth problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilbert's_thirteenth_problem "Hilbert's thirteenth problem") in 1957 when he was 19. He co-founded three new [branches of mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Areas_of_mathematics "Areas of mathematics"): [topological Galois theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_Galois_theory "Topological Galois theory") (with his student [Askold Khovanskii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Askold_Khovanskii "Askold Khovanskii")), [KAM theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kolmogorov–Arnold–Moser_theorem#KAM_theory "Kolmogorov–Arnold–Moser theorem") (with [Andrey Kolmogorov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrey_Kolmogorov "Andrey Kolmogorov") and [Jürgen Moser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jürgen_Moser "Jürgen Moser")) and [symplectic topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symplectic_topology "Symplectic topology").

Arnold was also a populariser of mathematics. Through his lectures, seminars, and as the author of several textbooks (such as *[Mathematical Methods of Classical Mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_Methods_of_Classical_Mechanics "Mathematical Methods of Classical Mechanics")* and *[Ordinary Differential Equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordinary_Differential_Equations_(textbook) "Ordinary Differential Equations (textbook)")*) and popular mathematics books, he influenced many mathematicians and physicists. Many of his books were translated into English. His views on education were opposed to those of [Bourbaki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolas_Bourbaki "Nicolas Bourbaki").

A controversial and often quoted dictum of his is "[Mathematics is the part of physics where experiments are cheap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relationship_between_mathematics_and_physics#Mathematics_is_physics "Relationship between mathematics and physics")". The [Arnold principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stigler's_law_of_eponymy "Stigler's law of eponymy"): "Discoveries are rarely attributed to the correct person" is also named after him.

Arnold worked at the [Moscow State University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moscow_State_University "Moscow State University") from 1961 to 1986, at the [Steklov Mathematical Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steklov_Mathematical_Institute "Steklov Mathematical Institute") since 1986, and at the [Paris Dauphine University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paris_Dauphine_University "Paris Dauphine University") since 1993. He was one of the founders of the [Independent University of Moscow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_University_of_Moscow "Independent University of Moscow").

Arnold received many major prizes, including the inaugural [Crafoord Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crafoord_Prize "Crafoord Prize") in 1982 (with [Louis Nirenberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Nirenberg "Louis Nirenberg")), the [Wolf Prize in Mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolf_Prize_in_Mathematics "Wolf Prize in Mathematics") in 2001 and the [Shaw Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shaw_Prize#Mathematical_sciences "Shaw Prize") in 2008 (with [Ludwig Faddeev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludwig_Faddeev "Ludwig Faddeev")).

## Early life

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/fa/%D0%92%D0%BB%D0%B0%D0%B4%D0%B8%D0%BC%D0%B8%D1%80_%D0%90%D1%80%D0%BD%D0%BE%D0%BB%D1%8C%D0%B4_%D0%B2_1963_%D0%B3.jpg/250px-%D0%92%D0%BB%D0%B0%D0%B4%D0%B8%D0%BC%D0%B8%D1%80_%D0%90%D1%80%D0%BD%D0%BE%D0%BB%D1%8C%D0%B4_%D0%B2_1963_%D0%B3.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Владимир_Арнольд_в_1963_г.jpg)

Arnold in 1963

Vladimir Igorevich Arnold was born on 12 June 1937 in [Odessa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odessa "Odessa"), [Ukrainian SSR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukrainian_SSR "Ukrainian SSR"), [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union "Soviet Union") (now Odesa, Ukraine). His father was [Igor Vladimirovich Arnold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Igor_Vladimirovich_Arnold?action=edit&redlink=1 "Igor Vladimirovich Arnold") (1900–1948), a mathematician known for his work in [mathematical education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_education "Mathematical education") and who learned [algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebra "Algebra") from [Emmy Noether](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emmy_Noether "Emmy Noether") in the late 1920s. His mother was Nina Alexandrovna Arnold (1909–1986, [née](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_name#Maiden_and_married_names "Birth name") Isakovich), a Jewish art historian.

While a school student, Arnold once asked his father why the multiplication of two negative numbers yielded a positive number, and his father provided an answer involving the [field properties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_(mathematics)#Classic_definition "Field (mathematics)") of [real numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_number "Real number") and the preservation of the [distributive property](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distributive_property "Distributive property"). Arnold was deeply disappointed with this answer, and developed an aversion to the [axiomatic method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axiomatic_method "Axiomatic method") that lasted his whole life. When Arnold was thirteen, his uncle Nikolai B. Zhitkov, who was an engineer, told him about [calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calculus "Calculus") and how it could be used to understand some physical phenomena. This contributed to sparking his interest in mathematics, and he started to study the mathematics books his father had left him, which included some works by [Leonhard Euler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonhard_Euler "Leonhard Euler") and [Charles Hermite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Hermite "Charles Hermite").

Arnold entered [Moscow State University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moscow_State_University "Moscow State University") in 1954. Among his teachers there were [A. N. Kolmogorov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrey_Kolmogorov "Andrey Kolmogorov"), [I. M. Gelfand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Israel_Gelfand "Israel Gelfand"), [L. S. Pontriagin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lev_Pontryagin "Lev Pontryagin") and [Pavel Alexandrov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pavel_Alexandrov "Pavel Alexandrov"). While a student of Andrey Kolmogorov at Moscow State University and still a teenager, Arnold showed in 1957 that any [continuous function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_function "Continuous function") of several variables can be constructed with a finite number of two-variable functions, thereby solving [Hilbert's thirteenth problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilbert's_thirteenth_problem "Hilbert's thirteenth problem"). This is the [Kolmogorov–Arnold representation theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kolmogorov–Arnold_representation_theorem "Kolmogorov–Arnold representation theorem").

## Mathematical work

Arnold obtained his PhD in 1961, with [Andrey Kolmogorov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrey_Kolmogorov "Andrey Kolmogorov") as his advisor (thesis: *On The Representation of Continuous Functions of 3 Variables By The Superpositions of Continuous Functions of 2 Variables*).

He became an academician of the [Academy of Sciences of the Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academy_of_Sciences_of_the_Soviet_Union "Academy of Sciences of the Soviet Union") ([Russian Academy of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Academy_of_Science "Russian Academy of Science") since 1991) in 1990. Arnold can be considered to have initiated the theory of [symplectic topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symplectic_topology "Symplectic topology") as a distinct discipline. The [Arnold conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arnold_conjecture "Arnold conjecture") on the number of fixed points of [Hamiltonian symplectomorphisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symplectomorphism "Symplectomorphism") and [Lagrangian intersections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagrangian_intersection?action=edit&redlink=1 "Lagrangian intersection") was also a motivation in the development of [Floer homology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floer_homology "Floer homology").

Arnold worked at the Steklov Mathematical Institute in Moscow and at [Paris Dauphine University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paris_Dauphine_University "Paris Dauphine University") until his death. He supervised 46 PhD students, including [Rifkat Bogdanov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rifkat_Bogdanov "Rifkat Bogdanov"), [Alexander Givental](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Givental "Alexander Givental"), [Victor Goryunov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victor_Goryunov "Victor Goryunov"), [Sabir Gusein-Zade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sabir_Gusein-Zade "Sabir Gusein-Zade"), [Emil Horozov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emil_Horozov "Emil Horozov"), [Yulij Ilyashenko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yulij_Ilyashenko "Yulij Ilyashenko"), [Boris Khesin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boris_Khesin "Boris Khesin"), [Askold Khovanskii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Askold_Khovanskii "Askold Khovanskii"), [Nikolay Nekhoroshev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nikolay_Nekhoroshev "Nikolay Nekhoroshev"), [Boris Shapiro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boris_Shapiro_(mathematician) "Boris Shapiro (mathematician)"), [Alexander Varchenko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Varchenko "Alexander Varchenko"), [Victor Vassiliev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victor_Anatolyevich_Vassiliev "Victor Anatolyevich Vassiliev") and [Vladimir Zakalyukin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladimir_Zakalyukin "Vladimir Zakalyukin").

Arnold worked on [dynamical systems theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamical_systems_theory "Dynamical systems theory"), [catastrophe theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catastrophe_theory "Catastrophe theory"), [topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topology "Topology"), [algebraic geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_geometry "Algebraic geometry"), [symplectic geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symplectic_geometry "Symplectic geometry"), [differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_equation "Differential equation"), [classical mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_mechanics "Classical mechanics"), [hydrodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrodynamics "Hydrodynamics") and [singularity theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singularity_theory "Singularity theory"). [Michèle Audin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michèle_Audin "Michèle Audin") described him as "a geometer in the widest possible sense of the word" and said that "he was very fast to make connections between different fields".

### Hilbert's thirteenth problem

[Hilbert's thirteenth problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilbert's_thirteenth_problem "Hilbert's thirteenth problem") asks whether every [continuous function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_function "Continuous function") of three variables can be expressed as a [composition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Function_composition "Function composition") of finitely many continuous functions of two variables. The affirmative answer to this question was given in 1957 by Arnold, then nineteen years old and a student of [Andrey Kolmogorov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrey_Kolmogorov "Andrey Kolmogorov"). Kolmogorov had shown the previous year that any function of several variables can be constructed with a finite number of three-variable functions. Arnold then expanded on this work to show that only two-variable functions were required, thus answering Hilbert's question for the class of continuous functions.

### Dynamical systems

[Jürgen Moser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jürgen_Moser "Jürgen Moser") and Arnold expanded the ideas of Kolmogorov (who was inspired by questions of [Henri Poincaré](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_Poincaré "Henri Poincaré")) and gave rise to what is now known as [Kolmogorov–Arnold–Moser theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kolmogorov–Arnold–Moser_theorem "Kolmogorov–Arnold–Moser theorem") (or "KAM theory"), which concerns the persistence of some quasi-periodic motions (nearly integrable [Hamiltonian systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamiltonian_system "Hamiltonian system")) when they are perturbed. KAM theory shows that, despite the perturbations, such systems can be stable over an infinite period, and specifies what the conditions for this are.

In 1961, he introduced [Arnold tongues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arnold_tongue "Arnold tongue"); they are observed in a large variety of natural phenomena that involve oscillating quantities, such as concentration of enzymes and substrates in biological processes.

In 1964, Arnold introduced the [Arnold web](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arnold_web?action=edit&redlink=1 "Arnold web"), the first example of a stochastic web.

In 1974, Arnold [proved](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_proof "Mathematical proof") the [Liouville–Arnold theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liouville–Arnold_theorem "Liouville–Arnold theorem"), now a classic result deeply geometric in character.

In the 1980s, Arnold reformulated [Hilbert's sixteenth problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilbert's_sixteenth_problem "Hilbert's sixteenth problem"), proposing its infinitesimal version (the [Hilbert–Arnold problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilbert–Arnold_problem "Hilbert–Arnold problem")) that inspired many deep works in dynamical systems theory by mathematicians seeking its solution.

### Singularity theory

In 1965, Arnold attended [René Thom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/René_Thom "René Thom")'s seminar on [catastrophe theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catastrophe_theory "Catastrophe theory"). He later said of it: "I am deeply indebted to Thom, whose singularity seminar at the [Institut des Hautes Etudes Scientifiques](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institut_des_Hautes_Études_Scientifiques "Institut des Hautes Études Scientifiques"), which I frequented throughout the year 1965, profoundly changed my mathematical universe." After this event, [singularity theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singularity_theory "Singularity theory") became one of the major interests of Arnold and his students. Among his most famous results in this area is his classification of simple singularities, contained in his paper "Normal forms of functions near degenerate critical points, the Weyl groups of Ak,Dk,Ek and Lagrangian singularities".

### Fluid dynamics

In 1966, Arnold published the paper "*Sur la géométrie différentielle des groupes de Lie de dimension infinie et ses applications à l'hydrodynamique des fluides parfaits*" ('On the [differential geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_geometry "Differential geometry") of [infinite-dimensional Lie groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite-dimensional_Lie_group "Infinite-dimensional Lie group") and its applications to the [hydrodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrodynamics "Hydrodynamics") of [perfect fluids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perfect_fluid "Perfect fluid")'), in which he presented a common geometric interpretation for both the [Euler's equations for rotating rigid bodies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler's_equations_(rigid_body_dynamics) "Euler's equations (rigid body dynamics)") and the [Euler's equations of fluid dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler_equations_(fluid_dynamics) "Euler equations (fluid dynamics)"); this linked topics previously thought to be unrelated, and enabled mathematical solutions to many questions related to flows and [turbulence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turbulence "Turbulence").

### Real algebraic geometry

In 1971, Arnold published "On the arrangement of ovals of real plane algebraic curves, involutions of four-dimensional smooth manifolds, and the arithmetic of integral quadratic forms", which gave new life to [real algebraic geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_algebraic_geometry "Real algebraic geometry"). In it, he made major advances in towards a solution to [Gudkov's conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gudkov's_conjecture "Gudkov's conjecture"), by finding a connection between it and [four-dimensional topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low-dimensional_topology#Four_dimensions "Low-dimensional topology"). The [conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjecture "Conjecture") was later fully solved by [V. A. Rokhlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladimir_Abramovich_Rokhlin "Vladimir Abramovich Rokhlin") building on Arnold's work.

### Symplectic geometry

The [Arnold conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arnold_conjecture "Arnold conjecture"), linking the number of fixed points of Hamiltonian [symplectomorphisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symplectomorphism "Symplectomorphism") and the topology of the subjacent [manifolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manifold "Manifold"), was the motivating source of many of the pioneer studies in [symplectic topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symplectic_topology "Symplectic topology"). He also proposed the [nearby Lagrangian conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nearby_Lagrangian_conjecture "Nearby Lagrangian conjecture"), a still open problem in mathematics.

According to [Michèle Audin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michèle_Audin "Michèle Audin"), the birth-date of symplectic topology was 27 October 1965, which is the day Arnold's paper "Sur une propriété topologique des applications globalement canoniques de la mécanique classique" was presented to the [Paris Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paris_Academy_of_Sciences "Paris Academy of Sciences").

### Topology

According to [Victor Vassiliev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victor_Vassiliev "Victor Vassiliev"), Arnold "worked comparatively little on topology for topology's sake," being motivated by problems on other areas of mathematics where [topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topology "Topology") could be of use. His contributions include the invention of a topological form of the [Abel–Ruffini theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abel–Ruffini_theorem "Abel–Ruffini theorem") and the initial development of some of the consequent ideas, a work which resulted in the creation of the field of [topological Galois theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_Galois_theory "Topological Galois theory") in the 1960s.

### Theory of plane curves

According to [Marcel Berger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcel_Berger "Marcel Berger"), Arnold revolutionised [plane curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_curve "Plane curve") theory. He developed the theory of smooth closed plane curves in the 1990s. Among his contributions are the introduction of the three [Arnold invariants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arnold_invariants "Arnold invariants") of plane curves: *J*+, *J*− and *St*.

### Discrete mathematics

In the last years of his life, Arnold's interests shifted to [discrete mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_mathematics "Discrete mathematics"). He investigated [number theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_theory "Number theory") and [combinatorics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combinatorics "Combinatorics"), producing around twenty papers on these topics, according to [Anatoly Vershik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatoly_Vershik "Anatoly Vershik"). Arnold's conjecture of a [matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_(mathematics) "Matrix (mathematics)") generalization of [Fermat's little theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermat's_little_theorem "Fermat's little theorem") dates from this period.

### Other

In 1995, Arnold conjectured the existence of the [gömböc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gömböc "Gömböc"), a body with one stable and one unstable [point of equilibrium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanical_equilibrium "Mechanical equilibrium") when resting on a flat surface. This conjecture was proved by [Gábor Domokos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gábor_Domokos "Gábor Domokos") in 2006.

In [classical mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_mechanics "Classical mechanics"), Arnold generalised the results of [Isaac Newton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Newton "Isaac Newton"), [Pierre-Simon Laplace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre-Simon_Laplace "Pierre-Simon Laplace"), and [James Ivory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Ivory_(mathematician) "James Ivory (mathematician)") on the [shell theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shell_theorem "Shell theorem"), showing it to be applicable to algebraic hypersurfaces.

In [algebraic geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_geometry "Algebraic geometry"), [Arnold's strange duality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arnold's_strange_duality?action=edit&redlink=1 "Arnold's strange duality") was one of the first examples of [mirror symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mirror_symmetry_(string_theory) "Mirror symmetry (string theory)") (for [K3 surfaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K3_surface "K3 surface")).

In [magnetohydrodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetohydrodynamics "Magnetohydrodynamics"), Arnold and E. I. Korkina investigated in 1983 the dynamo property of the [ABC flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arnold–Beltrami–Childress_flow "Arnold–Beltrami–Childress flow").

The [Arnold complexity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arnold_complexity?action=edit&redlink=1 "Arnold complexity") in [dynamical systems theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamical_systems_theory "Dynamical systems theory"), and the [Arnold's stability theorems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arnold's_stability_theorems?action=edit&redlink=1 "Arnold's stability theorems") in [analysis of PDEs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_differential_equation "Partial differential equation") are named after him.

## Popular mathematical writings

Arnold is well known for his lucid writing style, combining mathematical rigour with physical intuition, and an easy conversational style of teaching and education. His writings present a fresh, often [geometric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric "Geometric") approach to traditional mathematical topics like [ordinary differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordinary_differential_equation "Ordinary differential equation"), and his many textbooks have been influential in the development of new areas of mathematics. The standard criticism about Arnold's pedagogy is that his books "are beautiful treatments of their subjects that are appreciated by experts, but too many details are omitted for students to learn the mathematics required to prove the statements that he so effortlessly justifies." His defence was that his books are meant to teach the subject to "those who truly wish to understand it".

Arnold was an outspoken critic of the trend towards high levels of abstraction in mathematics during the middle of the 20th century. He strongly believed that this approach—popularly implemented by the [Bourbaki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolas_Bourbaki "Nicolas Bourbaki") school in France—initially had a negative impact on French [mathematical education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_education "Mathematical education") and later in other countries. He was very concerned about what he saw as the divorce of mathematics from the [natural sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_science "Natural science") in the 20th century. Arnold was very interested in the [history of mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_mathematics "History of mathematics"), and in an interview, remarked that he had learned much of what he knew about mathematics through the study of [Felix Klein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Felix_Klein "Felix Klein")'s book *[Development of Mathematics in the 19th Century](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vorlesungen_über_die_Entwicklung_der_Mathematik_im_19._Jahrhundert "Vorlesungen über die Entwicklung der Mathematik im 19. Jahrhundert")* –a book he often recommended to his students. He studied the works of [Christiaan Huygens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christiaan_Huygens "Christiaan Huygens"), [Isaac Newton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Newton "Isaac Newton") and [Henri Poincaré](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_Poincaré "Henri Poincaré"), and reported finding ideas that had yet to be explored in the works of Newton and Poincaré.

## Later life and death

In 1999 Arnold suffered a serious bicycle accident in Paris, resulting in a [traumatic brain injury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traumatic_brain_injury "Traumatic brain injury"). He regained consciousness after a few weeks but had [amnesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amnesia "Amnesia") and for some time could not even recognise his own wife at the hospital. He went on to make a good recovery.

To his students and colleagues Arnold was known also for his sense of humour. For example, once at his seminar in Moscow, at the beginning of the school year, when he usually was formulating new problems, he said:

> There is a general principle that a stupid man can ask such questions to which one hundred wise men would not be able to answer. In accordance with this principle I shall formulate some problems.

Arnold died of [acute pancreatitis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acute_pancreatitis "Acute pancreatitis") on 3 June 2010 in Paris, nine days before his 73rd birthday. He was buried on 15 June in Moscow, at the [Novodevichy Monastery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Novodevichy_Convent "Novodevichy Convent").

At Arnold's tombstone are engraved a quote by him and mathematical formulae from his discoveries.

In a telegram to Arnold's family, [Russian president](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_president "Russian president") [Dmitry Medvedev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dmitry_Medvedev "Dmitry Medvedev") stated:

> The death of Vladimir Arnold, one of the greatest mathematicians of our time, is an irretrievable loss for world science. It is difficult to overestimate the contribution made by academician Arnold to modern mathematics and the prestige of Russian science.
>
> Teaching had a special place in Vladimir Arnold's life and he had great influence as an enlightened mentor who taught several generations of talented scientists.
>
> The memory of Vladimir Arnold will forever remain in the hearts of his colleagues, friends and students, as well as everyone who knew and admired this brilliant man.

The [minor planet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minor_planet "Minor planet") [10031 Vladarnolda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/10031_Vladarnolda "10031 Vladarnolda") was named after him in 1981 by [Lyudmila Georgievna Karachkina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyudmila_Georgievna_Karachkina "Lyudmila Georgievna Karachkina").

The Arnold Fellowships, of the [London Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Institute "London Institute") are named after him.

He was a plenary speaker at both the 1974 and 1983 [International Congress of Mathematicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Congress_of_Mathematicians "International Congress of Mathematicians") in Vancouver and [Warsaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warsaw "Warsaw"), respectively.

### Fields Medal omission

Arnold was nominated for the 1974 [Fields Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fields_Medal "Fields Medal"), one of the highest honours a mathematician could receive, but interference from the Soviet government led to it being withdrawn. Arnold's public opposition to the persecution of [dissidents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissident#Eastern_bloc "Dissident") had led him into direct conflict with influential Soviet officials, and he suffered persecution himself, including not being allowed to leave the Soviet Union during most of the 1970s and 1980s.

## Personal life

Arnold was married to Eleonora Arnold. They had two children, Igor Arnold and Dmitry Arnold.
