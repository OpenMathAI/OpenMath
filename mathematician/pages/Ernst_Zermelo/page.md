**Ernst Friedrich Ferdinand Zermelo** ([/zɜːrˈmɛloʊ/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English"); German: [[tsɛɐ̯ˈmeːlo]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Standard_German "Help:IPA/Standard German"); 27 July 1871 – 21 May 1953) was a German [logician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logic "Logic") and [mathematician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematician "Mathematician"), whose work has major implications for the [foundations of mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foundations_of_mathematics "Foundations of mathematics"). He is known for his role in developing [Zermelo–Fraenkel axiomatic set theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zermelo–Fraenkel_set_theory "Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory") and his proof of the [well-ordering theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Well-ordering_theorem "Well-ordering theorem"). Furthermore, his 1929 work on ranking chess players is the first description of a model for [pairwise comparison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pairwise_comparison_(psychology) "Pairwise comparison (psychology)") that continues to have a profound impact on various applied fields utilizing this method.

## Life

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7d/Ernst_Zermelo.jpeg/250px-Ernst_Zermelo.jpeg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Ernst_Zermelo.jpeg)

Ernst Zermelo in Freiburg (1953)

Ernst Zermelo graduated from Berlin's Luisenstädtisches Gymnasium (now [Heinrich-Schliemann-Oberschule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heinrich-Schliemann-Oberschule?action=edit&redlink=1 "Heinrich-Schliemann-Oberschule")) in 1889. He then studied [mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics "Mathematics"), [physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics "Physics") and [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy "Philosophy") at the [University of Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Berlin "University of Berlin"), the [University of Halle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Halle "University of Halle"), and the [University of Freiburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Freiburg "University of Freiburg"). He finished his doctorate in 1894 at the University of Berlin, awarded for a dissertation on the [calculus of variations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calculus_of_variations "Calculus of variations") (*Untersuchungen zur Variationsrechnung*). Zermelo remained at the University of Berlin, where he was appointed assistant to [Planck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Planck "Max Planck"), under whose guidance he began to study [hydrodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrodynamics "Hydrodynamics"). In 1897, Zermelo went to the [University of Göttingen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Göttingen "University of Göttingen"), at that time the leading centre for mathematical research in the world, where he completed his [habilitation thesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habilitation_thesis "Habilitation thesis") in 1899.

In 1910, Zermelo left Göttingen upon being appointed to the chair of mathematics at the [University of Zurich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Zurich "University of Zurich"), which he resigned in 1916.
He was appointed to an honorary chair at the [University of Freiburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Freiburg "University of Freiburg") in 1926, which he resigned in 1935 because he disapproved of [Adolf Hitler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adolf_Hitler "Adolf Hitler")'s regime. At the end of [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II") and at his request, Zermelo was reinstated to his honorary position in Freiburg.

## Research in set theory

In 1900, in the Paris conference of the [International Congress of Mathematicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Congress_of_Mathematicians "International Congress of Mathematicians"), [David Hilbert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Hilbert "David Hilbert") challenged the mathematical community with his famous [Hilbert's problems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilbert's_problems "Hilbert's problems"), a list of 23 unsolved fundamental questions which mathematicians should attack during the coming century. The first of these, a problem of [set theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Set_theory "Set theory"), was the [continuum hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuum_hypothesis "Continuum hypothesis") introduced by [Cantor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georg_Cantor "Georg Cantor") in 1878, and in the course of its statement Hilbert also mentioned the need to prove the [well-ordering theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Well-ordering_theorem "Well-ordering theorem").

Zermelo began to work on the problems of set theory under Hilbert's influence and in 1902 published his first work concerning the addition of [transfinite cardinals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardinal_numbers "Cardinal numbers"). By that time he had also discovered the so-called [Russell paradox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russell_paradox "Russell paradox") as evidenced by Husserl’s *Nachlass*. In 1904, he succeeded in taking the first step suggested by Hilbert towards the continuum hypothesis when he proved the [well-ordering theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Well-ordering_theorem "Well-ordering theorem") (*every set can be well ordered*). This result brought fame to Zermelo, who was appointed Professor in Göttingen, in 1905. His proof of the [well-ordering theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Well-ordering_theorem "Well-ordering theorem"), based on the powerset axiom and the [axiom of choice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axiom_of_choice "Axiom of choice"), was not accepted by all mathematicians, mostly because the axiom of choice was a paradigm of non-constructive mathematics. In 1908, Zermelo succeeded in producing an improved proof making use of Dedekind's notion of the "chain" of a set, which became more widely accepted; this was mainly because that same year he also offered an [axiomatization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axiomatization "Axiomatization") of set theory.

Zermelo began to axiomatize set theory in 1905; in 1908, he published his results despite his failure to prove the consistency of his axiomatic system. See the article on [Zermelo set theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zermelo_set_theory "Zermelo set theory") for an outline of this paper, together with the original axioms, and the original numbering.

In 1922, [Abraham Fraenkel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraham_Fraenkel "Abraham Fraenkel") and [Thoralf Skolem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thoralf_Skolem "Thoralf Skolem") independently expanded Zermelo's axiom system, adding the axioms of [replacement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axiom_schema_of_replacement "Axiom schema of replacement") and [regularity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axiom_of_regularity "Axiom of regularity"). The resulting system, now called [Zermelo–Fraenkel axioms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zermelo–Fraenkel_set_theory "Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory") (ZF), is now the most commonly used system for [axiomatic set theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axiomatic_set_theory "Axiomatic set theory").

## Zermelo's navigation problem

Proposed in 1931, [Zermelo's navigation problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zermelo's_navigation_problem "Zermelo's navigation problem") is a classic [optimal control](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optimal_control "Optimal control") problem. The problem deals with a boat navigating on a body of water, originating from a point O to a destination point D. The boat is capable of a certain maximum speed, and we want to derive the best possible control to reach D in the least possible time.

Without considering external forces such as current and wind, the optimal control is for the boat to always head towards D. Its path then is a line segment from O to D, which is trivially optimal. With consideration of current and wind, if the combined force applied to the boat is non-zero, the control for no current and wind does not yield the optimal path.
