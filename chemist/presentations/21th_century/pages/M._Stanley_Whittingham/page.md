**Sir Michael Stanley Whittingham** (born 22 December 1941) is a British-American [chemist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemist "Chemist"). He is a professor of chemistry and past founding director of both the Institute for Materials Research and the [Materials Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Materials_Science "Materials Science") and Engineering program at [Binghamton University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binghamton_University "Binghamton University"), [State University of New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_University_of_New_York "State University of New York"). He also served as director of the Northeastern Center for Chemical Energy Storage (NECCES) of the [U.S. Department of Energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Department_of_Energy "U.S. Department of Energy") at Binghamton. He was awarded the [Nobel Prize in Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Chemistry "Nobel Prize in Chemistry") in 2019 alongside [Akira Yoshino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akira_Yoshino "Akira Yoshino") and [John B. Goodenough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_B._Goodenough "John B. Goodenough").

Whittingham is a key figure in the history of [lithium-ion batteries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithium-ion_batteries "Lithium-ion batteries"), which are used in everything from mobile phones to electric vehicles. He discovered [intercalation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intercalation_(chemistry) "Intercalation (chemistry)") electrodes and thoroughly described intercalation reactions in rechargeable batteries in the 1970s. He holds the patents on the concept of using intercalation chemistry in high power-density, highly reversible lithium-ion batteries. He also invented the first rechargeable lithium metal battery (LMB), patented in 1977 and assigned to [Exxon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exxon "Exxon") for commercialization in small devices and electric vehicles. Whittingham's rechargeable lithium metal battery is based on a LiAl anode and an intercalation-type TiS2 cathode. His work on lithium batteries laid the foundation for others' developments, so he is called the founding father of lithium-ion batteries.

## Education and career

Whittingham was born in the Carlton suburb of [Nottingham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nottingham "Nottingham"), [England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/England "England"), on 22 December 1941. His father was a civil engineer, the first in the family to go to college. His mother Dorothy Mary (née Findley) was a chemist before marriage. He was educated at [Stamford School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stamford_School "Stamford School") from 1951 to 1960, before going up to [New College, Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_College,_Oxford "New College, Oxford") to read chemistry. At the [University of Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Oxford "University of Oxford"), he took his [BA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_Arts "Bachelor of Arts") (1964), [MA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master_of_Arts_(Oxbridge) "Master of Arts (Oxbridge)") (1967), and [DPhil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Philosophy "Doctor of Philosophy") (1968). After completing his graduate studies, Whittingham became a postdoctoral fellow at [Stanford University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford_University "Stanford University"). He worked 16 years for Exxon Research & Engineering Company and four years working for [Schlumberger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schlumberger "Schlumberger") prior to becoming a professor at [Binghamton University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binghamton_University "Binghamton University").

From 1994 to 2000, he served as the university's vice provost for research. He also served as vice-chair of the Research Foundation of the State University of New York for six years. He is a Distinguished Professor of Chemistry and Materials Science and Engineering at Binghamton University. Whittingham was named Chief Scientific Officer of NAATBatt International in 2017.

Whittingham co-chaired the DOE study of Chemical Energy Storage in 2007, and is a director of the Northeastern Center for Chemical Energy Storage (NECCES), a U.S. Department of Energy [Energy Frontier Research Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_Frontier_Research_Center "Energy Frontier Research Center") (EFRC) at Binghamton. In 2014, NECCES was awarded $12.8 million, from the U.S. Department of Energy to help accelerate scientific breakthroughs needed to build the 21st-century economy. In 2018, NECCES was granted another $3 million by the Department of Energy to continue its research on batteries. The NECCES team is using the funding to improve energy-storage materials and to develop new materials that are "cheaper, environmentally friendly, and able to store more energy than current materials can".

## Research

Whittingham, in a multidisciplinary group led by Fred Gamble, PhD, conceived the [intercalation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intercalation_(chemistry) "Intercalation (chemistry)") electrode. Exxon manufactured Whittingham's lithium-ion battery in the 1970s, based on a [titanium disulfide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Titanium_disulfide "Titanium disulfide") cathode and a lithium-aluminum anode. The battery had high energy density and the diffusion of lithium ions into the titanium disulfide cathode was reversible, making the battery rechargeable. In addition, titanium disulfide has a particularly fast rate of lithium ion diffusion into the crystal lattice. Exxon threw its resources behind the commercialization of a Li/LiBX/ TiS2 battery. However, The small market size led Exxon to end the project, and license the technology to 3 companies, one in each of the USA, Japan and Germany. Whittingham and his team continued to publish their work in academic journals of electrochemistry and solid-state physics. He left Exxon in 1984, following Gamble to Schlumberger where he worked as a manager. In 1988, he became Professor at the Chemistry Department, Binghamton University, U.S. to pursue his academic interests.

"All these batteries are called intercalation batteries. It’s like putting jam in a sandwich. In the chemical terms, it means you have a crystal structure, and we can put lithium ions in, take them out, and the structure’s exactly the same afterwards," Whittingham said. "We retain the crystal structure. That’s what makes these lithium batteries so good, allows them to cycle for so long."

Lithium batteries have limited capacity because less than one lithium-ion/electron is reversibly intercalated per transition metal redox center. To achieve higher energy densities, one approach is to go beyond the one-electron redox intercalation reactions. Whittingham's research has advanced to multi-electron intercalation reactions, which can increase the storage capacity by intercalating multiple lithium ions. A few multi-electron intercalation materials have been successfully developed by Whittingham, like LiVOPO4/VOPO4. The multivalent [vanadium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vanadium "Vanadium") cation (V3+<->V5+) plays an important role to accomplish the multi-electron reactions. These promising materials shine lights on the battery industry to increase energy density rapidly.

Whittingham received the [Young Author Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_Hackerman_Young_Author_Award "Norman Hackerman Young Author Award") from [The Electrochemical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Electrochemical_Society "The Electrochemical Society") in 1971, the *Battery Research Award* in 2003, and was elected a Fellow in 2004. In 2010, he was listed as one of the Top 40 innovators for contributions to advancing green technology by [Greentech Media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greentech_Media "Greentech Media"). In 2012, Whittingham received the IBA Yeager Award for Lifetime Contribution to Lithium Battery Materials Research, and he was elected a Fellow of [Materials Research Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Materials_Research_Society "Materials Research Society") in 2013. He was listed along with [John B. Goodenough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_B._Goodenough "John B. Goodenough"), for pioneering research leading to the development of the [lithium-ion battery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithium-ion_battery "Lithium-ion battery") on a list of [Clarivate Citation Laureates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clarivate_Citation_Laureates "Clarivate Citation Laureates") for the [Nobel Prize in Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Chemistry "Nobel Prize in Chemistry") by Thomson Reuters in 2015. In 2018, Whittingham was elected to the [National Academy of Engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Academy_of_Engineering "National Academy of Engineering"), "for pioneering the application of [intercalation chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intercalation_(chemistry) "Intercalation (chemistry)") for energy storage materials."

In 2019, Whittingham, along with [John B. Goodenough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_B._Goodenough "John B. Goodenough") and [Akira Yoshino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akira_Yoshino "Akira Yoshino"), was awarded the 2019 [Nobel Prize in Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Chemistry "Nobel Prize in Chemistry") "for the development of lithium-ion batteries."

## Personal life

Stanley is married to Dr. Georgina Whittingham, a professor of Spanish at the [State University of New York at Oswego](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_University_of_New_York_at_Oswego "State University of New York at Oswego"). He has two children, Michael Whittingham and Jenniffer Whittingham-Bras.
