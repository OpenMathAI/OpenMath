**Sir James Fraser Stoddart**, [FRS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow_of_the_Royal_Society "Fellow of the Royal Society") [FRSE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow_of_the_Royal_Society_of_Edinburgh "Fellow of the Royal Society of Edinburgh") [HonFRSC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honorary_Fellows_of_the_Royal_Society_of_Chemistry "Honorary Fellows of the Royal Society of Chemistry") (24 May 1942 – 30 December 2024) was a British-American chemist who was Chair Professor in Chemistry at the [University of Hong Kong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Hong_Kong "University of Hong Kong"). He was the Board of Trustees Professor of Chemistry and head of the Stoddart Mechanostereochemistry Group in the Department of Chemistry at [Northwestern University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northwestern_University "Northwestern University") in the United States. He worked in the area of [supramolecular chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supramolecular_chemistry "Supramolecular chemistry") and [nanotechnology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanotechnology "Nanotechnology"). Stoddart developed highly efficient syntheses of [mechanically-interlocked molecular architectures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanically-interlocked_molecular_architectures "Mechanically-interlocked molecular architectures") such as [molecular Borromean rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_Borromean_rings "Molecular Borromean rings"), [catenanes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catenane "Catenane") and [rotaxanes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotaxane "Rotaxane") utilising [molecular recognition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_recognition "Molecular recognition") and [molecular self-assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_self-assembly "Molecular self-assembly") processes. He demonstrated that these topologies can be employed as molecular switches. His group has even applied these structures in the fabrication of nanoelectronic devices and [nanoelectromechanical systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanoelectromechanical_systems "Nanoelectromechanical systems") (NEMS). His efforts were recognized by numerous awards, including the 2007 [King Faisal International Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Faisal_International_Prize "King Faisal International Prize") in Science. He shared the [Nobel Prize in Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Chemistry "Nobel Prize in Chemistry") together with [Ben Feringa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ben_Feringa "Ben Feringa") and [Jean-Pierre Sauvage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Pierre_Sauvage "Jean-Pierre Sauvage") in 2016 for the design and synthesis of [molecular machines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_machine "Molecular machine").

## Early life and education

Fraser Stoddart was born in [Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edinburgh "Edinburgh"), [Scotland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scotland "Scotland"), on 24 May 1942, the only child of Tom and Jean Stoddart. He was brought up as a tenant farmer on Edgelaw Farm, a small community consisting of three families. Sir Fraser professed a passion for jigsaw puzzles and construction toys in his formative years, which he believed was the basis for his interest in molecular construction.

Stoddart received early schooling at the local village school in [Carrington, Midlothian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carrington,_Midlothian "Carrington, Midlothian"), before going on to [Melville College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melville_College "Melville College") in Edinburgh. He started at the [University of Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Edinburgh "University of Edinburgh") in 1960 where he initially studied chemistry, physics and mathematics. He was awarded a [Bachelor of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_Science "Bachelor of Science") degree in Chemistry in 1964 followed by a [Doctor of Philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Philosophy "Doctor of Philosophy") in 1966 for research on [natural gums](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_gum "Natural gum") in *[Acacias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acacia "Acacia")* supervised by Sir [Edmund Langley Hirst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmund_Langley_Hirst "Edmund Langley Hirst") and D M W Anderson from the University of Edinburgh.

## Career

In 1967, Stoddart went to [Queen's University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen's_University_at_Kingston "Queen's University at Kingston") (Canada) as a [National Research Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Research_Council_of_Canada "National Research Council of Canada") Postdoctoral Fellow. In 1970 he moved to the [University of Sheffield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Sheffield "University of Sheffield") as an [Imperial Chemical Industries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_Chemical_Industries "Imperial Chemical Industries") (ICI) Research Fellow, before joining the academic staff as a lecturer in chemistry. In early 1978 he was a [Science Research Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_Research_Council "Science Research Council") Senior Visiting Fellow at the [University of California, Los Angeles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Los_Angeles "University of California, Los Angeles") (UCLA) Department of Chemistry and Biochemistry. Later in 1978, he was transferred to the ICI Corporate Laboratory in [Runcorn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Runcorn "Runcorn"), England where he first started investigating the mechanically interlocked molecules that would eventually become molecular machines. At the end of the three year secondment he returned to Sheffield where he was promoted to a Readership in 1982.

Stoddart was awarded a [Doctor of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Science "Doctor of Science") degree from the [University of Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Edinburgh "University of Edinburgh") in 1980 for his research into [stereochemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stereochemistry "Stereochemistry") beyond the molecule. In 1990, he moved to the Chair of [Organic Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_Chemistry "Organic Chemistry") at the [University of Birmingham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Birmingham "University of Birmingham") and was Head of the School of Chemistry there (1993–97) before moving to UCLA as the [Saul Winstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saul_Winstein "Saul Winstein") Professor of Chemistry in 1997, succeeding Nobel laureate [Donald Cram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Cram "Donald Cram").

In July 2002, Stoddart became the Acting Co-Director of the [California NanoSystems Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California_NanoSystems_Institute "California NanoSystems Institute") (CNSI). In May 2003, he became the [Fred Kavli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fred_Kavli "Fred Kavli") Chair of NanoSystems Sciences and served from then through August 2007 as the Director of the CNSI.

In 2008, Stoddart established the Mechanostereochemistry Group and was named Board of Trustees Professor in Chemistry at Northwestern University. He went on to be the Director of the Center for the Chemistry of Integrated Systems (CCIS) at Northwestern University in 2010.

In 2017, Stoddart was appointed a part-time position at the [University of New South Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_New_South_Wales "University of New South Wales") to establish his *New Chemistry* initiative at the UNSW School of Chemistry.

In 2019, Stoddart introduced a skincare brand called Noble Panacea.

In 2021, Stoddart co-founded a startup called H2MOF, dedicated to solving the challenges associated with hydrogen storage and transportation.

In 2023, Stoddart joined the University of Hong Kong as Chair Professor of Chemistry.

During 35 years, nearly 300 PhD students and [postdoctoral researchers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postdoctoral_research "Postdoctoral research") have been trained in his laboratories.

## Research

Stoddart is one of only a few chemists of the past quarter century to have pioneered a new field in organic chemistry. By establishing a new field where the main feature is mechanical bonds, he paved the way for molecular recognition, self-assembly processes for template-directed mechanically interlocked syntheses, molecular switches, and motor-molecules. These advances have formed the basis of the fields of nanoelectronic devices, nanoelectromechanical systems, and molecular machines.

One of his major contributions to the development of [mechanically-interlocked molecular architectures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanically-interlocked_molecular_architectures "Mechanically-interlocked molecular architectures") such as [rotaxanes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotaxane "Rotaxane") and [catenanes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catenane "Catenane") was the establishment of efficient synthetic protocols based on the binding of [cyclobis(paraquat-*p*-phenylene)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclobis(paraquat-p-phenylene) "Cyclobis(paraquat-p-phenylene)") with electron-rich aromatic guests. His group reported the synthesis of an advanced mechanically interlocked molecular architecture called [molecular Borromean rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_Borromean_rings "Molecular Borromean rings") through the use of [dynamic covalent chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamic_covalent_chemistry "Dynamic covalent chemistry"). The efficient procedures developed to synthesize these molecular architectures was applied to the construction of [molecular switches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_switch "Molecular switch") that operate based on the movement of the various components with respect to one another. These interlocked molecules have potential uses as [molecular sensors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_sensor "Molecular sensor"), [actuators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Actuators "Actuators"), [amplifiers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amplifiers "Amplifiers"), and [molecular switches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_switch "Molecular switch"), and can be controlled chemically, electrically, and optically.

> His work bridged the gap between chemistry and the scientific and engineering challenges of nanoelectromechanical systems."

Stoddart pioneered the use of [mechanically interlocked molecular architectures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanically_interlocked_molecular_architectures "Mechanically interlocked molecular architectures") to create [nanomechanical systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanomechanics "Nanomechanics"). He has demonstrated that such devices can be fabricated using a combination of the [bottom-up](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Top-down_and_bottom-up_design "Top-down and bottom-up design") approach of [molecular self-assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_self-assembly "Molecular self-assembly") and a [top-down approach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Top-down_and_bottom-up_design "Top-down and bottom-up design") of [lithography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithography "Lithography") and [microfabrication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microfabrication "Microfabrication").

> The credit for making molecular machines attractive to chemists goes to Fraser Stoddart, (... who) had the vision to realise that these architectures gave you the possibility of large amplitude-controlled motions, and that that could be the basis of molecular machines.
>
> — [David Leigh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Leigh_(scientist) "David Leigh (scientist)").

Stoddart's papers and other material are instantly recognizable due to a distinctive "[cartoon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartoon "Cartoon")"-style of [representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_visualization "Scientific visualization") he developed beginning in the late 1980s. A solid circle is often placed in the middle of the [aromatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aromatic "Aromatic") rings of the molecular structures he has reported, and different colours to highlight different parts of the molecules. The different colours usually correspond to the different parts of a cartoon representation of the molecule, but are also used to represent specific molecular properties (blue, for example, is used to represent [electron-poor recognition units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_acceptor "Electron acceptor") while red is used to represent the corresponding [electron-rich recognition units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_donor "Electron donor")). The distinctive colouring has led to coining the term 'little blue box' for the cyclophane cyclobis(paraquat-*p*-phenylene); an important π-acceptor used to synthesize mechanically bonded structures. Stoddart maintains this standardized colour scheme across all of his publications and presentations, and his style has been adopted by other researchers reporting mechanically interlocked molecules based on his syntheses.

### ISI ratings

As of 2024 Stoddart had an [h-index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H-index "H-index") of 168. As of 2023 he had published more than 1200 publications.

## Personal life and death

Stoddart was an American and British citizen. He was married to Norma Agnes Scholan from 1968 until her death in 2004 from cancer. They had two daughters: Fiona Jane and Alison Margaret. Norma Stoddart obtained a PhD in biochemistry and helped support the research efforts of her husband at the Universities of Sheffield, Birmingham, and California, Los Angeles.

On 30 December 2024, Stoddart died from [cardiac arrest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiac_arrest "Cardiac arrest") at a hotel in Melbourne, Australia, where he was visiting his daughter, at the age of 82.

## Philanthropy

The Fraser and Norma Stoddart Prize for PhD students was established at their alma mater, the University of Edinburgh. It was first awarded in 2013.

## Awards and honours

Stoddart was appointed a [Knight Bachelor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knight_Bachelor "Knight Bachelor") in the New Year's Honours December 2006, by [Queen Elizabeth II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_II_of_the_United_Kingdom "Elizabeth II of the United Kingdom") for Services to Chemistry and Molecular Nanotechnology.

In 2007, he received the [Albert Einstein World Award of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Einstein_World_Award_of_Science "Albert Einstein World Award of Science") in recognition for his outstanding and pioneering work in molecular recognition and self-assembly, and the introduction of quick and efficient template-directed synthetic routes to mechanically interlocked molecular compounds, which have changed the way chemists think about molecular switches and machines.

In 2016, he shared the [Nobel Prize in Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Chemistry "Nobel Prize in Chemistry") together with [Ben Feringa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ben_Feringa "Ben Feringa") and [Jean-Pierre Sauvage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Pierre_Sauvage "Jean-Pierre Sauvage") for the design and synthesis of [molecular machines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_machine "Molecular machine").
