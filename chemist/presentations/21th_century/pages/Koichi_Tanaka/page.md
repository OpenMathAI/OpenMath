**Koichi Tanaka** (田中 耕一, *Tanaka Kōichi*; born August 3, 1959) is a Japanese [electrical engineer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_engineer "Electrical engineer") who shared the [Nobel Prize in Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Chemistry "Nobel Prize in Chemistry") in 2002 for developing a novel method for [mass spectrometric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_spectrometry "Mass spectrometry") analyses of biological [macromolecules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macromolecules "Macromolecules") with [John Bennett Fenn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Bennett_Fenn "John Bennett Fenn") and [Kurt Wüthrich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurt_Wüthrich "Kurt Wüthrich") (the latter for work in NMR spectroscopy).

## Early life and education

Tanaka was born and raised in [Toyama, Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toyama,_Toyama "Toyama, Toyama"), his biological mother died one month after he was born. Tanaka graduated from [Tohoku University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tohoku_University "Tohoku University") with a bachelor's degree in electrical engineering in 1983, afterward he joined [Shimadzu Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shimadzu_Corp. "Shimadzu Corp."), where he engaged in the development of mass spectrometers.

## Soft laser desorption

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/1e/Masatoshi_Koshiba_Junichiro_Koizumi_and_Koichi_Tanaka_20021011.jpg/250px-Masatoshi_Koshiba_Junichiro_Koizumi_and_Koichi_Tanaka_20021011.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Masatoshi_Koshiba_Junichiro_Koizumi_and_Koichi_Tanaka_20021011.jpg)

with [Masatoshi Koshiba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masatoshi_Koshiba "Masatoshi Koshiba") and [Jun'ichirō Koizumi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jun'ichirō_Koizumi "Jun'ichirō Koizumi") (at the [Prime Minister's Official Residence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_Minister's_Official_Residence "Prime Minister's Official Residence") on October 11, 2002)

For [mass spectrometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_spectrometry "Mass spectrometry") analyses of a macromolecule, such as a [protein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protein "Protein"), the analyte must be ionized and vaporized by [laser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laser "Laser") irradiation. The problem is that the direct irradiation of an intense laser pulse on a macromolecule causes cleavage of the analyte into tiny fragments and the loss of its structure. In February 1985, Tanaka found that by using a mixture of ultra fine metal powder in [glycerol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glycerol "Glycerol") as a matrix, an analyte can be ionized without losing its structure. His work was filed as a patent application in 1985, and after the patent application was made public reported at the Annual Conference of the Mass Spectrometry Society of Japan held in [Kyoto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyoto "Kyoto"), in May 1987 and became known as [soft laser desorption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soft_laser_desorption "Soft laser desorption") (SLD).

The obscure Tanaka had been at the second-lowest rank at Shimadzu when he won the Nobel Prize. The embarrassed company immediately promoted Tanaka to [research fellow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Research_fellow "Research fellow"), and named a laboratory after him. However, there was some criticism about his winning the prize, saying that contribution by two German scientists, [Franz Hillenkamp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_Hillenkamp "Franz Hillenkamp") and [Michael Karas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Karas "Michael Karas") was also big enough not to be dismissed, and therefore they should also be included as prize winners. This is because they first reported in 1985 a method, with higher sensitivity using a small [organic compound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_compound "Organic compound") as a matrix, that they named [matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix-assisted_laser_desorption/ionization "Matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization") (MALDI). Also Tanaka's SLD is not used currently for biomolecules analysis, meanwhile MALDI is widely used in mass spectrometry research laboratories. But while MALDI was developed prior to SLD, it was not used to ionize [proteins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protein "Protein") until after Tanaka's report.

## Blood-based early disease detection

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/78/LCMS-IT-TOF.jpg/250px-LCMS-IT-TOF.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:LCMS-IT-TOF.jpg)

[Shimadzu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shimadzu "Shimadzu") LCMS-IT-TOF liquid chromatography mass spectrometer, used in the development of next-generation diagnostic methods for detecting disease-related proteins from blood samples.

Tanaka Team have developed a diagnostic technology that enables early detection of diseases from a small amount of blood. By artificially modifying [antibodies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antibodies "Antibodies") with [polyethylene glycol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyethylene_glycol "Polyethylene glycol") at their base, the arms can move like springs, allowing simultaneous binding to antigens. In experiments with protein fragments related to [Alzheimer's disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alzheimer's_disease "Alzheimer's disease"), the modified antibodies captured antigens more than 100 times more strongly than conventional antibodies. Later improvements enabled glycan analysis from trace mixed samples without peptide selection, leading to the detection of Alzheimer’s-related proteins from 1 mL of blood and the identification of eight previously unknown related substances. This technology is expected to contribute to the early detection of various diseases, including Alzheimer’s disease and [prostate cancer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prostate_cancer "Prostate cancer").

The research originated from work awarded the [Nobel Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize "Nobel Prize") in 2002, though the initial methods lacked sufficient sensitivity for medical applications. In 2009, it was selected for the [FIRST Program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FIRST_Program?action=edit&redlink=1 "FIRST Program"), Development of Next-Generation [Mass Spectrometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_Spectrometry "Mass Spectrometry") Systems and Contributions to Drug Discovery and Diagnosis, which provided about 4 billion yen over five years. With a team of around 60 researchers, a breakthrough analytical method was developed within a year, achieving up to a 10,000-fold increase in sensitivity.

In November 2011, the team described the results as a technology applicable to early diagnosis and antibody-based drug development, publishing findings in the electronic edition of an English journal issued by the [Japan Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan_Academy "Japan Academy"). On August 23, 2012, further results were published in the U.S. journal *[PLOS ONE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PLOS_ONE "PLOS ONE")* in collaboration with Motoharu Seiki of the [University of Tokyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Tokyo "University of Tokyo") Institute of Medical Science. By 2014, the technology had advanced to the stage of detecting Alzheimer’s-related substances directly from [blood samples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blood_samples "Blood samples"), and since April 2014, efforts under a new framework have been directed toward practical application.
