**Akira Yoshino** (吉野 彰, *Yoshino Akira*; born 30 January 1948) is a Japanese [chemist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemist "Chemist"). He is a [fellow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow "Fellow") of [Asahi Kasei Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asahi_Kasei "Asahi Kasei") and a professor at [Meijo University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meijo_University "Meijo University") in [Nagoya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nagoya "Nagoya"). He created the first safe, production-viable [lithium-ion battery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithium-ion_battery "Lithium-ion battery"), which became used widely in [cellular phones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cellular_phone "Cellular phone") and [notebook computers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Notebook_computer "Notebook computer"). Yoshino was awarded the [Nobel Prize in Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Chemistry "Nobel Prize in Chemistry") in 2019 alongside [M. Stanley Whittingham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M._Stanley_Whittingham "M. Stanley Whittingham") and [John B. Goodenough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_B._Goodenough "John B. Goodenough").

## Early life and education

Yoshino was born in [Suita](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suita "Suita"), Japan, on 30 January 1948. He graduated from Kitano High School in Osaka City (1966). He earned a [B.S.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_Science "Bachelor of Science") in 1970 and an [M.S.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master_of_Science "Master of Science") degree in 1972, both in [engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineering "Engineering") from [Kyoto University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyoto_University "Kyoto University"), and a [Dr.Eng.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Engineering "Doctor of Engineering") degree from [Osaka University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osaka_University "Osaka University") in 2005.

During his time in elementary school, one of his teachers suggested that he read [The Chemical History of a Candle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Chemical_History_of_a_Candle "The Chemical History of a Candle") by [Michael Faraday](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Faraday "Michael Faraday"), and this sparked a multitude of questions for Yoshino regarding chemistry, a subject he had not been interested in prior to reading the book.

During his college years, Yoshino had attended a course taught by Japanese chemist [Kenichi Fukui](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenichi_Fukui "Kenichi Fukui"), the first recipient of East Asian ancestry to be awarded the Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

## Career

Yoshino spent his entire non-academic career at [Asahi Kasei Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asahi_Kasei "Asahi Kasei"). Immediately after graduating with his master's degree in 1972, Yoshino began working at Asahi Kasei. He joined the Exploratory Research Team at Asahi Kasei Corporation in the early 1970s to explore new general-purpose materials, initially exploring practical applications for polyacetylene but turned to experimenting with using polyacetylene as an anode material once Japan's electronics industry attempted to create new lightweight and compact rechargeable battery to power their mobile devices.

He began work in the Kawasaki Laboratory in 1982 and was promoted to manager of product development for ion batteries in 1992. In 1994, he became manager of technical development for the LIB manufacturer A&T Battery Corp., a joint venture company of Asahi Kasei and [Toshiba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toshiba "Toshiba"). Asahi Kasei made him a fellow in 2003 and, in 2005, general manager of his own laboratory. Since 2017, he has been a professor at [Meijo University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meijo_University "Meijo University") and his status at Asahi Kasei has changed to honorary fellow.

## Research

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7b/Akira_Yoshino_cropped_1_Akira_Yoshino_201910.jpg/250px-Akira_Yoshino_cropped_1_Akira_Yoshino_201910.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Akira_Yoshino_cropped_1_Akira_Yoshino_201910.jpg)

Akira Yoshino

In 1981 Yoshino started doing research on rechargeable batteries using [polyacetylene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyacetylene "Polyacetylene"). Polyacetylene is the electroconductive [polymer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polymer "Polymer") discovered by [Hideki Shirakawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hideki_Shirakawa "Hideki Shirakawa"), who later (in 2000) would be awarded the [Nobel Prize in Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Chemistry "Nobel Prize in Chemistry") for its discovery.

In 1983 Yoshino fabricated a prototype rechargeable battery using [lithium cobalt oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithium_cobalt_oxide "Lithium cobalt oxide") (LiCoO2) (discovered in 1979 by Godshall et al. at Stanford University, and [John Goodenough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Goodenough "John Goodenough") and [Koichi Mizushima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koichi_Mizushima_(scientist) "Koichi Mizushima (scientist)") at Oxford University) as [cathode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathode "Cathode") and polyacetylene as [anode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anode "Anode"). This prototype, in which the anode material itself contains no [lithium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithium "Lithium"), and lithium ions migrate from the LiCoO2 cathode into the anode during charging, was the direct precursor to the modern [lithium-ion battery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithium-ion_battery "Lithium-ion battery") (LIB).

Polyacetylene had low real [density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density "Density") which meant high capacity required large battery volume, and also had problems with instability, so Yoshino switched to carbonaceous material as anode and in 1985 fabricated the first prototype of the LIB and received the basic patent.

This was the birth of the current lithium-ion battery.

The LIB in this configuration was commercialized by [Sony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sony "Sony") in 1991 and by A&T Battery in 1992. Yoshino described challenges and history of the invention process in a book chapter from 2014.

Yoshino discovered that carbonaceous material with a certain crystalline structure was suitable as anode material, and this is the anode material that was used in the first generation of commercial LIBs. Yoshino developed the aluminum foil current collector which formed a passivation layer to enable high cell voltage at low cost, and developed the functional separator membrane and the use of a positive temperature coefficient (PTC) device for additional safety.

The LIB's coil-wound structure was conceived by Yoshino to provide large electrode surface area and enable high current discharge despite the low conductivity of the organic electrolyte.

In 1986 Yoshino commissioned the manufacture of a batch of LIB prototypes. Based on safety test data from those prototypes, the [United States Department of Transportation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Transportation "United States Department of Transportation") (DOT) issued a letter stating that the batteries were different from the metallic [lithium battery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithium_battery "Lithium battery").
