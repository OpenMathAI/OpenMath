**Richard Henderson** (born 19 July 1945) is a British molecular biologist and biophysicist and pioneer in the field of [electron microscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_microscopy "Electron microscopy") of [biological molecules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biological_molecule "Biological molecule"). Henderson shared the [Nobel Prize in Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Chemistry "Nobel Prize in Chemistry") in 2017 with [Jacques Dubochet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques_Dubochet "Jacques Dubochet") and [Joachim Frank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joachim_Frank "Joachim Frank"). "Thanks to his work, we can look at individual atoms of living nature, thanks to cryo-electron microscopes we can see details without destroying samples, and for this he won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry."

## Education

Henderson was educated at Newcastleton primary school, [Hawick High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawick_High_School "Hawick High School") and [Boroughmuir High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boroughmuir_High_School "Boroughmuir High School"). His father was a baker. He went on to study Physics at the [University of Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Edinburgh "University of Edinburgh") graduating with a [BSc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_Science "Bachelor of Science") degree in Physics, 1st Class honours in 1966. He then commenced postgraduate study at [Corpus Christi College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corpus_Christi_College,_Cambridge "Corpus Christi College, Cambridge"), and obtained his [PhD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Philosophy "Doctor of Philosophy") degree from the [University of Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Cambridge "University of Cambridge") in 1969.

## Career and research

### Research

Henderson worked on the structure and mechanism of [chymotrypsin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chymotrypsin "Chymotrypsin") for his doctorate under the supervision of [David Mervyn Blow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Mervyn_Blow "David Mervyn Blow") at the [MRC Laboratory of Molecular Biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laboratory_of_Molecular_Biology "Laboratory of Molecular Biology"). His interest in [membrane proteins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Membrane_protein "Membrane protein") led to him working on voltage-gated [sodium channels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium_channel "Sodium channel") as a post-doctoral researcher at [Yale University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yale_University "Yale University"). Returning to the [MRC Laboratory of Molecular Biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laboratory_of_Molecular_Biology "Laboratory of Molecular Biology") in 1975, Henderson worked with [Nigel Unwin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nigel_Unwin "Nigel Unwin") to study the structure of the [membrane protein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Membrane_protein "Membrane protein") [bacteriorhodopsin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bacteriorhodopsin "Bacteriorhodopsin") by [electron microscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_microscopy "Electron microscopy"). A seminal paper in *[Nature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nature_(journal) "Nature (journal)")* by Henderson and Unwin (1975) established a low resolution structural model for bacteriorhodopsin showing the protein to consist of seven transmembrane helices. This paper was important for a number of reasons, not the least of which was that it showed that membrane proteins had well defined structures and that transmembrane alpha-helices could occur. After 1975 Henderson continued to work on the structure of [bacteriorhodopsin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bacteriorhodopsin "Bacteriorhodopsin") without [Unwin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nigel_Unwin "Nigel Unwin"). In 1990 Henderson published an atomic model of [bacteriorhodopsin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bacteriorhodopsin "Bacteriorhodopsin") by electron crystallography in the *[Journal of Molecular Biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Journal_of_Molecular_Biology "Journal of Molecular Biology")*. This model was the second ever atomic model of a membrane protein. The techniques Henderson developed for electron crystallography are still in use.

Together with Chris Tate, Henderson helped develop conformational thermostabilisation: a method that allows any protein to be made more stable while still holding a chosen conformation of interest. This method has been critical in crystallising and solving the structures of several [G protein–coupled receptors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G_protein–coupled_receptor "G protein–coupled receptor") (GPCRs). With help from the charity [LifeArc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_Research_Council_Technology "Medical Research Council Technology"), Henderson and Tate founded the [MRC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_Research_Council_(United_Kingdom) "Medical Research Council (United Kingdom)") start-up company, Heptares Therapeutics Ltd (HTL) in 2007. HTL continues to develop new drugs targeting medically important GPCRs linked to a wide range of human diseases.

In the last few years, Henderson has returned to hands-on research focusing on single particle electron microscopy. Having been an early proponent of the idea that single particle electron microscopy is capable of determining atomic resolution models for proteins, explained in a 1995 paper in Quarterly Reviews of Biophysics. Henderson aims to be able to routinely obtain atomic structures without crystals. He has made seminal contributions to many of the approaches used in single particle electron microscopy, including pioneering the development of direct electron detectors that recently allowed single particle cryo-electron microscopy to achieve its goals.

### Other positions

Henderson has worked at the [Medical Research Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_Research_Council_(UK) "Medical Research Council (UK)") [Laboratory of Molecular Biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laboratory_of_Molecular_Biology "Laboratory of Molecular Biology") (MRC LMB) in [Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge "Cambridge") since 1973, and was its director between 1996 and 2006. He was also a visiting professor at the Miller Institute of the [University of California, Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Berkeley "University of California, Berkeley") in Spring 1993. He is currently a mentor for the [Academy of Medical Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academy_of_Medical_Sciences,_United_Kingdom "Academy of Medical Sciences, United Kingdom") Mentoring Scheme. Outside academia, he lists his interests as hill walking in Scotland, kayaking and drinking good wine.
