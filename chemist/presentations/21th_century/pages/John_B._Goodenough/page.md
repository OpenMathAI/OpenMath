**John Bannister Goodenough** ([/ˈɡʊdɪnʌf/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English") [*GUUD-in-uf*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:Pronunciation_respelling_key "Help:Pronunciation respelling key"); July 25, 1922 – June 25, 2023) was an American materials scientist, a [solid-state physicist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid-state_physicist "Solid-state physicist"), and a [Nobel laureate in chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_laureate_in_chemistry "Nobel laureate in chemistry"). From 1986 he was a professor of Materials Science, Electrical Engineering and Mechanical Engineering, at the [University of Texas at Austin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Texas_at_Austin "University of Texas at Austin"). He is credited with
identifying the [Goodenough–Kanamori rules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goodenough–Kanamori_rules "Goodenough–Kanamori rules") of the sign of the magnetic [superexchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superexchange "Superexchange") in materials, with developing materials for computer [random-access magnetic memory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_Access_Memory "Random Access Memory") and with inventing cathode materials for [lithium-ion batteries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithium-ion_batteries "Lithium-ion batteries").

Goodenough was awarded the [National Medal of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Medal_of_Science "National Medal of Science"), the [Copley Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copley_Medal "Copley Medal"), the [Fermi Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enrico_Fermi_Award "Enrico Fermi Award"), the [Draper Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Stark_Draper_Prize "Charles Stark Draper Prize"), and the [Japan Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan_Prize "Japan Prize"). The [John B. Goodenough Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_B._Goodenough_Award "John B. Goodenough Award") in materials science is named for him. In 2019, he was awarded the [Nobel Prize in Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Chemistry "Nobel Prize in Chemistry") alongside [M. Stanley Whittingham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M._Stanley_Whittingham "M. Stanley Whittingham") and [Akira Yoshino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akira_Yoshino "Akira Yoshino"); at 97 years old, he became the oldest Nobel laureate in history. From August 27, 2021, until his death, he was the oldest living Nobel Prize laureate.

## Personal life and education

John Goodenough was born in [Jena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jena "Jena"), Germany, on July 25, 1922, to American parents, [Erwin Ramsdell Goodenough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erwin_Ramsdell_Goodenough "Erwin Ramsdell Goodenough") (1893–1965) and Helen Miriam (Lewis) Goodenough. He came from an academic family. His father, a graduate student at [Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Oxford "University of Oxford") when John was born, eventually became a professor of religious history at [Yale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yale "Yale"). His brother [Ward](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ward_Goodenough "Ward Goodenough") became an anthropology professor at the [University of Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Pennsylvania "University of Pennsylvania"). John also had two half-siblings from his father's second marriage: [Ursula Goodenough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ursula_Goodenough "Ursula Goodenough"), emeritus professor of [biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biology "Biology") at [Washington University in St. Louis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington_University_in_St._Louis "Washington University in St. Louis"); and Daniel Goodenough, emeritus professor of [biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biology "Biology") at [Harvard Medical School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_Medical_School "Harvard Medical School").

In his school years Goodenough suffered from [dyslexia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dyslexia "Dyslexia"). At the time, dyslexia was poorly understood by the medical community, and Goodenough's condition went undiagnosed and untreated. Although his primary schools considered him "a backward student," he taught himself to write so that he could take the entrance exam for [Groton School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groton_School "Groton School"), the boarding school where his older brother was studying at the time. He was awarded a full scholarship. At Groton, his grades improved and he eventually graduated at the top of his class in 1940. He also developed an interest in exploring nature, plants, and animals. Although he was raised an atheist, he converted to [Protestant Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestantism "Protestantism") in high school.

After Groton, Goodenough graduated *summa cum laude* from [Yale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yale_College "Yale College"), where he was a member of [Skull and Bones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skull_and_Bones "Skull and Bones"). He completed his coursework in early 1943 (after just two and a half years) and received his degree in 1944, covering his expenses by tutoring and grading exams. He had initially sought to enlist in the military following the [Japanese attack on Pearl Harbor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attack_on_Pearl_Harbor "Attack on Pearl Harbor"), but his mathematics professor convinced him to stay at Yale for another year so that he could finish his coursework, which qualified him to join the [U.S. Army Air Corps'](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Army_Air_Corps "United States Army Air Corps") meteorology department.

After World War II ended, Goodenough obtained a master's degree and a Ph.D. in physics from the [University of Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Chicago "University of Chicago"), the latter in 1952. His doctoral supervisor was [Clarence Zener](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clarence_Zener "Clarence Zener"), a theorist in [electrical breakdown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_breakdown "Electrical breakdown"); he also worked and studied with physicists, including [Enrico Fermi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enrico_Fermi "Enrico Fermi") and [John A. Simpson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Alexander_Simpson "John Alexander Simpson"). While at Chicago, he met Canadian history graduate student Irene Wiseman. They married in 1951. The couple had no children. Irene died in 2016.

Goodenough [turned 100](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centenarian "Centenarian") on July 25, 2022. He died at an assisted living facility in [Austin, Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austin,_Texas "Austin, Texas"), on June 25, 2023, one month shy of what would have been his 101st birthday.

## Career and research

[![](//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/18/John_Goodenough_-_National_Medal_of_Science%2C_2011.webm/250px--John_Goodenough_-_National_Medal_of_Science%2C_2011.webm.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser)](//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/1/18/John_Goodenough_-_National_Medal_of_Science%2C_2011.webm?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=index&utm_content=original)

Goodenough discusses his research and career.

Over his career, Goodenough authored more than 550 articles, 85 book chapters and reviews, and five books, including two seminal works, *Magnetism and the Chemical Bond* (1963) and *Les oxydes des metaux de transition* (1973).

### MIT Lincoln Laboratory

After his studies, Goodenough was a research scientist and team leader at the [MIT Lincoln Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MIT_Lincoln_Laboratory "MIT Lincoln Laboratory") for 24 years. At MIT, he was part of an interdisciplinary team responsible for developing [random-access magnetic memory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_Access_Memory "Random Access Memory"). His research focused on [magnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetism "Magnetism") and on the [metal–insulator transition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metal–insulator_transition "Metal–insulator transition") behavior in [transition-metal oxides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transition-metal_oxides "Transition-metal oxides"). His research efforts on RAM led him to develop the concepts of cooperative orbital ordering, also known as a cooperative [Jahn–Teller distortion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jahn–Teller_distortion "Jahn–Teller distortion"), in [oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxide "Oxide") materials. They subsequently led him to develop (with [Junjiro Kanamori](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/金森順次郎 "ja:金森順次郎")) the [Goodenough–Kanamori rules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goodenough–Kanamori_rules "Goodenough–Kanamori rules"), a set of semi-empirical rules to predict the sign of the magnetic [superexchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superexchange "Superexchange") in materials; superexchange is a core property for [high-temperature superconductivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-temperature_superconductivity "High-temperature superconductivity").

### University of Oxford

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/42/Inorganic-chemistry-lab-Oxford-plaque.jpg/250px-Inorganic-chemistry-lab-Oxford-plaque.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Inorganic-chemistry-lab-Oxford-plaque.jpg)

[Blue plaque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_plaque "Blue plaque") erected by the [Royal Society of Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society_of_Chemistry "Royal Society of Chemistry") commemorating work towards the rechargeable lithium-ion battery at Oxford

The U.S. government eventually terminated Goodenough's research funding, so during the late 1970s and early 1980s, he left the United States and continued his career as head of the [Inorganic Chemistry Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inorganic_Chemistry_Laboratory "Inorganic Chemistry Laboratory") at the [University of Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Oxford "University of Oxford"). Among the highlights of his work at Oxford, Goodenough is credited with significant research essential to the development of commercial [lithium-ion rechargeable batteries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Li-ion_battery "Li-ion battery"). Goodenough was able to expand upon previous work from [M. Stanley Whittingham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M._Stanley_Whittingham "M. Stanley Whittingham") on battery materials, and found in 1980 that by using LixCoO2 as a lightweight, high energy density [cathode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathode "Cathode") material, he could double the capacity of lithium-ion batteries.

Although Goodenough saw a commercial potential of batteries with his LiCoO2 and LiNiO2 cathodes and approached the [University of Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Oxford "University of Oxford") with a request to patent this invention, it refused. Unable to afford the patenting expenses with his academic salary, Goodenough turned to UK's [Atomic Energy Research Establishment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_Energy_Research_Establishment "Atomic Energy Research Establishment") in [Harwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harwell,_Oxfordshire "Harwell, Oxfordshire"), which accepted his offer, but under the terms, which provided zero [royalty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royalty_payment "Royalty payment") payment to the inventors John B. Goodenough and [Koichi Mizushima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koichi_Mizushima_(scientist) "Koichi Mizushima (scientist)"). In 1990, the [AERE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_Energy_Research_Establishment "Atomic Energy Research Establishment") licensed Goodenough's patents to [Sony Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sony_Corporation "Sony Corporation"), which was followed by other battery manufacturers. It was estimated, that the AERE made over 10 million [British pounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_pounds "British pounds") from this licensing.

The work at [Sony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sony "Sony") on further improvements to Goodenough's invention was led by [Akira Yoshino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akira_Yoshino "Akira Yoshino"), who had developed a scaled up design of the battery and manufacturing process. Goodenough received the [Japan Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan_Prize "Japan Prize") in 2001 for his discoveries of the materials critical to the development of lightweight high energy density rechargeable lithium batteries, and he, Whittingham, and Yoshino shared the 2019 [Nobel Prize in Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Chemistry "Nobel Prize in Chemistry") for their research in lithium-ion batteries.

### University of Texas

From 1986, Goodenough was a professor at the [University of Texas at Austin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Texas_at_Austin "University of Texas at Austin") in the [Cockrell School of Engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cockrell_School_of_Engineering "Cockrell School of Engineering") departments of Mechanical Engineering and [Electrical Engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_Engineering "Electrical Engineering"). During his tenure there, he continued his research on ionic conducting solids and electrochemical devices; he continued to study improved materials for batteries, aiming to promote the development of [electric vehicles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_vehicles "Electric vehicles") and to help reduce human dependency on [fossil fuels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fossil_fuels "Fossil fuels"). [Arumugam Manthiram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arumugam_Manthiram "Arumugam Manthiram") and Goodenough discovered the polyanion class of cathodes. They showed that positive electrodes containing [polyanions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyanion "Polyanion"), e.g., [sulfates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulfate "Sulfate"), produce higher voltages than oxides due to the [inductive effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inductive_effect "Inductive effect") of the polyanion. The polyanion class includes materials such as lithium-iron [phosphates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phosphates "Phosphates") that are used for smaller devices like power tools. His group also identified various promising [electrode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrode "Electrode") and [electrolyte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrolyte "Electrolyte") materials for solid oxide fuel cells. He held the Virginia H. Cockrell Centennial Chair in Engineering.

Goodenough still worked at the university at age 98 as of 2021, hoping to find another breakthrough in battery technology.

On February 28, 2017, Goodenough and his team at the University of Texas published a paper in the [journal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_journal "Scientific journal") *[Energy and Environmental Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_and_Environmental_Science "Energy and Environmental Science")* on their demonstration of a [glass battery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glass_battery "Glass battery"), a low-cost all-solid-state battery that is noncombustible and has a long [cycle life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charge_cycle "Charge cycle") with a high volumetric [energy density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_density "Energy density"), and fast rates of charge and discharge. Instead of liquid electrolytes, the battery uses glass electrolytes that enable the use of an [alkali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkali "Alkali")-metal [anode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anode "Anode") without the formation of [dendrites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dendrites "Dendrites"). However, this paper was met with widespread skepticism by the battery research community and remains controversial after several follow-up works. The work was criticized for a lack of comprehensive data, spurious interpretations of the data obtained, and that the proposed mechanism of battery operation would violate the [first law of thermodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_law_of_thermodynamics "First law of thermodynamics").

In April 2020, a patent was filed for the glass battery on behalf of Portugal's [National Laboratory of Energy and Geology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laboratório_Nacional_de_Energia_e_Geologia "Laboratório Nacional de Energia e Geologia") (LNEG), the [University of Porto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Porto "University of Porto"), Portugal, and the University of Texas.

### Advisory work

In 2010, Goodenough joined the technical advisory board of Enevate, a [silicon-dominant Li-ion battery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithium–silicon_battery "Lithium–silicon battery") technology startup based in [Irvine, California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irvine,_California "Irvine, California"). Goodenough also served as an adviser to the [Joint Center for Energy Storage Research (JCESR)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argonne_National_Laboratory#User_facilities_and_centers "Argonne National Laboratory"), a collaboration led by [Argonne National Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argonne_National_Laboratory "Argonne National Laboratory") and funded by the [Department of Energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Energy "United States Department of Energy"). From 2016, Goodenough also worked as an adviser for Battery500, a national consortium led by [Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (PNNL)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pacific_Northwest_National_Laboratory "Pacific Northwest National Laboratory") and partially funded by the [U.S. Department of Energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Department_of_Energy "U.S. Department of Energy").

## Distinctions and awards

[![](https://thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/78/John_B._Goodenough_%282010%29.jpg/250px-John_B._Goodenough_%282010%29.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:John_B._Goodenough_(2010).jpg)

Goodenough receiving the 2009 [Enrico Fermi Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enrico_Fermi_Award "Enrico Fermi Award") from U.S. Energy Secretary [Steven Chu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steven_Chu "Steven Chu").

Goodenough was elected a member of the [National Academy of Engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Academy_of_Engineering "National Academy of Engineering") in 1976 for his work designing materials for electronic components and clarifying the relationships between the properties, structures, and chemistry of substances. He was also a member of the American [National Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Academy_of_Sciences "National Academy of Sciences") and its [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Academy_of_Sciences "French Academy of Sciences"), [Spanish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_Royal_Academy_of_Sciences "Spanish Royal Academy of Sciences"), and [Indian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Academy_of_Sciences,_India "National Academy of Sciences, India") counterparts. In 2010, he was elected a [Foreign Member of the Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_Member_of_the_Royal_Society "Foreign Member of the Royal Society"). The [Royal Society of Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society_of_Chemistry "Royal Society of Chemistry") grants a [John B. Goodenough Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_B._Goodenough_Award "John B. Goodenough Award") in his honor. [The Electrochemical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Electrochemical_Society "The Electrochemical Society") awards a biannual [John B. Goodenough Award of The Electrochemical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_B._Goodenough_Award_of_The_Electrochemical_Society?action=edit&redlink=1 "John B. Goodenough Award of The Electrochemical Society").

Goodenough received the following awards:

- [Fermi Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enrico_Fermi_Award "Enrico Fermi Award") (2009), alongside metallurgist [Siegfried Hecker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siegfried_S._Hecker "Siegfried S. Hecker")
- [National Medal of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Medal_of_Science "National Medal of Science") (2013), presented by U.S. President [Barack Obama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barack_Obama "Barack Obama")
- [Draper Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Stark_Draper_Prize "Charles Stark Draper Prize") in engineering (2014).
- [Welch Award in Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welch_Award_in_Chemistry "Welch Award in Chemistry") (2017)
- C.K. Prahalad Award (2017)
- [Copley Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copley_Medal "Copley Medal") of the Royal Society (2019)
- [Nobel Prize in Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Chemistry "Nobel Prize in Chemistry") (2019), alongside [M. Stanley Whittingham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M._Stanley_Whittingham "M. Stanley Whittingham") and [Akira Yoshino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akira_Yoshino "Akira Yoshino")

Goodenough was 97 when he received the Nobel Prize. He remains the oldest person ever to have been awarded the prize.
