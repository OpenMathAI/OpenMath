**Lars Onsager** (27 November 1903 – 5 October 1976) was a Norwegian American [physical chemist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_chemistry "Physical chemistry") and [theoretical physicist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theoretical_physicist "Theoretical physicist"). He held the Gibbs Professorship of Theoretical Chemistry at [Yale University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yale_University "Yale University"). He was awarded the [Nobel Prize in Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Chemistry "Nobel Prize in Chemistry") in 1968.

## Education and early life

Lars Onsager was born in Kristiania (now [Oslo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oslo "Oslo")), [Norway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norway "Norway"). His father was a [lawyer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawyer "Lawyer"). After completing secondary school in Oslo, he attended the [Norwegian Institute of Technology (NTH)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norwegian_Institute_of_Technology "Norwegian Institute of Technology") in [Trondheim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trondheim "Trondheim"), graduating as a [chemical engineer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_engineering "Chemical engineering") in 1925. While there he worked through *[A Course of Modern Analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Course_of_Modern_Analysis "A Course of Modern Analysis")*, which was instrumental in his later work.

## Career and research

In 1925 he arrived at a correction to the [Debye-Hückel theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Debye–Hückel_equation "Debye–Hückel equation") of [electrolytic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrolyte "Electrolyte") [solutions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solution_(chemistry) "Solution (chemistry)"), to specify [Brownian movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brownian_movement "Brownian movement") of [ions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ion "Ion") in solution, and during 1926 published it. He traveled to [Zürich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zürich "Zürich"), where [Peter Debye](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Debye "Peter Debye") was teaching, and confronted Debye, telling him his theory was wrong. He impressed Debye so much that he was invited to become Debye's assistant at the [Eidgenössische Technische Hochschule (ETH)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ETH_Zürich "ETH Zürich"), where he remained until 1928.

### Johns Hopkins University

In 1928 he went to the United States to take a faculty position at the [Johns Hopkins University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johns_Hopkins_University "Johns Hopkins University") in [Baltimore, Maryland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baltimore,_Maryland "Baltimore, Maryland"). At JHU he had to teach freshman classes in [chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemistry "Chemistry"), and it quickly became apparent that, while he was a genius at developing theories in physical chemistry, he had little talent for teaching. He was dismissed by JHU after one semester.

### Brown University

On leaving JHU, he accepted a position (involving the teaching of [statistical mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_mechanics "Statistical mechanics") to graduate students in chemistry) at [Brown University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brown_University "Brown University") in [Providence, Rhode Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Providence,_Rhode_Island "Providence, Rhode Island"), where it became clear that he was no better at teaching advanced students than freshmen, but he made significant contributions to statistical mechanics and [thermodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermodynamics "Thermodynamics"). His graduate student [Raymond Fuoss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raymond_Fuoss "Raymond Fuoss") worked under him and eventually joined him on the Yale chemistry faculty. His statistical mechanics course was nicknamed "Sadistical Mechanics" by the students.

His research at Brown was concerned mainly with the effects on [diffusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffusion "Diffusion") of [temperature gradients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temperature_gradient "Temperature gradient"), and produced the [Onsager reciprocal relations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Onsager_reciprocal_relations "Onsager reciprocal relations") in [statistical mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_mechanics "Statistical mechanics"), a set of equations published in 1929 and, in an expanded form, in 1931, whose importance went unrecognized for many years. However, their value became apparent during the decades following [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II"), and by 1968 they were considered important enough to gain Onsager that year's [Nobel Prize in Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Chemistry "Nobel Prize in Chemistry").

In 1933, when the [Great Depression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Depression "Great Depression") limited Brown's ability to support a faculty member who was only useful as a researcher and not a teacher, he was let go by Brown. He traveled to [Austria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austria "Austria") to visit [electrochemist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrochemistry "Electrochemistry") [Hans Falkenhagen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Falkenhagen "Hans Falkenhagen"). He met Falkenhagen's sister-in-law, Margrethe Arledter. They were married on September 7, 1933, and had three sons and a daughter.

### Yale University

After the trip to [Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Europe "Europe"), he was hired by [Yale University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yale_University "Yale University"), where he remained for most of the rest of his life, retiring in 1972.

At Yale, he had been hired as a postdoctoral fellow, but it was discovered that he had never received a [Ph.D.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Philosophy "Doctor of Philosophy") While he had submitted an outline of his work in reciprocal relations to the [Norwegian Institute of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norwegian_Institute_of_Technology "Norwegian Institute of Technology"), they had decided it was too incomplete to qualify as a doctoral dissertation. He was told that he could submit one of his published papers to the Yale faculty as a dissertation, but insisted on doing a new research project instead. His dissertation laid the mathematical background for his interpretation of deviations from Ohm's law in weak electrolytes. It dealt with the solutions of the [Mathieu equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathieu_function "Mathieu function") of period

4
π

![{\displaystyle 4\pi }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/057444bf35a0c22b19bcae1ef06e06ecdf8abe56) and certain related functions and was beyond the comprehension of the [chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemistry "Chemistry") and [physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics "Physics") faculty. Only when some members of the [mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics "Mathematics") department, including the director of graduate studies [Einar Hille](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Einar_Hille "Einar Hille") (who also liked *A Course of Modern Analysis*), insisted that the work was good enough that *they* would grant the doctorate if the chemistry department would not, was he granted a Ph.D. in chemistry in 1935.

Even before the dissertation was finished, he was appointed assistant professor in 1934, and promoted to associate professor in 1940. He quickly showed at Yale the same traits he had at JHU and Brown: he produced brilliant theoretical research, but was incapable of giving a lecture at a level that a student (even a graduate student) could comprehend. He was also unable to direct the research of graduate students, except for the occasional outstanding one. His two courses on statistical mechanics were nicknamed "Advanced Norwegian I" and "Advanced Norwegian II" for being incomprehensible.

During the late 1930s, Onsager researched the [dipole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dipole "Dipole") theory of [dielectrics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dielectric "Dielectric"), making improvements for another topic that had been studied by Peter Debye. However, when he submitted his paper to a journal that Debye edited in 1936, it was rejected. Debye would not accept Onsager's ideas until after [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II"). During the 1940s, Onsager studied the [statistical-mechanical theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_mechanics "Statistical mechanics") of [phase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phases_of_matter "Phases of matter") transitions in [solids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid "Solid"), deriving a mathematically elegant theory which was enthusiastically received. In what is widely considered a tour de force of mathematical physics, he obtained the exact solution for the two dimensional [Ising model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ising_model "Ising model") in zero field in 1944.

In 1960 he was awarded an [honorary degree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honorary_degree "Honorary degree"), doctor techn. honoris causa, at the [Norwegian Institute of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norwegian_Institute_of_Technology "Norwegian Institute of Technology"), later part of [Norwegian University of Science and Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norwegian_University_of_Science_and_Technology "Norwegian University of Science and Technology").

In 1945, Onsager was [naturalized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naturalization "Naturalization") as an American citizen, and the same year he was awarded the title of *J. Willard Gibbs Professor of Theoretical Chemistry*. This was particularly appropriate because Onsager, like [Willard Gibbs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Willard_Gibbs "Willard Gibbs"), had been involved primarily in the application of [mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics "Mathematics") to problems in [physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics "Physics") and [chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemistry "Chemistry") and, in a sense, could be considered to be continuing in the same areas Gibbs had pioneered.
In 1947, he was elected to the [National Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_National_Academy_of_Sciences "United States National Academy of Sciences"), the [American Academy of Arts and Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Academy_of_Arts_and_Sciences "American Academy of Arts and Sciences") in 1949, and in 1950 he joined the ranks of [Alpha Chi Sigma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpha_Chi_Sigma "Alpha Chi Sigma").

After [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II"), Onsager researched new topics of interest.
He proposed a theoretical explanation of the [superfluid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superfluidity "Superfluidity") properties of [liquid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid "Liquid") [helium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helium "Helium") in 1949; two years later the [physicist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physicist "Physicist") [Richard Feynman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Feynman "Richard Feynman") independently proposed the same theory. He also worked on the theories of [liquid crystals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid_crystal "Liquid crystal") and the electrical properties of [ice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice "Ice"). While on a [Fulbright scholarship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fulbright_scholarship "Fulbright scholarship") to the [University of Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Cambridge "University of Cambridge"), he worked on the magnetic properties of [metals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metal "Metal"). He developed important ideas on the quantization of magnetic flux in metals. He was awarded the [Lorentz Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentz_Medal "Lorentz Medal") in 1958, [Willard Gibbs Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Willard_Gibbs_Award "Willard Gibbs Award") in 1962, and the [Nobel Prize in Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Chemistry "Nobel Prize in Chemistry") in 1968. He was elected a member of the [American Philosophical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Philosophical_Society "American Philosophical Society") in 1959 and a [Foreign Member of the Royal Society (ForMemRS) in 1975](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Fellows_of_the_Royal_Society_elected_in_1975 "List of Fellows of the Royal Society elected in 1975").

### After Yale

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f5/Kirkwood_onsager.jpg/250px-Kirkwood_onsager.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Kirkwood_onsager.jpg)

Graves of Onsager and Kirkwood

In 1972 Onsager retired from Yale and became emeritus. He then became a member of the [Center for Theoretical Studies, University of Miami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_for_Theoretical_Studies,_University_of_Miami "Center for Theoretical Studies, University of Miami"), and was appointed Distinguished University Professor of Physics. At the [University of Miami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Miami "University of Miami") he remained active in guiding and inspiring postdoctoral students as his teaching skills, although not his lecturing skills, had improved during the course of his career. He developed interests in semiconductor physics, biophysics and radiation chemistry. However, his death came before he could produce any breakthroughs comparable to those of his earlier years.

## Research

### Exact solution of the 2D Ising model

To solve the 2D Ising model, Onsager began by diagonalizing increasingly large transfer matrices. He said that this was because he had a lot of time during WWII. He began by computing the 2 × 2 transfer matrix of the 1D Ising model, which was already solved by Ising himself. He then computed the transfer matrix of the "Ising ladder", meaning two 1D Ising models side-by-side, connected by links. The transfer matrix is then 4 × 4. He repeated this for up to six 1D Ising models, resulting in transfer matrices of up to 64 × 64. He diagonalized all of them and found that all the eigenvalues were of a special form, so he guessed that the algebra of the problem was an [associative algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associative_algebra "Associative algebra") (later called the Onsager algebra).

The solution involved generalized quaternion algebra and the theory of elliptic functions, which he learned from *A Course of Modern Analysis*.

## Personal life

He remained in Florida until his death from an aneurysm in Coral Gables, Florida in 1976. Onsager was buried next to [John Gamble Kirkwood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Gamble_Kirkwood "John Gamble Kirkwood") at New Haven's [Grove Street Cemetery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grove_Street_Cemetery "Grove Street Cemetery"). While Kirkwood's tombstone has a long list of awards and positions, including the [American Chemical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Chemical_Society "American Chemical Society") Award in Pure Chemistry, the Richards Medal, and the Lewis Award, Onsager's tombstone, in its original form, simply said "Nobel Laureate". When Onsager's wife Gretel died in 1991 and was buried there, his children added an asterisk after "Nobel Laureate" and "\*etc." in the lower right corner of the stone. He was identified as a Protestant.

### Legacy

The [Norwegian Institute of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norwegian_Institute_of_Technology "Norwegian Institute of Technology") established the Lars Onsager Lecture and The Lars Onsager Professorship in 1993 to award outstanding scientists in the scientific fields of Lars Onsager; Chemistry, Physics and Mathematics. The [American Physical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Physical_Society "American Physical Society") established [Lars Onsager Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lars_Onsager_Prize "Lars Onsager Prize") in statistical physics in 1993. In 1997 his sons and daughter donated his scientific works and professional belongings to [NTNU](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norwegian_University_of_Science_and_Technology "Norwegian University of Science and Technology") (before 1996 [NTH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norwegian_Institute_of_Technology "Norwegian Institute of Technology")) in [Trondheim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trondheim "Trondheim"), [Norway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norway "Norway") as his alma mater. These are now organized as *The Lars Onsager Archive* at the [Gunnerus Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gunnerus_Library "Gunnerus Library") in Trondheim.
