**Theodore William Richards** (January 31, 1868 – April 2, 1928) was an American physical chemist and the first American scientist to receive the [Nobel Prize in Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Chemistry "Nobel Prize in Chemistry"), earning the award "in recognition of his exact determinations of the [atomic weights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_weight "Atomic weight") of a large number of the [chemical elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_element "Chemical element")."

## Biography

Theodore Richards was born in [Germantown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germantown,_Philadelphia "Germantown, Philadelphia"), [Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pennsylvania "Pennsylvania"), to [William Trost Richards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Trost_Richards "William Trost Richards"), a land- and seascape painter, and [Anna Matlack Richards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anna_Matlack_Richards "Anna Matlack Richards"), a poet. Richards received most of his pre-college education from his mother. During one summer's stay at [Newport, Rhode Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newport,_Rhode_Island "Newport, Rhode Island"), Richards met Professor [Josiah Parsons Cooke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josiah_Parsons_Cooke "Josiah Parsons Cooke") of [Harvard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard "Harvard"), who showed the young boy [Saturn's rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturn's_rings "Saturn's rings") through a small telescope. Years later Cooke and Richards would work together in Cooke's laboratory.

Beginning in 1878, the Richards family spent two years in Europe, largely in England, where Theodore Richards' scientific interests grew stronger. After the family's return to the United States, he entered [Haverford College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haverford_College "Haverford College"), Pennsylvania in 1883 at the age of 14, earning a Bachelor of Science degree in 1885. He then enrolled at [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University "Harvard University") and received a Bachelor of Arts degree in 1886, as further preparation for graduate studies.

Richards continued on at Harvard, taking as his dissertation topic the determination of the atomic weight of oxygen relative to hydrogen. His doctoral advisor was Josiah Parsons Cooke. Next he performed a year of post-doctoral work in [Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germany "Germany"), where he studied under [Victor Meyer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victor_Meyer "Victor Meyer") at the [University of Göttingen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Göttingen "University of Göttingen") and others. Richards returned to Harvard as an assistant in chemistry, then instructor, assistant professor, and finally full professor in 1901. He became one of the first American scientists ever offered a full professorship in a major European university, from Göttingen, in 1901, but instead of taking the position, he chose to continue in America. In 1903 he became chairman of the department of chemistry at Harvard, and in 1912 he was appointed Erving Professor of Chemistry and director of the new Wolcott Gibbs Memorial Laboratory.

In 1896, Richards married Miriam Stuart Thayer. The couple had one daughter, Grace Thayer (who married [James Bryant Conant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Bryant_Conant "James Bryant Conant")), and two sons, Greenough Thayer and William Theodore. Both sons died by suicide.

Richards maintained interests in both art and music. Among his recreations were sketching, golf, and sailing. He died at Cambridge, Massachusetts, on April 2, 1928, at the age of 60. According to one of his descendants, Richards suffered from "chronic respiratory problems and a prolonged depression."

He was a [Quaker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_Society_of_Friends "Religious Society of Friends").

## Scientific research

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b9/Richards_Theodore_William_graph.jpg/330px-Richards_Theodore_William_graph.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Richards_Theodore_William_graph.jpg)

Graph of periodic properties by Richards

About half of Richards's scientific research concerned [atomic weights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_weight "Atomic weight"), starting in 1886 with his graduate studies. On returning to Harvard in 1889, this was his first line of research, continuing up to his death. According to Forbes, by 1932 the atomic weights of 55 [elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_elements "Chemical elements") had been studied by Richards and his students. Among the potential sources of error Richards uncovered in such determinations was the tendency of certain salts to occlude gases or foreign solutes on precipitation. As an example of the care Richards used in his work, Emsley reports that he carried out 15,000 recrystallizations of thulium bromate in order to obtain the pure element [thulium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thulium "Thulium") for an atomic weight measurement.

Richards was the first to show, by chemical analysis, that an element could have different atomic weights. He was asked to analyze samples of naturally occurring [lead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead "Lead") and lead produced by radioactive decay. His measurements showed that the two samples had different atomic weights, supporting the concepts of [isotopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isotopes "Isotopes").

Other scientific work of Theodore Richards included investigations of the compressibilities of atoms, heats of solution and neutralization, and the electrochemistry of amalgams. His investigation of electrochemical potentials at low temperatures was among the work that led, in the hands of others, to the [Nernst heat theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nernst_heat_theorem "Nernst heat theorem") and the [Third law of thermodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_law_of_thermodynamics "Third law of thermodynamics"), although not without heated debate between Nernst and Richards.

Richards also is credited with the invention of the adiabatic [calorimeter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calorimeter "Calorimeter") as well as the [nephelometer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nephelometer "Nephelometer"), which was devised for his work on the atomic weight of [strontium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strontium "Strontium").
