**Dorothy Mary Crowfoot Hodgkin** ([née](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_name#Maiden_and_married_names "Birth name") **Crowfoot**; 12 May 1910 – 29 July 1994) was an English [chemist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemist "Chemist") who advanced the technique of [X-ray crystallography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray_crystallography "X-ray crystallography") to determine the structure of [biomolecules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biomolecule "Biomolecule"), which became essential for [structural biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structural_biology "Structural biology"). She received the 1964 [Nobel Prize in Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Chemistry "Nobel Prize in Chemistry"), and is the only British woman scientist to have been awarded a Nobel Prize.

Among her most influential discoveries are the confirmation of the structure of [penicillin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penicillin "Penicillin") as previously surmised by [Edward Abraham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Abraham "Edward Abraham") and [Ernst Boris Chain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernst_Boris_Chain "Ernst Boris Chain"); and mapping the structure of [vitamin B12](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vitamin_B12 "Vitamin B12"), for which in 1964 she became the third woman to win the [Nobel Prize in Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Chemistry "Nobel Prize in Chemistry"). Hodgkin also elucidated the structure of [insulin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insulin "Insulin") in 1969 after 35 years of work.

Hodgkin used the name "Dorothy Crowfoot" until twelve years after marrying [Thomas Lionel Hodgkin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Lionel_Hodgkin "Thomas Lionel Hodgkin"), when she began using "Dorothy Crowfoot Hodgkin". Hodgkin is referred to as "Dorothy Hodgkin" by the Royal Society (when referring to its sponsorship of the Dorothy Hodgkin fellowship), and by Somerville College. [The National Archives of the United Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_National_Archives_(United_Kingdom) "The National Archives (United Kingdom)") refer to her as "Dorothy Mary Crowfoot Hodgkin". The case of her Nobel prize is inscribed 'Crowfoot Hodgkin'.

## Early life

Dorothy Mary Crowfoot was born in [Cairo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cairo "Cairo"), [Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egypt "Egypt"), the oldest of the four daughters whose parents worked in North Africa and the middle East in the colonial administration and later as archaeologists. Dorothy came from a distinguished family of archaeologists. Her parents were [John Winter Crowfoot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Winter_Crowfoot "John Winter Crowfoot") (1873–1959), working for the country's Ministry of Education, and his wife [Grace Mary (née Hood)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grace_Mary_Crowfoot "Grace Mary Crowfoot") (1877–1957), known to friends and family as Molly. The family lived in Cairo during the winter months, returning to England each year to avoid the hotter part of the season in Egypt.

In 1914, Hodgkin's mother left her (age 4) and her two younger sisters [Joan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joan_Crowfoot_Payne "Joan Crowfoot Payne") (age 2) and Elisabeth (age 7 months) with their Crowfoot grandparents near [Worthing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worthing "Worthing"), and returned to her husband in Egypt. They spent much of their childhood apart from their parents, yet they were supportive from afar. Her mother would encourage Dorothy to pursue the interest in crystals first displayed at the age of 10. In 1923, Dorothy and her sister would study pebbles that they had found in nearby streams using portable mineral analysis kit. Their parents then moved south to Sudan where, until 1926, her father was in charge of education and archaeology. Her mother's four brothers were killed in World War I and as a result she became an ardent supporter of the new [League of Nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/League_of_Nations "League of Nations").

In 1921 Hodgkin's father entered her in the [Sir John Leman Grammar School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_John_Leman_High_School "Sir John Leman High School") in [Beccles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beccles "Beccles"), [England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/England "England"), where she was one of two girls allowed to study chemistry. Only once, when she was 13, did she make an extended visit to her parents, then living in [Khartoum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khartoum "Khartoum"), the capital of Sudan, where her father was Principal of [Gordon College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gordon_Memorial_College "Gordon Memorial College"). When she was 14, her distant cousin, the chemist [Charles Harington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Harington_(chemist) "Charles Harington (chemist)") (later Sir Charles), recommended D. S. Parsons' *Fundamentals of Biochemistry*. Resuming the pre-war pattern, her parents lived and worked abroad for part of the year, returning to England and their children for several months every summer. In 1926, on his retirement from the Sudan Civil Service, her father took the post of Director of the British School of Archaeology in Jerusalem, where he and her mother remained until 1935.

In 1928, Hodgkin joined her parents at the archaeological site of [Jerash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerash "Jerash"), in present-day Jordan, where she documented the patterns of mosaics from multiple Byzantine-era Churches dated to the 5th–6th centuries. She spent more than a year finishing the drawings as she started her studies in Oxford, while also conducting chemical analyses of [glass tesserae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tessera "Tessera") from the same site. Her attention to detail through the creation of precise scale drawings of these mosaics mirrors her subsequent work in recognising and documenting patterns in chemistry. Hodgkin enjoyed the experience of field archaeology so much that she considered giving up chemistry in favour of archaeology. Her drawings are archived by Yale University.

Hodgkin developed a passion for chemistry from a young age, and her mother, a proficient botanist, fostered her interest in the sciences. On her 16th birthday her mother gave her a book by W. H. Bragg on [X-ray crystallography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray_crystallography "X-ray crystallography"), "*Concerning the Nature of Things*", which helped her decide her future. She was further encouraged by the chemist A.F. Joseph, a family friend who also worked in Sudan.

Her state school education did not include [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin "Latin"), then required for entrance to [Oxbridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxbridge "Oxbridge"). Her Leman School headmaster, George Watson, gave her personal tuition in the subject, enabling her to pass the [University of Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Oxford "University of Oxford") entrance examination.

When Hodgkin was asked in later life to name her childhood heroes, she named three women: first and foremost, her mother, [Molly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grace_Crowfoot "Grace Crowfoot"); the medical missionary [Mary Slessor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Slessor "Mary Slessor"); and [Margery Fry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Margery_Fry "Margery Fry"), the Principal of [Somerville College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somerville_College,_Oxford "Somerville College, Oxford").

## Higher education

In 1928 at age 18 Hodgkin entered [Somerville College, Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somerville_College,_Oxford "Somerville College, Oxford"), where she studied chemistry. She graduated in 1932 with a first-class honours degree, the third woman at this institution to achieve this distinction.

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/67/Professor_Dorothy_Hodgkin.jpg/250px-Professor_Dorothy_Hodgkin.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Professor_Dorothy_Hodgkin.jpg)

Dorothy Hodgkin as Chancellor of the University of Bristol

In the autumn of that year, she began studying for a [PhD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Philosophy "Doctor of Philosophy") at [Newnham College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newnham_College,_Cambridge "Newnham College, Cambridge"), under the supervision of [John Desmond Bernal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Desmond_Bernal "John Desmond Bernal"). It was then that she became aware of the potential of X-ray crystallography to determine the structure of [proteins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protein "Protein"). She was working with Bernal on the technique's first application to the analysis of a biological substance, [pepsin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pepsin "Pepsin"). The pepsin experiment is largely credited to Hodgkin, however she always made it clear that it was Bernal who initially took the photographs and gave her additional key insights. Her PhD was awarded in 1937 for research on X-ray crystallography and the chemistry of [sterols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sterol "Sterol").

## Career and discoveries

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/99/Cobalamin.svg/250px-Cobalamin.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Cobalamin.svg)

In 1933 Hodgkin was awarded a [research fellowship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Research_fellow "Research fellow") by Somerville College, and in 1934, she moved back to Oxford. She started teaching chemistry with her own lab equipment. The college appointed her its first fellow and tutor in chemistry in 1936, a post which she held until 1977. In the 1940s, one of her students was Margaret Roberts (later [Margaret Thatcher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Margaret_Thatcher "Margaret Thatcher")) who, while [Prime Minister](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_Minister_of_the_United_Kingdom "Prime Minister of the United Kingdom"), hung a portrait of Hodgkin in her office at [Downing Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Downing_Street "Downing Street") out of respect for her former teacher. Hodgkin was, however a life-long [Labour Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labour_Party_(UK) "Labour Party (UK)") supporter.

In April 1953, together with [Sydney Brenner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sydney_Brenner "Sydney Brenner"), [Jack Dunitz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jack_Dunitz "Jack Dunitz"), [Leslie Orgel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leslie_Orgel "Leslie Orgel"), and Beryl M. Oughton, Hodgkin was one of the first people to travel from Oxford to Cambridge to see the model of the [double helix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_helix "Double helix") structure of [DNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA "DNA"), constructed by [Francis Crick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Crick "Francis Crick") and [James Watson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_D._Watson "James D. Watson"), which was based on data and technique acquired by [Maurice Wilkins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maurice_Wilkins "Maurice Wilkins") and [Rosalind Franklin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosalind_Franklin "Rosalind Franklin"). According to the late Dr Beryl Oughton (married name, Rimmer), they drove to Cambridge in two cars after Hodgkin announced that they were off to see the model of the structure of DNA.

Hodgkin became a [reader](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reader_(academic_rank) "Reader (academic rank)") at Oxford in 1955 and she was given a fully modern laboratory the following year. In 1960, Hodgkin was appointed the [Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society "Royal Society")'s Wolfson Research Professor, a position she held until 1970. This provided her salary, research expenses and research assistance to continue her work at the [University of Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Oxford "University of Oxford"). She was a [fellow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow "Fellow") of [Wolfson College, Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolfson_College,_Oxford "Wolfson College, Oxford"), from 1977 to 1983.

### Steroid structure

Hodgkin was particularly noted for discovering three-dimensional [biomolecular structures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biomolecular_structure "Biomolecular structure"). In 1945, working with C.H. (Harry) Carlisle, she published the first such structure of a [steroid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steroid "Steroid"), cholesteryl iodide (having worked with cholesteryls since the days of her doctoral studies).

### Penicillin structure

In 1945, Hodgkin and her colleagues, including biochemist [Barbara Low](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbara_Low_(biochemist) "Barbara Low (biochemist)"), solved the structure of [penicillin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penicillin "Penicillin"), demonstrating, contrary to scientific opinion at the time, that it contains a [β-lactam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beta-lactam "Beta-lactam") ring. The work was not published until 1949.

### Vitamin B12 structure

In 1948, Hodgkin first encountered [vitamin B12](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vitamin_B12 "Vitamin B12"), one of the most structurally complex vitamins known, and created new crystals. Vitamin B12 had first been discovered at Merck earlier that year. It had a structure at the time that was almost completely unknown, and when Hodgkin discovered it contained cobalt, she realized the structure actualization could be determined by X-ray crystallography analysis. The large size of the molecule, and the fact that the atoms were largely unaccounted for—aside from cobalt—posed a challenge in structure analysis that had not been previously explored.

From these crystals, she deduced the presence of a [ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porphyrin_ring "Porphyrin ring") structure because the crystals were [pleochroic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pleochroic "Pleochroic"), a finding which she later confirmed using X-ray crystallography. The B12 study published by Hodgkin was described by [Lawrence Bragg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawrence_Bragg "Lawrence Bragg") as being as significant "as breaking the sound barrier". Scientists from Merck had previously crystallised B12, but had published only refractive indices of the substance. The final structure of B12, for which Hodgkin was later awarded the Nobel Prize, was published in 1955 and 1956.

### Insulin structure

[Insulin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insulin "Insulin") was one of Hodgkin's most extraordinary research projects. It began in 1934 when she was offered a small sample of crystalline insulin by [Robert Robinson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Robinson_(organic_chemist) "Robert Robinson (organic chemist)"). The [hormone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hormone "Hormone") captured her imagination because of the intricate and wide-ranging effect it has in the body. However, at this stage X-ray crystallography had not been developed far enough to cope with the complexity of the insulin molecule. She and others spent many years improving the technique.

It took 35 years after taking her first photograph of an insulin crystal for X-ray crystallography and computing techniques to be able to tackle larger and more complex molecules like insulin. Hodgkin's dream of unlocking the structure of insulin was put on hold until 1969 when she was finally able to work with her team of young, international scientists to uncover the structure for the first time. Hodgkin's work with insulin was instrumental in paving the way for insulin to be mass-produced and used on a large scale for treatment of both type one and type two diabetes. She went on to cooperate with other laboratories active in insulin research, giving advice, and traveling the world giving talks about insulin and its importance for the future of [diabetes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diabetes "Diabetes"). Solving the structure of insulin had two important implications for the treatment of diabetes, both making mass production of insulin possible and allowing scientists to alter the structure of insulin to create even better drug options for patients going forward.

## Personal life

### Personality

Her structural studies of biologically important [molecules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecule "Molecule") set standards for a field that was very much in development during her work life. She made contributions to the understanding of how these molecules carry out their tasks in living system.

### Mentor

Hodgkin's mentor [Professor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Professor "Professor") [John Desmond Bernal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Desmond_Bernal "John Desmond Bernal") greatly influenced her life: scientifically, politically, and personally. Bernal was a key scientific adviser to the UK government during the Second World War. He was also an open and vocal member of the [Communist Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communist_Party_of_Great_Britain "Communist Party of Great Britain") and a faithful supporter of the [Soviet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union "Soviet Union") regime until its [invasion of Hungary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1956_Hungarian_Revolution "1956 Hungarian Revolution") in 1956. He was a chemist who believed in equal opportunity for women. In his laboratory, Hodgkin extended work that he began on biological molecules including sterols. She helped him to make the first X-ray diffraction studies of pepsin and [crystalline protein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystallin "Crystallin"). Hodgkin always referred to him as "Sage". They were lovers before she met Thomas Hodgkin. The marriages of both Dorothy and Bernal were unconventional by the standards of the present and of those days.

### Health

In 1934, at the age of 24, Dorothy began experiencing pain in her hands causing them to become swollen and distorted. She was diagnosed with rheumatoid arthritis, and went to a clinic in Buxton for thermal baths and gold treatments. After some treatment, Hodgkin returned to the lab, where she struggled to use the main switch on the x-ray equipment due to the condition of her hands. She had to create a lever on her own in order to operate the switch. Her condition would become progressively worse and debilitating over time, with deformities in both her hands and feet, and prolonged periods of pain. While Hodgkin spent a great deal of time in a wheelchair in her later years, she remained scientifically active in her career.

### Marriage and family

In 1937, Dorothy Crowfoot married Thomas Lionel Hodgkin, an historian's son, who was then teaching an adult-education class in mining and industrial communities in the north of England after he resigned from the [Colonial Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colonial_Office "Colonial Office"). He was an intermittent member of the [Communist Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communist_Party_of_Great_Britain "Communist Party of Great Britain") and later wrote several major works on African politics and history, becoming a well-known lecturer at [Balliol College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balliol_College "Balliol College") in Oxford. As his health was too poor for active military service, he continued working throughout World War II, returning to Oxford on the weekends, where his wife remained working on [penicillin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penicillin "Penicillin"). The couple had three children: Luke (1938 – October 2020), Elizabeth (born 1941) and Toby (born 1946). The oldest son, Luke, became a mathematics instructor at the new University of Warwick. Their daughter, Elizabeth, followed her father's career as a historian. Their younger son, Toby, studied botany and agriculture. Overall, Thomas Hodgkin spent extended periods of time in West Africa, where he was an enthusiastic supporter and chronicler of the emerging postcolonial states. Thomas Hodgkin died in [Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greece "Greece") on 25 March 1982.

### Aliases

Hodgkin published as "Dorothy Crowfoot" until 1949, when she was persuaded by [Hans Clarke's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Thacher_Clarke "Hans Thacher Clarke") secretary to use her married name on a chapter she contributed to *The Chemistry of Penicillin*. By then she had been married for 12 years, given birth to three children and been elected a [Fellow of the Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow_of_the_Royal_Society "Fellow of the Royal Society") (FRS).

Thereafter she would publish as "Dorothy Crowfoot Hodgkin", and this was the name used by the [Nobel Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Foundation "Nobel Foundation") in its award to her and the biography it included among other [Nobel Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize "Nobel Prize") recipients; it is also what the [Science History Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_History_Institute "Science History Institute") calls her. For simplicity's sake, Hodgkin is referred to as "Dorothy Hodgkin" by the Royal Society, when referring to its sponsorship of the Dorothy Hodgkin fellowship, and by Somerville College, after it inaugurated the annual lectures in her honour.

[The National Archives of the United Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_National_Archives_(United_Kingdom) "The National Archives (United Kingdom)") refer to her as "Dorothy Mary Crowfoot Hodgkin"; on a variety of plaques commemorating places where she worked or lived, e.g. 94 [Woodstock Road, Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woodstock_Road,_Oxford "Woodstock Road, Oxford"), she is "Dorothy Crowfoot Hodgkin". In 2022, the Department of Biochemistry in Oxford renamed its much expanded building after Hodgkin, calling it the "Dorothy Crowfoot Hodgkin Building".

### Contacts with scientists abroad

Between the 1950s and the 1970s, Hodgkin established and maintained lasting contacts with scientists in her field abroad—at the Institute of Crystallography in [Moscow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moscow "Moscow"); in India; and with the Chinese group working in [Beijing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beijing "Beijing") and [Shanghai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shanghai "Shanghai") on the structure of insulin.

Her first visit to China was in 1959. Over the next quarter century, she travelled there seven more times, the last visit a year before her death. Particularly memorable was the visit in 1971 after the Chinese group themselves independently solved the structure of insulin, later than Hodgkin's team but to a higher resolution. During the subsequent three years, 1972–1975, when she was President of the [International Union of Crystallography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Union_of_Crystallography "International Union of Crystallography") she was unable to persuade the Chinese authorities, however, to permit the country's scientists to become members of the Union and attend its meetings.

Her relations with a supposed scientist in another "People's Democracy" had less happy results. At the age of 73, Hodgkin wrote a foreword to the English edition of *Stereospecific Polymerization of Isoprene*, published by [Robert Maxwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Maxwell "Robert Maxwell") as the work of [Elena Ceaușescu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elena_Ceaușescu "Elena Ceaușescu"), wife of Romania's communist dictator. Hodgkin wrote of the author's "outstanding achievements" and "impressive" career. Following the overthrow of Ceausescu during the [Romanian Revolution of 1989](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanian_Revolution_of_1989 "Romanian Revolution of 1989"), it was revealed that Elena Ceausescu had neither finished secondary school nor attended university. Her scientific credentials were a [hoax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoax "Hoax"), and the publication in question was written for her by a team of scientists to obtain a fraudulent doctorate.

### Political views and activities

Because of Hodgkin's political activities, and her husband's association with the Communist Party, she was banned from entering the US in 1953 and subsequently not allowed to visit the country except by [CIA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CIA "CIA") waiver.

In 1961 Thomas became an advisor to [Kwame Nkrumah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kwame_Nkrumah "Kwame Nkrumah"), [President of Ghana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_of_Ghana "President of Ghana"), a country he visited for extended periods before Nkrumah's ouster in 1966. Hodgkin was in Ghana with her husband when they received the news that she had been awarded the Nobel Prize.

She acquired from her mother, Molly, a concern about [social inequalities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_inequality "Social inequality") and a determination to do what she could to prevent armed conflict. Dorothy became particularly concerned about the threat of nuclear war. In 1976, she became president of the [Pugwash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pugwash_Conferences_on_Science_and_World_Affairs "Pugwash Conferences on Science and World Affairs") Conference and served longer than any who preceded or succeeded her in this post. She stepped down in 1988, the year after the [Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces Treaty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intermediate-Range_Nuclear_Forces_Treaty "Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces Treaty") imposed "a global ban on short- and long-range nuclear weapons systems, as well as an intrusive verification regime". She accepted the [Lenin Peace Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lenin_Peace_Prize "Lenin Peace Prize") from the Soviet government in 1987 in recognition of her work for peace and disarmament.

### Disability and death

Due to distance, Hodgkin decided not to attend the 1987 Congress of the International Union of Crystallography in Australia. However, despite increasing frailty, she astounded close friends and family by going to Beijing for the 1993 Congress, where she was welcomed by all.

She died in July 1994 after a stroke, at her husband's home in the village of [Ilmington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ilmington "Ilmington"), near [Shipston-on-Stour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shipston-on-Stour "Shipston-on-Stour"), [Warwickshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warwickshire "Warwickshire").

## Portraits

The [National Portrait Gallery, London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Portrait_Gallery,_London "National Portrait Gallery, London") lists 17 portraits of Dorothy Hodgkin including an oil painting of her at her desk by [Maggi Hambling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maggi_Hambling "Maggi Hambling") and a photograph portrait by [David Montgomery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Montgomery_(photographer) "David Montgomery (photographer)").

[Graham Sutherland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graham_Sutherland "Graham Sutherland") made preliminary sketches for a portrait of Dorothy Crowfoot Hodgkin in 1978. One sketch is in the collection of the [Science History Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_History_Institute "Science History Institute") and another at the [Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society "Royal Society") in London. The portrait was never finished.

A portrait of Dorothy Hodgkin by [Bryan Organ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bryan_Organ "Bryan Organ") was commissioned by private subscription to become part of the collection of the Royal Society. Accepted by the president of the society on 25 March 1982, it was the first portrait of a woman Fellow to be included in the Society's collection.

## Honours and awards

### While living

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/8f/Order_of_Merit_Dorothy_Hodgkin.jpg/250px-Order_of_Merit_Dorothy_Hodgkin.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Order_of_Merit_Dorothy_Hodgkin.jpg)

[Order of Merit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_Merit "Order of Merit") insignia of Dorothy Hodgkin, displayed in the Royal Society, London

- By 1945 she had succeeded in identifying the structure of vitamin B12, describing the arrangement of its [atoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atom "Atom") in three dimensions.
- In 1947, she was elected a [Fellow of the Royal Society (FRS)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Fellows_of_the_Royal_Society_elected_in_1947 "List of Fellows of the Royal Society elected in 1947")
- In 1958, she was elected a Foreign Honorary Member of the [American Academy of Arts and Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Academy_of_Arts_and_Sciences "American Academy of Arts and Sciences").
- Hodgkin won the 1964 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, and is the only British woman scientist to have been awarded a [Nobel Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize "Nobel Prize") in any of the three sciences it recognizes.
- In 1965 she was appointed to the [Order of Merit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_Merit "Order of Merit"). She was the second woman to receive the Order.
- In 1966, she was awarded the [Iota Sigma Pi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iota_Sigma_Pi "Iota Sigma Pi") National Honorary Member for her significant contribution.
- In 1970, gained [EMBO Membership](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EMBO_Membership "EMBO Membership").
- She became a foreign member of the [USSR Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USSR_Academy_of_Sciences "USSR Academy of Sciences") in the 1970s.
- In 1976, she was the first woman to receive the prestigious [Copley Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copley_Medal "Copley Medal").
- Hodgkin was [Chancellor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chancellor_(education) "Chancellor (education)") of the [University of Bristol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Bristol "University of Bristol") from 1970 to 1988.
- In 1978, she was given an honorary Degree of Science from University of Bath.
- In 1981, she was awarded the [Dalton Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dalton_Medal "Dalton Medal") from the [Manchester Literary and Philosophical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manchester_Literary_and_Philosophical_Society "Manchester Literary and Philosophical Society")
- In 1982 she received the [Lomonosov Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lomonosov_Medal "Lomonosov Medal") of the Soviet Academy of Sciences.
- In 1987 she accepted the [Lenin Peace Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lenin_Peace_Prize "Lenin Peace Prize") from the government of [Mikhail Gorbachev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikhail_Gorbachev "Mikhail Gorbachev").
- In 1983, Hodgkin received the [Austrian Decoration for Science and Art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austrian_Decoration_for_Science_and_Art "Austrian Decoration for Science and Art").
- In 1993, an asteroid (5422) discovered on 23 December 1982 by L.G. Karachkina (at the Crimean Astrophysical Observatory, M.P.C. 22509, in the USSR) was named "Hodgkin" in her honour.

### Legacy

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/89/Royal_Mail_Dorothy_Hodgkin_1996.png/250px-Royal_Mail_Dorothy_Hodgkin_1996.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Royal_Mail_Dorothy_Hodgkin_1996.png)

Royal Mail stamp commemorating Dorothy Hodgkin (1996)

- The Dorothy Hodgkin Quarter, a student accommodation block at Somerville, is named after Hodgkin.
- British commemorative stamps - Hodgkin was one of five 'Women of Achievement' selected for a set issued in August 1996. The others were [Marea Hartman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marea_Hartman "Marea Hartman") (sports administrator), [Margot Fonteyn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Margot_Fonteyn "Margot Fonteyn") (ballerina/choreographer), [Elisabeth Frink](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elisabeth_Frink "Elisabeth Frink") (sculptor) & [Daphne du Maurier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daphne_du_Maurier "Daphne du Maurier") (writer). All except Hodgkin were Dames Commander of the Order of the British Empire (DBEs). In 2010, during the 350th anniversary of the founding of the Royal Society, Hodgkin was the only woman in a set of [stamps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystallography_on_stamps "Crystallography on stamps") celebrating ten of the Society's most illustrious members, taking her place alongside [Isaac Newton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Newton "Isaac Newton"), [Edward Jenner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Jenner "Edward Jenner"), [Joseph Lister](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Lister,_1st_Baron_Lister "Joseph Lister, 1st Baron Lister"), [Benjamin Franklin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benjamin_Franklin "Benjamin Franklin"), [Charles Babbage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Babbage "Charles Babbage"), [Robert Boyle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Boyle "Robert Boyle"), [Ernest Rutherford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_Rutherford "Ernest Rutherford"), [Nicholas Shackleton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicholas_Shackleton "Nicholas Shackleton") and [Alfred Russel Wallace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Russel_Wallace "Alfred Russel Wallace").
- The [Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society "Royal Society") awards the Dorothy Hodgkin [Fellowship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow "Fellow") (named in her honour) "for outstanding scientists at an early stage of their research career who require a flexible working pattern due to personal circumstances, such as parenting or caring responsibilities or health-related reasons."
- The Council offices in the [London Borough of Hackney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Borough_of_Hackney "London Borough of Hackney") and buildings at [University of York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_York "University of York"), [Bristol University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Bristol "University of Bristol") and [Keele University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keele_University "Keele University") are named after her, as is the science block at [Sir John Leman High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_John_Leman_High_School "Sir John Leman High School"), her former school.
- In 2012, Hodgkin was featured in the [BBC Radio 4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_Radio_4 "BBC Radio 4") series *[The New Elizabethans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_Elizabethans "The New Elizabethans")* to mark the [diamond Jubilee of Queen Elizabeth II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamond_Jubilee_of_Queen_Elizabeth_II "Diamond Jubilee of Queen Elizabeth II"). In this series a panel of seven academics, journalists and historians named her among the group of people in the UK "whose actions during the reign of Elizabeth II have had a significant impact on lives in these islands and given the age its character".
- In 2015 Hodgkin's 1949 paper *The X-ray Crystallographic Investigation of the Structure of Penicillin* was honoured by a Citation for Chemical Breakthrough Award from the Division of History of Chemistry of the American Chemical Society presented to the University of Oxford (England). This research is notable for its groundbreaking use of X-ray crystallography to determine the structure of complex natural products, in this instance, of penicillin.
- Since 1999, the Oxford International Women's Festival has presented the annual Dorothy Hodgkin Memorial Lecture, usually in March, in honour of Hodgkin's work. The Lecture is a collaboration between Oxford AWiSE (Association for Women in Science & Engineering), Somerville College and the Oxford University Museum of Natural History.
