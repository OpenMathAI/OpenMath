**Paul Berg** (June 30, 1926 – February 15, 2023) was an American [biochemist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biochemist "Biochemist") and professor at [Stanford University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford_University "Stanford University").
He received the 1980 [Nobel Prize in Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Chemistry "Nobel Prize in Chemistry") "for his fundamental studies of the biochemistry of [nucleic acids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nucleic_acids "Nucleic acids"), with particular regard to recombinant-DNA".

Berg received his undergraduate education at [Penn State University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pennsylvania_State_University "Pennsylvania State University"), where he majored in [biochemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biochemistry "Biochemistry"). He received his PhD in biochemistry from [Case Western Reserve University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Case_Western_Reserve_University "Case Western Reserve University") in 1952. Berg worked as a professor at [Washington University School of Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington_University_School_of_Medicine "Washington University School of Medicine") and [Stanford University School of Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford_University_School_of_Medicine "Stanford University School of Medicine"), in addition to serving as the director of the [Beckman Center for Molecular and Genetic Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beckman_Center_for_Molecular_and_Genetic_Medicine "Beckman Center for Molecular and Genetic Medicine").

In addition to the Nobel Prize, Berg was presented with the [National Medal of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Medal_of_Science "National Medal of Science") in 1983 and the National Library of Medicine Medal in 1986. Berg was a member of the Board of Sponsors for the *[Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bulletin_of_the_Atomic_Scientists "Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists")*.

## Early life and education

Berg was born in [Brooklyn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brooklyn "Brooklyn"), New York City. He was the son of a [Russian Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Jews_in_Russia "History of the Jews in Russia") immigrant couple, Sarah Brodsky, a homemaker, and Harry Berg, a clothing manufacturer. Berg graduated from [Abraham Lincoln High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraham_Lincoln_High_School_(Brooklyn,_New_York) "Abraham Lincoln High School (Brooklyn, New York)") in 1943, received his Bachelor of Science degree in biochemistry from [Penn State University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penn_State_University "Penn State University") in 1948 and PhD in biochemistry from [Case Western Reserve University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Case_Western_Reserve_University "Case Western Reserve University") in 1952. He was a member of the [Jewish fraternity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Jewish_fraternities_and_sororities "List of Jewish fraternities and sororities"), [ΒΣΡ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beta_Sigma_Rho "Beta Sigma Rho").

## Research and career

### Academic posts

After completing his graduate studies, Berg spent two years (1952–1954) as a [postdoctoral fellow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postdoctoral_fellow "Postdoctoral fellow") with the [American Cancer Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Cancer_Society "American Cancer Society"), working at the Institute of Cytophysiology in [Copenhagen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copenhagen "Copenhagen"), Denmark, and the [Washington University School of Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington_University_School_of_Medicine "Washington University School of Medicine"), and spent additional time in 1954 as a scholar in cancer research with the department of microbiology at the Washington University School of Medicine. He worked with [Arthur Kornberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Kornberg "Arthur Kornberg"), while at Washington University. Berg was also tenured as a research fellow at [Clare Hall, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clare_Hall,_Cambridge "Clare Hall, Cambridge"). He was a professor at [Washington University School of Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington_University_School_of_Medicine "Washington University School of Medicine") from 1955 until 1959. After 1959, Berg moved to [Stanford University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford_University "Stanford University"), where he taught biochemistry from 1959 until 2000 and served as director of the [Beckman Center for Molecular and Genetic Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beckman_Center_for_Molecular_and_Genetic_Medicine "Beckman Center for Molecular and Genetic Medicine") from 1985 until 2000. In 2000 he retired from his administrative and teaching posts, continuing to be active in research.

### Research interests

Berg's postgraduate studies involved the use of radioisotope tracers to study [intermediary metabolism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intermediary_metabolism "Intermediary metabolism"). This resulted in the understanding of how foodstuffs are converted to cellular materials, through the use of isotopic carbons or heavy nitrogen atoms. Paul Berg's doctorate paper is now known as the conversion of [formic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formic_acid "Formic acid"), [formaldehyde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formaldehyde "Formaldehyde") and [methanol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methanol "Methanol") to fully reduced states of methyl groups in [methionine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methionine "Methionine"). He was also one of the first to demonstrate that folic acid and B12 cofactors had roles in the processes mentioned.

Berg is arguably most famous for his pioneering work involving gene splicing of [recombinant DNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recombinant_DNA "Recombinant DNA"). Berg was the first scientist to create a molecule containing DNA from two different species by inserting DNA from another species into a molecule. This gene-splicing technique was a fundamental step in the development of modern [genetic engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_engineering "Genetic engineering"). After developing the technique, Berg used it for his studies of viral chromosomes.

Berg was a [professor emeritus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Professor_emeritus "Professor emeritus") at Stanford. As of 2000, he stopped doing active research, to focus on other interests, including involvement in public policy for biomedical issues involving recombinant DNA and embryonic stem cells and publishing a book about geneticist [George Beadle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Beadle "George Beadle").

Berg was a member of the Board of Sponsors of the *[Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bulletin_of_the_Atomic_Scientists "Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists")*. He was also an organizer of the [Asilomar conference on recombinant DNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asilomar_conference_on_recombinant_DNA "Asilomar conference on recombinant DNA") in 1975. The previous year, Berg and other scientists had called for a voluntary [moratorium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moratorium_(law) "Moratorium (law)") on certain [recombinant DNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recombinant_DNA "Recombinant DNA") research until they could evaluate the risks. That influential conference did evaluate the potential hazards and set guidelines for [biotechnology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biotechnology "Biotechnology") research. It can be seen as an early application of the [precautionary principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precautionary_principle "Precautionary principle").

#### Nobel Prize

Berg was awarded one-half of the 1980 [Nobel Prize in Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Chemistry "Nobel Prize in Chemistry"), with the other half being shared by [Walter Gilbert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Gilbert "Walter Gilbert") and [Frederick Sanger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_Sanger "Frederick Sanger"). Berg was recognized for "his fundamental studies of the biochemistry of [nucleic acids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nucleic_acids "Nucleic acids"), with particular regard to [recombinant DNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recombinant_DNA "Recombinant DNA")", while Sanger and Gilbert were honored for "their contributions concerning the determination of base sequences in nucleic acids."

#### Other awards and honors

He was elected a Fellow of the [American Academy of Arts and Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Academy_of_Arts_and_Sciences "American Academy of Arts and Sciences") and a member of the United States [National Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Academy_of_Sciences "National Academy of Sciences") in 1966. In 1983, [Ronald Reagan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Reagan "Ronald Reagan") presented Berg with the [National Medal of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Medal_of_Science "National Medal of Science"). That same year, he was elected to the [American Philosophical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Philosophical_Society "American Philosophical Society"). In 1989, he received the Golden Plate Award of the [American Academy of Achievement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academy_of_Achievement "Academy of Achievement"). He was elected a [Foreign Member of the Royal Society (ForMemRS) in 1992](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Fellows_of_the_Royal_Society_elected_in_1992 "List of Fellows of the Royal Society elected in 1992"). In 2005 he was awarded the [Biotechnology Heritage Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biotechnology_Heritage_Award "Biotechnology Heritage Award") by the [Biotechnology Industry Organization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biotechnology_Industry_Organization "Biotechnology Industry Organization") (BIO) and the [Chemical Heritage Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_Heritage_Foundation "Chemical Heritage Foundation"). In 2006 he received [Wonderfest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wonderfest "Wonderfest")'s Carl Sagan Prize for Science Popularization.

## Death

Berg died on February 15, 2023, at the age of 96.
