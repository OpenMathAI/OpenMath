**Hans Fischer** (German pronunciation: [[ˈhans ˈfɪʃɐ]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Standard_German "Help:IPA/Standard German") [ⓘ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:De-Hans_Fischer.ogg "File:De-Hans Fischer.ogg"); 27 July 1881 – 31 March 1945) was a German [organic chemist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_chemistry "Organic chemistry") and the recipient of the 1930 [Nobel Prize for Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_for_Chemistry "Nobel Prize for Chemistry") "for his researches into the constitution of [haemin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haemin "Haemin") and [chlorophyll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chlorophyll "Chlorophyll") and especially for his synthesis of haemin."

## Biography

### Early years

Fischer was born on July 27, 1881, in [Höchst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Höchst_(Frankfurt_am_Main) "Höchst (Frankfurt am Main)"), now a city district of [Frankfurt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frankfurt "Frankfurt") located in Germany. His parents were Dr. Eugen Fischer, Director of the firm of [Kalle & Co.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalle_&_Co.?action=edit&redlink=1 "Kalle & Co."), [Wiesbaden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wiesbaden "Wiesbaden"), and Privatdozent at the [Technische Hochschule Stuttgart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technische_Hochschule_Stuttgart "Technische Hochschule Stuttgart"), and Anna Herdegen was his mother. He went to a primary school in [Stuttgart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stuttgart "Stuttgart"), and later to the "Humanistisches [Gymnasium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gymnasium_(school) "Gymnasium (school)")" in [Wiesbaden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wiesbaden "Wiesbaden"), matriculating in 1899. He read [chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemistry "Chemistry") and [medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medicine "Medicine"), first at the [University of Lausanne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Lausanne "University of Lausanne") and then at [Marburg University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marburg_University "Marburg University"). He graduated in 1904 and obtained his chemistry degree. Two years later in 1906, he licensed for medicine, and in 1908, he qualified for his [M.D.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Medicine "Doctor of Medicine") with which he applied to the [Ludwig-Maximilians-Universität München](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludwig-Maximilians-Universität_München "Ludwig-Maximilians-Universität München").

### Career

He worked first at a Medical Clinic in [Munich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Munich "Munich") and then at the [First Berlin Chemical Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Berlin_Chemical_Institute?action=edit&redlink=1 "First Berlin Chemical Institute") under [Emil Fischer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann_Emil_Fischer "Hermann Emil Fischer"). He returned to the Ludwig-Maximilians-Universität München in 1911 and qualified as lecturer on internal medicine one year later. In 1913, he became a lecturer in [physiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physiology "Physiology") at the Physiological Institute of the Ludwig-Maximilians-Universität München. In 1916, he became Professor of Medical Chemistry at the [University of Innsbruck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Innsbruck "University of Innsbruck") and from there he went to the [University of Vienna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Vienna "University of Vienna") in 1918.

Then, from 1921 until his death, he held the position of Professor of Organic Chemistry at the [Technical University of Munich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technical_University_of_Munich "Technical University of Munich").

Fischer's scientific work was mostly concerned with the investigation of the pigments in [blood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blood "Blood"), [bile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bile "Bile"), and also [chlorophyll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chlorophyll "Chlorophyll") in [leaves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leaves "Leaves"), as well as with the chemistry of [pyrrole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyrrole "Pyrrole") from which these pigments are derived. Of special importance was his synthesis of [bilirubin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bilirubin "Bilirubin") and [haemin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haemin "Haemin"). He received many honors for this work, and received the [Nobel Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize "Nobel Prize") in 1930. The [lunar crater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_crater "Lunar crater") [Fischer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fischer_(crater) "Fischer (crater)") was named after him (and [Hermann Emil Fischer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann_Emil_Fischer "Hermann Emil Fischer")) in 1976. Hans Fisher had mapped the composition of a heme group. In 1929, Fischer succeeded in producing the substance and proving that its ring has a central atom of iron, as he also continued studying other pigmented substances of a biological importance of biochemistry such as chlorophyll, the color that plays part in a plants photosynthesis. Fischer also unraveled the bile pigments biliverdin (which causes the yellowish color characteristic of bruised skin) and bilirubin (which yellows skin in jaundice cases), and synthesized them in 1942 and 1944, successively. He conducted microanalyses of more than 60,000 chemical substance, and had won the Nobel Prize for Chemistry in 1930. The person that sparked Fischer's interest was von Muller, his former professor and supervisor whom interests were in pyrrole pigments by inviting Fischer to work with him at the well-known Second Medical Clinic in Munich in 1910. Under Muller, he began to examine the composition of the bile pigment bilirubin, something he would continue to be engaged in during the decades that followed. Fischer's succession also came with difficulty's as many of his experiments seemed to have failed but with time Fischer was able to perfect his acknowledgement with his failed attempts.

### Personal life

Fischer married Wiltrud Haufe around his 50s in the year 1935. Fischer was a man who was dedicated almost "exclusively" to his work. He continued his scientific research during Germany's Nazi era, and committed suicide on Easter Sunday in 1945, after his laboratory and life's work had been destroyed by the bombing of Munich in the last days of World War II.
