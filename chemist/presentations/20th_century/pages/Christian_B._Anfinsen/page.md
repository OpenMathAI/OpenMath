**Christian Boehmer Anfinsen Jr.** (March 26, 1916 – May 14, 1995) was an American [biochemist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biochemist "Biochemist"). He shared the 1972 [Nobel Prize in Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Chemistry "Nobel Prize in Chemistry") with [Stanford Moore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford_Moore "Stanford Moore") and [William Howard Stein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Howard_Stein "William Howard Stein") for work on [ribonuclease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ribonuclease "Ribonuclease"), especially concerning the connection between the [amino acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amino_acid "Amino acid") [sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sequence "Sequence") and the biologically active conformation (see [Anfinsen's dogma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anfinsen's_dogma "Anfinsen's dogma")).

## Background

Anfinsen was born in [Monessen, Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monessen,_Pennsylvania "Monessen, Pennsylvania"), into a family of [Norwegian-American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norwegian-American "Norwegian-American") immigrants. His parents were Sophie (née Rasmussen) and Christian Boehmer Anfinsen Sr., a mechanical engineer. The family moved to Philadelphia in the 1920s. In 1933, he went to [Swarthmore College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swarthmore_College "Swarthmore College") where he played varsity football and earned a bachelor's degree in chemistry in 1937.

In 1939, he earned a [master's degree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master's_degree "Master's degree") in organic chemistry from the [University of Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Pennsylvania "University of Pennsylvania") and was awarded an [American-Scandinavian Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American-Scandinavian_Foundation "American-Scandinavian Foundation") fellowship to develop new methods for analyzing the chemical structure of complex proteins, namely [enzymes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enzymes "Enzymes"), at the [Carlsberg Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carlsberg_Laboratory "Carlsberg Laboratory") in [Copenhagen, Denmark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copenhagen,_Denmark "Copenhagen, Denmark"). In 1941, Anfinsen was offered a university fellowship for doctoral study in the Department of Biological Chemistry at [Harvard Medical School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_Medical_School "Harvard Medical School") where he received his [PhD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PhD "PhD") in biochemistry in 1943. During [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II") he worked for the [Office of Scientific Research and Development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Office_of_Scientific_Research_and_Development "Office of Scientific Research and Development").

Anfinsen had three children with his first wife, Florence Kenenger, to whom he was married from 1941 to 1978. In 1979, he married Libby Shulman Ely, with whom he had 4 stepchildren, and [converted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conversion_to_Judaism "Conversion to Judaism") to Orthodox Judaism. However, Anfinsen wrote in 1987 that "my feelings about religion still very strongly reflect a fifty-year period of orthodox agnosticism."

His papers were donated to the National Library of Medicine by Libby Anfinsen between 1998 and 1999.

## Career

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/8a/Christian_B._AnfinsenNIH.jpg/250px-Christian_B._AnfinsenNIH.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Christian_B._AnfinsenNIH.jpg)

Anfinsen in the lab

In 1950, the [National Heart Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Heart_Institute "National Heart Institute"), part of the [National Institutes of Health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Institutes_of_Health "National Institutes of Health") in [Bethesda, Maryland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bethesda,_Maryland "Bethesda, Maryland"), recruited Anfinsen as chief of its laboratory of [cell physiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_physiology "Cell physiology"). In 1954, a [Rockefeller Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rockefeller_Foundation "Rockefeller Foundation") fellowship enabled Anfinsen to return to the Carlsberg Laboratory for a year and a [Guggenheim Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Simon_Guggenheim_Memorial_Foundation "John Simon Guggenheim Memorial Foundation") fellowship allowed him to study at the [Weizmann Institute of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weizmann_Institute_of_Science "Weizmann Institute of Science") in [Rehovot, Israel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rehovot,_Israel "Rehovot, Israel") from 1958 to 1959. He was elected a Fellow of the [American Academy of Arts and Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Academy_of_Arts_and_Sciences "American Academy of Arts and Sciences") in 1958.

In 1962, Anfinsen returned to Harvard Medical School as a visiting professor and was invited to become chair of the department of chemistry. He was subsequently appointed chief of the laboratory of chemical biology at the National Institute of Arthritis and Metabolic Diseases (now the National Institute of Arthritis, Diabetes, and Digestive and Kidney Diseases), where he remained until 1981. In 1981, Anfinsen became a founding member of the [World Cultural Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Cultural_Council "World Cultural Council"). From 1982 until his death in 1995, Anfinsen was Professor of Biology and (Physical) Biochemistry at [Johns Hopkins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johns_Hopkins_University "Johns Hopkins University").

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/46/RibonucleaseA_SS_line.png/250px-RibonucleaseA_SS_line.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:RibonucleaseA_SS_line.png)

Ribonuclease A 3D structure, with SS bonds in gold

Anfinsen published more than 200 original articles, mostly in the area of the relationships between structure and function in proteins, as well as a book, The Molecular Basis of Evolution (1959), in which he described the relationships between protein chemistry and genetics and the promise those areas held for the understanding of evolution. He was also a pioneer of ideas in the area of nucleic acid compaction. In 1961, he showed that [ribonuclease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ribonuclease "Ribonuclease") could be refolded after denaturation while preserving enzyme activity, thereby suggesting that all the information required by protein to adopt its final conformation is encoded in its [amino-acid sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primary_structure "Primary structure"). He belonged to the [National Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_National_Academy_of_Sciences "United States National Academy of Sciences") (USA), the [Royal Danish Academy of Sciences and Letters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Danish_Academy_of_Sciences_and_Letters "Royal Danish Academy of Sciences and Letters") and the [American Philosophical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Philosophical_Society "American Philosophical Society").

## Christian B. Anfinsen Award

Established in 1996, The Christian B. Anfinsen Award is presented annually to distinguished scientists, the Awards recognize excellence and outstanding achievements in the multidisciplinary fields of protein science, and honor distinguished contributions in the areas of leadership, education, or service. It is sponsored by the [Protein Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protein_Society "Protein Society"), and recognizes significant technical achievements in the field of protein science.
