**Sir John Cowdery Kendrew**, [CBE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commander_of_the_Order_of_the_British_Empire "Commander of the Order of the British Empire") [FRS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow_of_the_Royal_Society "Fellow of the Royal Society") (24 March 1917 – 23 August 1997) was an [English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/England "England") [biochemist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biochemist "Biochemist"), [crystallographer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystallography "Crystallography"), and science administrator. Kendrew shared the 1962 [Nobel Prize in Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Chemistry "Nobel Prize in Chemistry") with [Max Perutz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Perutz "Max Perutz"), for their work at the [Cavendish Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cavendish_Laboratory "Cavendish Laboratory") to investigate the structure of [haem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heme "Heme")-containing [proteins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protein "Protein").

## Education and early life

Kendrew was born in [Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford "Oxford"), son of [Wilfrid George Kendrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilfrid_George_Kendrew "Wilfrid George Kendrew"), [reader](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reader_(academic_rank) "Reader (academic rank)") in [climatology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climatology "Climatology") in the [University of Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Oxford "University of Oxford"), and [Evelyn May Graham Sandburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evelyn_Sandberg-Vavalà "Evelyn Sandberg-Vavalà"), art historian. After preparatory school at the [Dragon School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dragon_School "Dragon School") in Oxford, he was educated at [Clifton College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clifton_College "Clifton College") in [Bristol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol "Bristol"), 1930–1936. He attended [Trinity College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College,_Cambridge "Trinity College, Cambridge"), [Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Cambridge "University of Cambridge") in 1936, as a Major Scholar, graduating in chemistry in 1939. He spent the early months of [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II") doing research on reaction kinetics, and then became a member of the Air Ministry Research Establishment, working on [radar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radar "Radar"). In 1940 he became engaged in operational research at the [Royal Air Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Air_Force "Royal Air Force") headquarters; commissioned a [squadron leader](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Squadron_leader "Squadron leader") on 17 September 1941, he was appointed an honorary wing commander on 8 June 1944, and relinquished his commission on 5 June 1945. He was awarded his PhD after the war in 1949.

## Research and career

During the war years, he became increasingly interested in biochemical problems, and decided to work on the structure of proteins.

### Crystallography

In 1945 he approached [Max Perutz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Perutz "Max Perutz") in the [Cavendish Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cavendish_Laboratory "Cavendish Laboratory") in [Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge "Cambridge"). [Joseph Barcroft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Barcroft "Joseph Barcroft"), a respiratory physiologist, suggested he might make a comparative protein crystallographic study of adult and foetal sheep haemoglobin, and he started that work.

In 1947 he became a Fellow of [Peterhouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peterhouse,_Cambridge "Peterhouse, Cambridge"); and the [Medical Research Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_Research_Council_(UK) "Medical Research Council (UK)") (MRC) agreed to create a research unit for the study of the molecular structure of biological systems, under the direction of Sir [Lawrence Bragg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawrence_Bragg "Lawrence Bragg"). In 1954 he became a Reader at the Davy-Faraday Laboratory of the [Royal Institution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Institution "Royal Institution") in [London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London "London").

### Crystal structure of myoglobin

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/ec/KendrewMyoglobin.jpg/330px-KendrewMyoglobin.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:KendrewMyoglobin.jpg)

John Kendrew with model of myoglobin in progress. Copyright by the Laboratory of Molecular Biology in Cambridge, England.

Kendrew shared the 1962 [Nobel Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize "Nobel Prize") for [chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemistry "Chemistry") with [Max Perutz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Perutz "Max Perutz") for determining the first atomic structures of [proteins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protein "Protein") using [X-ray crystallography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray_crystallography "X-ray crystallography"). Their work was done at what is now the [MRC Laboratory of Molecular Biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MRC_Laboratory_of_Molecular_Biology "MRC Laboratory of Molecular Biology") in [Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge "Cambridge"). Kendrew determined the structure of the protein [myoglobin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myoglobin "Myoglobin"), which stores oxygen in [muscle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muscle "Muscle") [cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_(biology) "Cell (biology)").

In 1947 the MRC agreed to make a research unit for the Study of the Molecular Structure of Biological Systems. The original studies were on the structure of sheep haemoglobin, but when this work had progressed as far as was possible using the resources then available, Kendrew embarked on the study of [myoglobin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myoglobin "Myoglobin"), a molecule only a quarter the size of the haemoglobin molecule. His initial source of raw material was [horse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horse "Horse") heart, but the crystals thus obtained were too small for X-ray analysis. Kendrew realized that the oxygen-conserving tissue of [diving mammals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diving_mammals "Diving mammals") could offer a better prospect, and a chance encounter led to his acquiring a large chunk of [whale meat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whale_meat "Whale meat") from Peru. Whale myoglobin did give large crystals with clean X-ray diffraction patterns. However, the problem still remained insurmountable, until in 1953 [Max Perutz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Perutz "Max Perutz") discovered that the [phase problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_problem "Phase problem") in analysis of the diffraction patterns could be solved by [multiple isomorphous replacement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiple_isomorphous_replacement "Multiple isomorphous replacement") — comparison of patterns from several crystals; one from the native protein, and others that had been soaked in solutions of heavy metals and had metal ions introduced in different well-defined positions. An electron density map at 6 [angstrom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angstrom "Angstrom") (0.6 [nanometre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanometre "Nanometre")) resolution was obtained by 1957, and by 1959 an atomic model could be built at 2 angstrom (0.2 nm) resolution.

### Later career

In 1963, Kendrew became one of the founders of the [European Molecular Biology Organization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Molecular_Biology_Organization "European Molecular Biology Organization"); he also founded the *[Journal of Molecular Biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Journal_of_Molecular_Biology "Journal of Molecular Biology")* and was for many years its editor-in-chief. He became Fellow of the [American Society of Biological Chemists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Society_of_Biological_Chemists "American Society of Biological Chemists") in 1967 and honorary member of the International Academy of Science, Munich. In 1974, he succeeded in persuading governments to establish the [European Molecular Biology Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Molecular_Biology_Laboratory "European Molecular Biology Laboratory") (EMBL) in [Heidelberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heidelberg "Heidelberg") and became its first director. He was knighted in 1974. From 1974 to 1979, he was a Trustee of the [British Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Museum "British Museum"), and from 1974 to 1988 he was successively Secretary General, Vice-President, and President of the [International Council of Scientific Unions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Council_of_Scientific_Unions "International Council of Scientific Unions").

After his retirement from EMBL, Kendrew became President of [St John's College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_John's_College,_Oxford "St John's College, Oxford") at the [University of Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Oxford "University of Oxford"), a post he held from 1981 to 1987. In his will, he designated his bequest to St John's College for studentships in science and in music, for students from developing countries. The *Kendrew Quadrangle* at St John's College in Oxford, officially opened on 16 October 2010, is named after him.

Kendrew was married to the former Elizabeth Jarvie (née Gorvin) from 1948 to 1956. Their marriage ended in divorce. Kendrew was subsequently partners with the artist Ruth Harris. He had no surviving children.

A biography of Kendrew, entitled *A Place in History: The Biography of John C. Kendrew*, by [Paul M. Wassarman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_M._Wassarman "Paul M. Wassarman") was published by [Oxford University Press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford_University_Press "Oxford University Press") in 2020.
