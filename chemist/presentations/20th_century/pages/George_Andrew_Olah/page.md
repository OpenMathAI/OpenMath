**George Andrew Olah** (born **Oláh András György**; May 22, 1927 – March 8, 2017) was a Hungarian-American [chemist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemist "Chemist"). His research involved the generation and reactivity of [carbocations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbocation "Carbocation") via [superacids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superacids "Superacids"). For this research, Olah was awarded a [Nobel Prize in Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Chemistry "Nobel Prize in Chemistry") in 1994 "for his contribution to carbocation chemistry." He was also awarded the [Priestley Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Priestley_Medal "Priestley Medal"), the highest honor granted by the [American Chemical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Chemical_Society "American Chemical Society") and [F.A. Cotton Medal for Excellence in Chemical Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F._Albert_Cotton#F.A._Cotton_Medal_for_Excellence_in_Chemical_Research "F. Albert Cotton") of the [American Chemical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Chemical_Society "American Chemical Society") in 1996.

After the [Hungarian Revolution of 1956](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungarian_Revolution_of_1956 "Hungarian Revolution of 1956"), he immigrated to the United Kingdom, which he left for Canada in 1964, finally resettling in the United States in 1965. According to [György Marx](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/György_Marx "György Marx"), he was one of [The Martians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Martians_(scientists) "The Martians (scientists)").

## Early life and education

Olah was born in [Budapest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Budapest "Budapest"), Hungary, on May 22, 1927, to a [Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish "Jewish") couple, Magda (Krasznai) and Gyula Oláh, a lawyer. After the high school of Budapesti [Piarist Gimnazium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piarists "Piarists"), he studied under organic chemist [Géza Zemplén](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Géza_Zemplén "Géza Zemplén") at the Technical University of Budapest, now the [Budapest University of Technology and Economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Budapest_University_of_Technology_and_Economics "Budapest University of Technology and Economics"), where he earned M.S. and Ph.D. degrees in chemical engineering. From 1949 through 1954, he taught at the school as a professor of organic chemistry. In the subsequent two years, from 1954 to 1956, he worked at the research institute of the [Hungarian Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungarian_Academy_of_Sciences "Hungarian Academy of Sciences"), where he was associate scientific director and head of the department of organic chemistry.

## Career and research

As a result of the [1956 Hungarian Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1956_Hungarian_Revolution "1956 Hungarian Revolution"), he and his family moved briefly to England and then to Canada, where he joined [Dow Chemical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dow_Chemical "Dow Chemical") in [Sarnia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarnia "Sarnia"), Ontario, with another Hungarian chemist, Stephen J. Kuhn. Olah's pioneering work on carbocations started during his eight years with Dow. In 1965, he returned to academia at [Case Western Reserve University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Case_Western_Reserve_University "Case Western Reserve University") in Cleveland, Ohio, chairing the department of chemistry from 1965 to 1969, and from 1967 through 1977 he was the C. F. Maybery Distinguished Professor of Research in Chemistry. In 1971, Olah became a [naturalized citizen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naturalized_citizen "Naturalized citizen") of the United States. He then moved to the [University of Southern California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Southern_California "University of Southern California") in 1977.

At USC, Olah was a distinguished professor and the director of the [Loker Hydrocarbon Research Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loker_Hydrocarbon_Research_Institute "Loker Hydrocarbon Research Institute"). Starting in 1980, he served as the Distinguished Donald P. and Katherine B. Loker Professor
of Chemistry and later became a distinguished professor in USC's school of engineering. In 1994, Olah was awarded the [Nobel Prize in Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Chemistry "Nobel Prize in Chemistry") "for his contribution to [carbocation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbocation "Carbocation") chemistry". In particular, Olah's search for stable nonclassical [carbocations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbocation "Carbocation") led to the discovery of protonated [methane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methane "Methane") stabilized by [superacids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superacids "Superacids"), like FSO3H-SbF5 ("[Magic Acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magic_acid "Magic acid")").

:   CH4 + H+ → CH5+

Because these cations were able to be stabilized, scientists could now use [infrared spectroscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infrared_spectroscopy "Infrared spectroscopy") and [nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_magnetic_resonance_spectroscopy "Nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy") to study them in greater depth, as well as use them as catalysts in organic synthesis reactions.

Olah, with Canadian chemist [Saul Winstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saul_Winstein "Saul Winstein"), was also involved in a career-long battle with [Herbert C. Brown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbert_C._Brown "Herbert C. Brown") of [Purdue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purdue "Purdue") over the existence of so-called "[nonclassical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-classical_ion "Non-classical ion")" carbocations – such as the [norbornyl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norbornane "Norbornane") cation, which can be depicted as cationic character delocalized over several bonds. Olah's studies of the cation with NMR spectroscopy provided more evidence suggesting that Winstein's model of the non-classical cation, "featuring a pair of [delocalized] electrons smeared between three carbon atoms," was correct.

In 1997, the Olah family formed an [endowment fund](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Financial_endowment "Financial endowment") (the George A. Olah Endowment) which grants annual awards to outstanding chemists, including the George A. Olah Award in Hydrocarbon or Petroleum Chemistry, formerly known as the ACS Award in Petroleum Chemistry. The awards are selected and administered by the [American Chemical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Chemical_Society "American Chemical Society").

Later in his career, his research shifted from [hydrocarbons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrocarbon "Hydrocarbon") and their transformation into fuel to the [methanol economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methanol_economy "Methanol economy"), namely [generating methanol from methane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methane_functionalization "Methane functionalization"). He joined with [Robert Zubrin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Zubrin "Robert Zubrin"), [Anne Korin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anne_Korin "Anne Korin"), and [James Woolsey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Woolsey "James Woolsey") in promoting a flexible-fuel mandate initiative. In 2005, Olah wrote an essay promoting the methanol economy in which he suggested that methanol could be produced from [hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen "Hydrogen") gas (H2) and industrially derived or atmospheric [carbon dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide "Carbon dioxide") (CO2), using energy from renewable and nuclear sources to power the production process.

## Personal life

He married Judit Ágnes Lengyel (*Judith Agnes Lengyel*) in 1949, and they had two children, György (*George*), born in Hungary in 1954, and Ronald, born in the U.S. in 1959. Olah died on March 8, 2017, at his home in [Beverly Hills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beverly_Hills "Beverly Hills"), California. After his death, the Hungarian government said that the "country has lost a great patriot and one of the most outstanding figures of Hungarian scientific life."
