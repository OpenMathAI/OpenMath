**Peter Dennis Mitchell** [FRS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow_of_the_Royal_Society "Fellow of the Royal Society") (29 September 1920 – 10 April 1992) was a [British](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_people "English people") [biochemist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biochemist "Biochemist") who was awarded the 1978 [Nobel Prize for Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_for_Chemistry "Nobel Prize for Chemistry") for his theory of the [chemiosmotic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemiosmosis#The_chemiosmotic_theory "Chemiosmosis") mechanism of [ATP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adenosine_triphosphate "Adenosine triphosphate") synthesis.

## Early life and education

Mitchell was born in [Mitcham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitcham "Mitcham"), [Surrey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surrey "Surrey") on 29 September 1920. His parents were Christopher Gibbs Mitchell, a [civil servant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_servant "Civil servant"), and Kate Beatrice Dorothy (née) Taplin. His uncle was [Sir Godfrey Mitchell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Godfrey_Mitchell "Godfrey Mitchell"), chairman of [George Wimpey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Wimpey "George Wimpey"). He was educated at [Queen's College, Taunton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen's_College,_Taunton "Queen's College, Taunton") and [Jesus College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesus_College,_Cambridge "Jesus College, Cambridge") where he studied the [Natural Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_Sciences_(Cambridge) "Natural Sciences (Cambridge)") [Tripos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tripos "Tripos") specialising in [Biochemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biochemistry "Biochemistry").

He was appointed a research post in the Department of Biochemistry, Cambridge, in 1942, and was awarded a [Ph.D.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Philosophy "Doctor of Philosophy") in early 1951 for work on the mode of action of [penicillin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penicillin "Penicillin").

## Career and research

In 1955 he was invited by Professor [Michael Swann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Swann "Michael Swann") to set up a biochemical research unit, called the Chemical Biology Unit, in the Department of Zoology, at the [University of Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Edinburgh "University of Edinburgh"), where he was appointed a Senior [Lecturer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lecturer "Lecturer") in 1961, then [Reader](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reader_(academic_rank) "Reader (academic rank)") in 1962, although institutional opposition to his work coupled with ill health led to his resignation in 1963.

From 1963 to 1965, he supervised the restoration of a [Regency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regency_architecture "Regency architecture")-fronted Mansion, known as [Glynn House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glynn_House "Glynn House"), at [Cardinham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardinham "Cardinham") near [Bodmin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bodmin "Bodmin"), [Cornwall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornwall "Cornwall") - adapting a major part of it for use as a research laboratory. He and his former research colleague, [Jennifer Moyle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jennifer_Moyle "Jennifer Moyle"), founded a charitable company, known as Glynn Research Ltd., to promote fundamental biological research at Glynn House and they embarked on a programme of research on [chemiosmotic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemiosmotic_potential "Chemiosmotic potential") reactions and reaction systems.

### Chemiosmotic hypothesis

In the 1960s, [ATP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adenosine_triphosphate "Adenosine triphosphate") was known to be the energy currency of life, but the mechanism by which ATP was created in the [mitochondria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitochondria "Mitochondria") was assumed to be by [substrate-level phosphorylation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Substrate-level_phosphorylation "Substrate-level phosphorylation"). Mitchell's [chemiosmotic hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemiosmosis "Chemiosmosis") was the basis for understanding the actual process of [oxidative phosphorylation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxidative_phosphorylation "Oxidative phosphorylation"). At the time, the biochemical mechanism of ATP synthesis by oxidative phosphorylation was unknown.

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/9f/Chemiosmosis.svg/250px-Chemiosmosis.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Chemiosmosis.svg)

In chemiosmosis, ions move down their electrochemical gradient across a membrane.

Mitchell realised that the movement of ions across an [electrochemical potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrochemical_potential "Electrochemical potential") difference could provide the energy needed to produce ATP. His hypothesis was derived from information that was well known in the 1960s. He knew that living cells had a [membrane potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Membrane_potential "Membrane potential"); interior negative to the environment. The movement of charged ions across a membrane is thus affected by the electrical forces (the attraction of positive to negative charges). Their movement is also affected by [thermodynamic forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_thermodynamics "Chemical thermodynamics"), the tendency of substances to [diffuse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffusion "Diffusion") from regions of higher concentration. He went on to show that ATP synthesis was coupled to this [electrochemical gradient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrochemical_gradient "Electrochemical gradient").

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/3d/ATP-Synthase.svg/250px-ATP-Synthase.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:ATP-Synthase.svg)

The discovery of ATP synthase vindicated Mitchell's hypothesis. Today, it is well-accepted that chemiosmosis of H+ ions power the synthesis of ATP, and other biochemical processes.

His hypothesis was confirmed by the discovery of [ATP synthase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ATP_synthase "ATP synthase"), a membrane-bound protein that uses the potential energy of the electrochemical gradient to make ATP; and by the discovery by [André Jagendorf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/André_Tridon_Jagendorf "André Tridon Jagendorf") that a pH difference across the [thylakoid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thylakoid "Thylakoid") membrane in the [chloroplast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloroplast "Chloroplast") results in ATP synthesis.

#### Protonmotive Q-cycle

Later, Peter Mitchell also hypothesized some of the complex details of electron transport chains. He conceived of the coupling of proton pumping to quinone-based [electron bifurcation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_bifurcation "Electron bifurcation"), which contributes to the proton motive force and thus, ATP synthesis.

### Awards and honours

In 1978 he was awarded the [Nobel Prize in Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Chemistry "Nobel Prize in Chemistry") "for his contribution to the understanding of [biological energy transfer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitochondrion#Energy_conversion "Mitochondrion") through the formulation of the [chemiosmotic theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemiosmosis "Chemiosmosis")." He was elected a [Fellow of the Royal Society (FRS) in 1974](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Fellows_of_the_Royal_Society_elected_in_1974 "List of Fellows of the Royal Society elected in 1974").
