**Richard Adolf Zsigmondy** ([Hungarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungarian_language "Hungarian language"): *Zsigmondy Richárd Adolf*; 1 April 1865 – 23 September 1929) was an Austrian-born [chemist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemist "Chemist"). He was known for his research in [colloids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colloids "Colloids"), for which he was awarded the [Nobel Prize in Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Chemistry "Nobel Prize in Chemistry") in 1925, as well as for co-inventing the slit-[ultramicroscope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultramicroscope "Ultramicroscope"), and different [membrane filters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Membrane_technology "Membrane technology"). The crater [Zsigmondy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zsigmondy_(crater) "Zsigmondy (crater)") on the Moon is named in his honour.

## Biography

### Early years

Zsigmondy was born in [Vienna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vienna "Vienna"), [Austrian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austrian_Empire "Austrian Empire"), to a [Hungarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungarians "Hungarians") [gentry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gentry "Gentry") family. His mother Irma Szakmáry, a poet born in [Martonvásár](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martonvásár "Martonvásár"), and his father, [Adolf Zsigmondy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adolf_Zsigmondy "Adolf Zsigmondy") Sr., a scientist from [Pressburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressburg "Pressburg") (Pozsony, today's [Bratislava](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bratislava "Bratislava")) who invented several surgical instruments for use in dentistry. Zsigmondy family members were Lutherans. They originated from Johannes ([Hungarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungarian_language "Hungarian language"): *János*) Sigmondi (1686–1746, [Bártfa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bártfa "Bártfa"), [Kingdom of Hungary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Hungary "Kingdom of Hungary")) and included teachers, priests and Hungarian freedom-fighters. Richard was raised by his mother after his father's early death in 1880, and received a comprehensive education. He enjoyed hobbies such as [climbing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climbing "Climbing") and [mountaineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mountaineering "Mountaineering") with his siblings. His elder brothers, [Otto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otto_Zsigmondy?action=edit&redlink=1 "Otto Zsigmondy") (a dentist) and [Emil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emil_Zsigmondy "Emil Zsigmondy") (a physician), were well-known mountain climbers; his younger brother, [Karl Zsigmondy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Zsigmondy "Karl Zsigmondy"), became a notable mathematician in Vienna. In high school, Richard developed an interest in natural science, especially in chemistry and physics, and experimented in his home laboratory.

He began his academic career at the [University of Vienna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Vienna "University of Vienna") Medical Faculty, but soon moved to the [Technical University of Vienna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technical_University_of_Vienna "Technical University of Vienna"), and later to the [Ludwig-Maximilians-Universität München](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludwig-Maximilians-Universität_München "Ludwig-Maximilians-Universität München"), to study chemistry under [Wilhelm von Miller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilhelm_von_Miller?action=edit&redlink=1 "Wilhelm von Miller") (1848–1899). At the Ludwig-Maximilians-Universität München, he conducted research on [indene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indene "Indene") and received his PhD from the [University of Erlangen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Erlangen–Nuremberg "University of Erlangen–Nuremberg") in 1889.

### Career

In 1885, Zsigmondy published his very first article as a joint publication with his Viennese professor [Rudolf Benedikt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudolf_Benedikt?action=edit&redlink=1 "Rudolf Benedikt") on a method of determining [glycerin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glycerin "Glycerin"). His 1887 article *Neue Lüster und Farben auf Glas* (about colours on glass) marked the beginning of a research area on which he would work for another 30 years. Zsigmondy left organic chemistry to join the physics group of [August Kundt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/August_Kundt "August Kundt") at the [Friedrich Wilhelm University of Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Wilhelm_University_of_Berlin "Friedrich Wilhelm University of Berlin").

In July 1892, Zsigmondy held a colloquium at [Graz University of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graz_University_of_Technology "Graz University of Technology") assessed among others by [Albert von Ettingshausen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_von_Ettingshausen "Albert von Ettingshausen") and [Friedrich Emich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Emich "Friedrich Emich") in order to qualify as assistant professor. There he also completed his [habilitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habilitation "Habilitation") in 1893. Because of his knowledge about glass and its colouring, in 1897 the [Schott Glass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schott_Glass "Schott Glass") factory offered him a job which he accepted. He invented the Jenaer Milchglas and conducted some research on the red [Ruby glass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cranberry_glass "Cranberry glass"). Lecturing activities in Graz were documented until 1899.

Zsigmondy left Schott Glass in 1900, but remained in Jena as private lecturer to conduct his research. Together with the optical instrument manufacturer [Zeiss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Zeiss_AG "Carl Zeiss AG"), he developed the slit [ultramicroscope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultramicroscope "Ultramicroscope"). His scientific career continued in 1908 at the [University of Göttingen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Göttingen "University of Göttingen"), where he stayed for the rest of his professional career as professor of inorganic chemistry. In 1925, Zsigmondy received the [Nobel Prize for Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_for_Chemistry "Nobel Prize for Chemistry") for his work on colloids and the methods he used, such as the [ultramicroscope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultramicroscope "Ultramicroscope") upon which based his investigation on the [Purple of Cassius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purple_of_Cassius "Purple of Cassius").

Before Zsigmondy finished his PhD thesis in organic chemistry, he published research on colouring glass with [silver salts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silver_salts "Silver salts") and dissolved silver particles, which he recovered by dissolving the glass in [hydrofluoric acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrofluoric_acid "Hydrofluoric acid").

Vintage cranberry glass bowl

During his stay in Graz, Zsigmondy accomplished his most notable research work, on the chemistry of [colloids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colloid "Colloid"). The exact mechanism which yields the red colour of the [Cranberry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cranberry_glass "Cranberry glass") or [Ruby glass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruby_glass "Ruby glass") was a result of his studies of colloids.

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/6b/ColloidalGold_aq.png/120px-ColloidalGold_aq.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:ColloidalGold_aq.png)

Aqueous [colloidal gold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colloidal_gold "Colloidal gold").

In later years he worked on gold [hydrosols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbal_distillate "Herbal distillate") and used them to characterize [protein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protein "Protein") solutions. While in Jena, he developed the slit [ultramicroscope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultramicroscope "Ultramicroscope") together with [Henry Siedentopf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Siedentopf "Henry Siedentopf"). After moving to Göttingen, Zsigmondy improved his optical equipment for the observation of finest nanoparticles suspended in liquid solution. As a result, he introduced the immersion ultramicroscope in 1912.

Together with [Wilhelm Bachmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilhelm_Bachmann?action=edit&redlink=1 "Wilhelm Bachmann"), Zsigmondy developed a new membrane filter (1916). He later transferred his patents to a company established by him, other shareholders and [Sartorius AG](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sartorius_AG "Sartorius AG") which was [incorporated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incorporation_(business) "Incorporation (business)") to Sartorius in the late 1970s.

### Private life

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/9e/G%C3%B6ttingen_Stadtfriedhof_Richard_Adolf_Zsigmondy.jpg/250px-G%C3%B6ttingen_Stadtfriedhof_Richard_Adolf_Zsigmondy.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Göttingen_Stadtfriedhof_Richard_Adolf_Zsigmondy.jpg)

Göttingen, grave Zsigmondy's

In 1903, Zsigmondy married Laura Luise Müller, with whom he had two daughters, Annemarie and Käthe.

Richard Zsigmondy died due to his arteriosclerosis only a few months after retiring from his position at the University of Göttingen in early March.

He was a cousin of the architect [Frigyes Schulek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frigyes_Schulek "Frigyes Schulek"), whose mother was Auguszta Zsigmondy. He is also related to the violinist [Dénes Zsigmondy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dénes_Zsigmondy "Dénes Zsigmondy").
