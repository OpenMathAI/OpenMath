**Paul Delos Boyer** (July 31, 1918 – June 2, 2018) was an American [biochemist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biochemist "Biochemist"), [analytical chemist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytical_chemist "Analytical chemist"), and a professor of chemistry at [University of California, Los Angeles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Los_Angeles "University of California, Los Angeles") (UCLA). He shared the 1997 [Nobel Prize in Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Chemistry "Nobel Prize in Chemistry") for [research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Research "Research") on the "[enzymatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enzyme "Enzyme") mechanism underlying the [biosynthesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biosynthesis "Biosynthesis") of [adenosine triphosphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adenosine_triphosphate "Adenosine triphosphate") (ATP)" ([ATP synthase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ATP_synthase "ATP synthase")) with [John E. Walker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_E._Walker "John E. Walker"), making Boyer the first [Utah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utah "Utah")-born Nobel laureate; the remainder of the Prize in that year was awarded to Danish chemist [Jens Christian Skou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jens_Christian_Skou "Jens Christian Skou") for his discovery of the [Na+/K+-ATPase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Na+/K+-ATPase "Na+/K+-ATPase").

## Birth and education

Boyer was born in [Provo, Utah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Provo,_Utah "Provo, Utah"), and grew up in a nonpracticing Mormon family of Dutch, German, French, and English descent. He attended [Provo High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Provo_High_School "Provo High School"), where he was active in [student government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Student_government "Student government") and the debating team. He was also his high schools valedictorian and played intramural basketball in high school and college. He received a [B.S.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_Science "Bachelor of Science") in [chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemistry "Chemistry") from [Brigham Young University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brigham_Young_University "Brigham Young University") in 1939 and obtained a Wisconsin Alumni Research [Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foundation_(nonprofit_organization) "Foundation (nonprofit organization)") [Scholarship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scholarship "Scholarship") for graduate studies. Five days before leaving for [Wisconsin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wisconsin "Wisconsin"), Paul married Lyda Whicker in 1939, and they remained married for nearly eighty years until his death in 2018, making him the longest-married Nobel laureate. The Boyers had three children.

Though the Boyers connected with the Mormon community in [Wisconsin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wisconsin "Wisconsin"), they considered themselves "on the wayward fringe" and doubted the doctrinal beliefs of [the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Church_of_Jesus_Christ_of_Latter-day_Saints "The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints") (LDS Church). After experimenting with [Unitarianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unitarianism "Unitarianism"), Boyer eventually became an [atheist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atheist "Atheist"). In 2003 he was one of 22 Nobel laureates who signed the [Humanist Manifesto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humanism_and_Its_Aspirations "Humanism and Its Aspirations").

## Academic career

After Boyer received his [Ph.D.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctorate_of_Philosophy "Doctorate of Philosophy") degree in [biochemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biochemistry "Biochemistry") from the [University of Wisconsin–Madison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Wisconsin–Madison "University of Wisconsin–Madison") in 1943, he spent years at [Stanford University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford_University "Stanford University") on a war-related research project dedicated to stabilization of serum albumin for transfusions. He began his independent research career at the [University of Minnesota](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Minnesota "University of Minnesota") and introduced kinetic, isotopic, and chemical methods for investigating [enzyme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enzyme "Enzyme") mechanisms. In 1955, he received a [Guggenheim Fellowship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guggenheim_Fellowship "Guggenheim Fellowship") and worked with [Professor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Professor "Professor") [Hugo Theorell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugo_Theorell "Hugo Theorell") on the mechanism of [alcohol dehydrogenase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alcohol_dehydrogenase "Alcohol dehydrogenase"). In 1956, he accepted a Hill Foundation Professorship and moved to the medical campus of the [University of Minnesota](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Minnesota "University of Minnesota"). In 1959–1960, he served as [Chairman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chairman "Chairman") of the Biochemistry Section of the [American Chemical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Chemical_Society "American Chemical Society") (ACS) and in 1969–1970 as [President](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_(corporate_title) "President (corporate title)") of the American Society of Biological Chemists.

Since 1963, he had been a professor in the department of chemistry and biochemistry at [University of California, Los Angeles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Los_Angeles "University of California, Los Angeles"). In 1965, he became the founding director of the [Molecular Biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_biology "Molecular biology") Institute and spearheaded the construction of the building and the organization of an interdepartmental Ph.D. program. This institutional service did not diminish the creativity and originality of his research program, which led to three postulates for the binding mechanism for [ATP synthesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ATP_synthesis "ATP synthesis")—that energy input was not used primarily to form ATP but to promote the binding of [phosphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phosphate "Phosphate") and mostly the release of tightly bound ATP; that three identical catalytic sites went through compulsory, sequential binding changes; and that the binding changes of the catalytic subunits, circularly arranged on the periphery of the enzyme, were driven by the rotation of a smaller internal [subunit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protein_subunit "Protein subunit").

Paul Boyer was [editor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Editing "Editing") or associate editor of the *[Annual Review of Biochemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annual_Review_of_Biochemistry "Annual Review of Biochemistry")* from 1963 to 1989. He was editor of the classic series, "The Enzymes". When he worked on the series "The Enzymes", he was helped by his wife Lyda as she was a professional editor at UCLA. In 1981, he was faculty research lecturer at UCLA. In that same year, he was awarded the prestigious [Tolman Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tolman_Award "Tolman Award") by the Southern California Section of the [American Chemical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Chemical_Society "American Chemical Society").

## Death

Boyer died of [respiratory failure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Respiratory_failure "Respiratory failure") on June 2, 2018, at the age of 99, less than two months shy of his 100th birthday at his [Los Angeles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Los_Angeles "Los Angeles") home.
