**Sir Geoffrey Wilkinson** [FRS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow_of_the_Royal_Society "Fellow of the Royal Society") (14 July 1921 – 26 September 1996) was a [Nobel laureate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_laureate "Nobel laureate") English [chemist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemist "Chemist") who pioneered [inorganic chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inorganic_chemistry "Inorganic chemistry") and homogeneous [transition metal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transition_metal "Transition metal") [catalysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catalysis "Catalysis").

## Early life and education

Wilkinson was born at Springside, [Todmorden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Todmorden "Todmorden"), in the [West Riding of Yorkshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Riding_of_Yorkshire "West Riding of Yorkshire"). His father, Henry Wilkinson, was a master [house painter and decorator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_painter_and_decorator "House painter and decorator"); his mother, Ruth, worked in a local [cotton mill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cotton_mill "Cotton mill"). One of his uncles, an organist and [choirmaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conducting "Conducting"), had married into a family that owned a small chemical company making [Epsom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnesium_sulfate "Magnesium sulfate") and [Glauber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium_sulfate "Sodium sulfate")'s salts for the [pharmaceutical industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pharmaceutical_industry "Pharmaceutical industry"); this is where he first developed an interest in chemistry.

He was educated at the local council primary school and, after winning a County Scholarship in 1932, went to [Todmorden Grammar School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Todmorden_Grammar_School "Todmorden Grammar School"). His [physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics "Physics") teacher there, Luke Sutcliffe, had also taught Sir [John Cockcroft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Cockcroft "John Cockcroft"), who received a Nobel Prize for "splitting the atom". In 1939 he obtained a Royal Scholarship for study at [Imperial College London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_College_London "Imperial College London"), from where he graduated in 1941, with his PhD awarded in 1946 entitled "Some physico-chemical observations of hydrolysis in the homogeneous vapour phase".

## Career and research

In 1942 Professor [Friedrich Paneth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Paneth "Friedrich Paneth") was recruiting young chemists for the [nuclear energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_power "Nuclear power") project. Wilkinson joined and was sent out to Canada, where he stayed in [Montreal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montreal "Montreal") and later [Chalk River Laboratories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chalk_River_Laboratories "Chalk River Laboratories") until he could leave in 1946. For the next four years he worked with Professor [Glenn T. Seaborg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glenn_T._Seaborg "Glenn T. Seaborg") at [University of California, Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Berkeley "University of California, Berkeley"), mostly on nuclear taxonomy. He then became a research associate at the [Massachusetts Institute of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massachusetts_Institute_of_Technology "Massachusetts Institute of Technology") and began to return to his first interest as a student – [transition metal complexes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_(chemistry) "Complex (chemistry)") of [ligands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ligand "Ligand") such as [carbon monoxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_monoxide "Carbon monoxide") and [olefins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olefin "Olefin").

He was at [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University "Harvard University") from September 1951 until he returned to England in December 1955, with a sabbatical break of nine months in [Copenhagen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copenhagen "Copenhagen"). At Harvard, he still did some nuclear work on excitation functions for [protons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proton "Proton") in [cobalt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cobalt "Cobalt"), but had already begun to work on olefin complexes.

In June 1955 he was appointed to the chair of Inorganic Chemistry at [Imperial College London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_College_London "Imperial College London"), and from then on worked almost entirely on the complexes of transition metals.

Wilkinson is well known for his popularisation of the use of [Wilkinson's catalyst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilkinson's_catalyst "Wilkinson's catalyst") RhCl(PPh3)3 in catalytic hydrogenation, and for the discovery of the structure of [ferrocene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferrocene "Ferrocene"). Wilkinson's catalyst is used industrially in the [hydrogenation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogenation "Hydrogenation") of [alkenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkenes "Alkenes") to [alkanes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkanes "Alkanes").

Wilkinson supervised PhD students and postdoctoral researchers, including [F. Albert Cotton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F._Albert_Cotton "F. Albert Cotton"), [Richard A. Andersen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_A._Andersen_(chemist) "Richard A. Andersen (chemist)"), [John A. Osborn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_A._Osborn "John A. Osborn"), [Alan Davison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_Davison "Alan Davison") and [Malcolm Green](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malcolm_Green_(chemist) "Malcolm Green (chemist)"). Cotton and Wilkinson collaborated on the book *"Advanced Inorganic Chemistry"*, often referred to simply as "Cotton and Wilkinson", one of the standard inorganic chemistry textbooks.

## Awards and honours

Throughout his career, Wilkinson received many prestigious awards, including the [Nobel Prize in Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Chemistry "Nobel Prize in Chemistry") in 1973 for his work on "[organometallic compounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organometallic_compound "Organometallic compound")" (with [Ernst Otto Fischer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernst_Otto_Fischer "Ernst Otto Fischer")). Prior to winning the Nobel Prize, he had been elected a [Fellow of the Royal Society (FRS)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Fellows_of_the_Royal_Society_elected_in_1965 "List of Fellows of the Royal Society elected in 1965") in 1965. In 1968, he won the [Lavoisier Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lavoisier_Medal "Lavoisier Medal"), awarded by the [Chemical Society of France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Société_chimique_de_France "Société chimique de France"). In 1981, he received the [Royal Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Medal "Royal Medal") from the [Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society "Royal Society") as well as the [Ludwig Mond Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludwig_Mond_Award "Ludwig Mond Award") from the [Royal Society of Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society_of_Chemistry "Royal Society of Chemistry").

Wilkinson was made a [Knight Bachelor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knight_Bachelor "Knight Bachelor") by [Queen Elizabeth II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_II "Elizabeth II") in the [1976 Birthday Honours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1976_Birthday_Honours "1976 Birthday Honours"). Despite his knighthood, he did not consider himself part of the "establishment" and remained vocally critical of successive British prime ministers, education ministers and university vice-chancellors for insufficient support and funding for the sciences.

Wilkinson also received honorary doctorates and fellowships from several universities, including:

- [Columbia University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia_University "Columbia University") - [DSc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Science "Doctor of Science"), 1978
- [University of Bath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Bath "University of Bath") - DSc, 1980
- [University of Essex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Essex "University of Essex") - Honorary Doctorate, 1989
- Imperial College London - Honorary fellowship, 1993

## Personal life

Wilkinson was married to Lise Schou, a Danish plant physiologist whom he had met at UC Berkeley. They had two daughters, Anne and Pernille.

## Legacy and commemoration

Since 1999, the Royal Society of Chemistry has been awarding the annual Sir Geoffrey Wilkinson Prize (formerly the Sir Geoffrey Wilkinson Lectureship), aimed at celebrating contributions to any area of inorganic chemistry made by a mid-career scientist. The first winner of this prize was Professor [Malcolm Green](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malcolm_Green_(chemist) "Malcolm Green (chemist)"), one of Wilkinson's former students. Since 2022, Imperial College London has been hosting the annual Sir Geoffrey Wilkinson Lecture. Both the Royal Society of Chemistry prize and the annual lecture at Imperial are funded by the Geoffery Wilkinson Charitable Foundation, run by Professor Anne Hardy, one of Wilkinson's daughters. Since 2016, the foundation has also been funding an annual PhD studentship at Imperial in the field of inorganic chemistry.

A blue plaque was installed at 4 Wellington Road, [Todmorden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Todmorden "Todmorden") in 1990, marking Wilkinson's childhood home. In 2007, the Royal Society of Chemistry erected a plaque at [Imperial College London's Chemistry Building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Department_of_Chemistry,_Imperial_College_London "Department of Chemistry, Imperial College London") in honour of Wilkinson as part of its [Chemical Landmark Scheme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_blue_plaques_erected_by_the_Royal_Society_of_Chemistry#Chemical_Landmark_Scheme "List of blue plaques erected by the Royal Society of Chemistry"). Imperial College's [Wilkinson Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_College_Halls_of_Residence#Eastside "Imperial College Halls of Residence"), opened in 2009, is named in the chemist's honour.
