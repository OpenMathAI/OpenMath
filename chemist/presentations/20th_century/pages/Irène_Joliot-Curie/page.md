**Irène Joliot-Curie** (French: [[iʁɛn ʒɔljo kyʁi]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/French "Help:IPA/French") [ⓘ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Fr-Ir%C3%A8ne_Joliot-Curie.ogg "File:Fr-Irène Joliot-Curie.ogg"); [née](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_name#Maiden_and_married_names "Birth name") **Curie**; 12 September 1897 – 17 March 1956) was a French [chemist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemist "Chemist") and [physicist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physicist "Physicist") who received the 1935 [Nobel Prize in Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Chemistry "Nobel Prize in Chemistry") with her husband, [Frédéric Joliot-Curie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frédéric_Joliot-Curie "Frédéric Joliot-Curie"), for their discovery of [induced radioactivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Induced_radioactivity "Induced radioactivity"). The couple also discovered [positron emission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positron_emission "Positron emission") in their process. They were the [second married couple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize#Statistics "Nobel Prize"), after her parents, to win the Nobel Prize, adding to the [Curie family legacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize#Family_laureates "Nobel Prize") of five Nobel Prizes. This made the [Curies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curie_family "Curie family") the family with the most Nobel laureates to date.

Her mother Marie Skłodowska-Curie and she also form the only [mother–daughter pair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_second-generation_physicists "List of second-generation physicists") to have won Nobel Prizes whilst Pierre and Irène Curie form the only father-daughter pair to have won Nobel Prizes by the same occasion, whilst six father-son pairs have won Nobel Prizes by comparison.

She was also one of the first three women to be a member of a French government, becoming [undersecretary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Undersecretary_of_State_(France) "Undersecretary of State (France)") for Scientific Research under the [Popular Front](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Popular_Front_(France) "Popular Front (France)") in 1936. Both children of the Joliot-Curies, [Hélène](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hélène_Langevin-Joliot "Hélène Langevin-Joliot") and [Pierre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Joliot "Pierre Joliot"), are also scientists.

In 1945, she was one of the six commissioners of the new [French Alternative Energies and Atomic Energy Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Alternative_Energies_and_Atomic_Energy_Commission "French Alternative Energies and Atomic Energy Commission") (CEA) created by de Gaulle and the [Provisional Government of the French Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Provisional_Government_of_the_French_Republic "Provisional Government of the French Republic"). She died in Paris on 17 March 1956 from an [acute leukemia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acute_leukemia "Acute leukemia") linked to her exposure to [polonium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polonium "Polonium") and [X-rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray "X-ray").

## Biography

### Early life and education

Irène was born in Paris, France, on 12 September 1897. She was the first of [Marie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marie_Curie "Marie Curie") and [Pierre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Curie "Pierre Curie")'s two daughters. Her sister was named [Ève](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ève_Curie "Ève Curie"), born in 1904 also in Paris. They lost their father early on in 1906 due to a horse-drawn wagon incident, and Marie was left to raise them. Education was important to Marie and Irène's education began at a school near the Paris Observatory. This school was chosen because it had a more challenging curriculum than the school nearby the Curie's home. In 1906, it was obvious Irène was talented in mathematics. Her mother chose to focus on that instead of public school. Marie joined forces with a number of eminent French scholars, including the prominent French physicist [Paul Langevin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Langevin "Paul Langevin"), to form "[The Cooperative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Cooperative "The Cooperative")", which included a private gathering of nine students who were children of the most distinguished academics in France. Each contributed to educating these children in their respective homes. The curriculum of The Cooperative was varied and included not only the principles of science and scientific research but also such diverse subjects as Chinese and sculpture, and with great emphasis placed on self-expression and play. Irène studied in this environment for about two years.

Irène and her sister [Ève](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ève_Curie "Ève Curie") were sent to Poland to spend the summer with their Aunt Bronia (Marie's sister) when Irène was thirteen. Irène's education was so rigorous that she still had a German and trigonometry lesson every day of that break. Irène re-entered a more orthodox learning environment by going back to high school at the [Collège Sévigné](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collège_Sévigné "Collège Sévigné") in central Paris until 1914. She then went onto the Faculty of Science at the [Sorbonne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Paris "University of Paris") to complete her [baccalaureate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor's_degree "Bachelor's degree"), until 1916 when her studies were interrupted by [World War I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I "World War I").

### World War I

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7a/Ir%C3%A8ne%2C_Marie_et_%C3%88ve_Curie.jpg/250px-Ir%C3%A8ne%2C_Marie_et_%C3%88ve_Curie.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Irène,_Marie_et_Ève_Curie.jpg)

Irène (left), her mother and her sister [Ève Curie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ève_Curie "Ève Curie")

Irène took a nursing course during college to assist her mother, Marie Curie, in the field as her assistant. She began her work as a nurse radiographer on the battlefield alongside her mother. After a few months she was left to work alone at a radiological facility in Belgium. She taught doctors how to locate shrapnel in bodies using radiology and taught herself how to repair the equipment. She moved throughout facilities and battlegrounds including two bombsites, [Furnes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Veurne "Veurne") and [Ypres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ypres "Ypres"), and [Amiens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amiens "Amiens"). She received a military medal for her assistance in X-ray facilities in France and Belgium.

After the war, Irène returned to the Sorbonne in Paris to complete her second baccalaureate degree in mathematics and physics in 1918. Irène then went on to work as her mother's assistant, teaching radiology at the [Radium Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curie_Institute,_Paris "Curie Institute, Paris"), which had been built by her parents. Her doctoral thesis was concerned with the [alpha decay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpha_decay "Alpha decay") of [polonium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polonium "Polonium"), the element discovered by her parents (along with radium) and named after Marie's country of birth, Poland. Irène became a Doctor of Science in 1925.

### Research

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/aa/Irene_and_Marie_Curie_1925.jpg/250px-Irene_and_Marie_Curie_1925.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Irene_and_Marie_Curie_1925.jpg)

Irène and [Marie Curie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marie_Curie "Marie Curie") in 1925

As she neared the end of her doctorate in 1924, Irène Curie was asked to teach the precision laboratory techniques required for radiochemical research to the young chemical engineer [Frédéric Joliot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frédéric_Joliot "Frédéric Joliot"), whom she would later wed. From 1928, Joliot-Curie and her husband, Frédéric, combined their research efforts on the study of atomic nuclei. In 1932, Joliot-Curie and her husband Frédéric had full access to Marie's polonium. Experiments were done using gamma rays to identify the [positron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positron "Positron"). Though their experiments identified both the [positron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positron "Positron") and the [neutron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron "Neutron"), they failed to interpret the significance of the results and the discoveries were later claimed by [Carl David Anderson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_David_Anderson "Carl David Anderson") and [James Chadwick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Chadwick "James Chadwick") respectively. Together with [J. J. Thomson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._J._Thomson "J. J. Thomson")'s discovery of the [electron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron "Electron") in 1897, they finally replaced [John Dalton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Dalton "John Dalton")'s [model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_theory#Dalton's_law_of_multiple_proportions "Atomic theory") of atoms as solid spherical particles.

In 1933, Joliot-Curie and her husband were the first to calculate accurately the mass of the neutron. The Joliot-Curies continued trying to make a name in the scientific community; in doing so, they developed a new theory from an interesting experiment they conducted. During an experiment bombarding aluminium with alpha rays, they discovered that only protons were detected. Based on the undetectable electron and positron pair, they proposed that the protons changed into neutrons and positrons. Later in October 1933, this new theory was presented to the Seventh [Solvay Conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solvay_Conference "Solvay Conference"). The Solvay Conferences consisted of prominent scientists in the physics and chemistry community. Irène and her husband presented their theory and results to their fellow scientists, but they received criticism of their findings from most of the 46 scientists attending. However, they were able to build on the controversial theory later on.

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/af/Ir%C3%A8ne_Joliot-Curie_Harcourt.jpg/250px-Ir%C3%A8ne_Joliot-Curie_Harcourt.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Irène_Joliot-Curie_Harcourt.jpg)

Curie, c. 1920s

In 1934, the Joliot-Curies made a discovery that sealed their place in scientific history. Building on the work of Marie and Pierre Curie, who had isolated naturally occurring radioactive elements, the Joliot-Curies succeeded in transforming one element into another by creating radioactive [nitrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen "Nitrogen") from [boron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boron "Boron"), radioactive isotopes of [phosphorus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phosphorus "Phosphorus") from aluminium, and [silicon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon "Silicon") from [magnesium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnesium "Magnesium"). Irradiating the natural stable isotope of aluminium with [alpha particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpha_particles "Alpha particles") (i.e. helium nuclei) resulted in an [unstable isotope of phosphorus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isotopes_of_phosphorus "Isotopes of phosphorus"): 27Al + 4He → 30P + 1n. This phosphorus isotope is not found in nature and decays emitting a positron. This discovery is formally known as [positron emission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positron_emission "Positron emission") or [beta decay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beta_decay "Beta decay"), where a proton in the radioactive nucleus changes to a neutron and releases a positron and an electron neutrino. By then, the application of radioactive materials for use in medicine was growing, and this discovery allowed radioactive materials to be created quickly, cheaply, and plentifully. The Nobel Prize for chemistry in 1935 brought with it fame and recognition from the scientific community and Joliot-Curie was awarded a professorship at the Faculty of Science.

The work that Irène's laboratory pioneered, research into radium nuclei, would also help another group of physicists within Germany. [Otto Hahn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otto_Hahn "Otto Hahn") and [Fritz Strassman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fritz_Strassman "Fritz Strassman") on 19 December 1938 bombarded uranium with neutrons, but misinterpreted their findings. [Lise Meitner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lise_Meitner "Lise Meitner") and Otto Frisch would theoretically correct Hahn and Strassmann's findings, and after replicating their experiment based on Hungarian physicist Leo Szilard's theory that he had confided to Meitner back in 1933, confirmed on 13 January 1939 that Hahn and Strassmann had indeed observed [nuclear fission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_fission "Nuclear fission"): the splitting of the nucleus itself, emitting vast amounts of energy. Lise Meitner's now-famous calculations actually proved that nuclear fission was possible and replicable.

In 1948, using work on nuclear fission, the Joliot-Curies, along with other scientists, created the first French nuclear reactor. The Joliot-Curies were a part of the organization in charge of the project, the Atomic Energy Commission, *[Commissariat à l'énergie atomique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commissariat_à_l'énergie_atomique "Commissariat à l'énergie atomique")* (CEA). Irène was the commissioner of the CEA, and Irène's husband, Frédéric, was the director of the CEA. The reactor, [Zoé](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoé_(reactor) "Zoé (reactor)") (Zéro énergie Oxyde et Eau lourde) used nuclear fission to generate five kilowatts of power. This was the beginning of nuclear energy as a source of power for France.

Years of working so closely with radioactive materials finally caught up with Joliot-Curie, and she was diagnosed with [leukemia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leukemia "Leukemia"). She had been accidentally exposed to [polonium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polonium "Polonium") when a sealed capsule of the element exploded on her laboratory bench in 1946. Treatment with antibiotics and a series of operations relieved her suffering temporarily, but her condition continued to deteriorate. Despite this, Joliot-Curie continued to work and in 1955 drew up plans for new physics laboratories at the [Orsay Faculty of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paris-Saclay_Faculty_of_Sciences "Paris-Saclay Faculty of Sciences"), which is now a part of the [Paris-Saclay University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paris-Saclay_University "Paris-Saclay University"), south of Paris.

### Political views

The Joliot-Curies had become increasingly aware of the growth of the fascist movement. They opposed its ideals and joined the [Socialist Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Section_of_the_Workers'_International "French Section of the Workers' International") in 1934, the *[Comité de vigilance des intellectuels antifascistes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comité_de_vigilance_des_intellectuels_antifascistes "Comité de vigilance des intellectuels antifascistes")* a year later, and in 1936 they actively supported the [Republican faction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republican_faction_(Spanish_Civil_War) "Republican faction (Spanish Civil War)") in the [Spanish Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_Civil_War "Spanish Civil War"). In the same year, Joliot-Curie was appointed Undersecretary of State for Scientific Research by the French government, in which capacity she helped in founding the *Centre National de la Recherche Scientifique*.

Frédéric and Irène visited Moscow for the two hundred and twentieth anniversary of the [Russian Academy of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Academy_of_Sciences "Russian Academy of Sciences") and returned sympathizing with Russian colleagues. Frédéric's close connection with the [Communist Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Communist_Party "French Communist Party") caused Irène to later be detained on [Ellis Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ellis_Island "Ellis Island") during her third trip to the US, coming to speak in support of Spanish refugees, at the [Joint Antifascist Refugee Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joint_Anti-Fascist_Refugee_Committee "Joint Anti-Fascist Refugee Committee")'s invitation.

The Joliot-Curies had continued Pierre and Marie's policy of publishing all of their work for the benefit of the global scientific community, but afraid of the danger that might result should it be developed for military use, they stopped: on 30 October 1939, they placed all of their documentation on nuclear fission in the vaults of the French Academy of Sciences, where it remained until 1949.

Joliot-Curie's political career continued after the war and she became a commissioner in the *Commissariat à l'énergie atomique*. However, she still found time for scientific work and in 1946 became director of her mother's *[Institut Curie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curie_Institute_(Paris) "Curie Institute (Paris)")*.

Joliot-Curie became actively involved in promoting women's education, serving on the National Committee of the Union of French Women (*Comité National de l'Union des Femmes Françaises*) and the [World Peace Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Peace_Council "World Peace Council"). The Joliot-Curies were given memberships to the French *[Légion d'honneur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Légion_d'honneur "Légion d'honneur")*; Irène as an officer and Frédéric as a commander, recognising his earlier work for the resistance.

### Personal life

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/39/Frederic_and_Irene_Joliot-Curie.jpg/330px-Frederic_and_Irene_Joliot-Curie.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Frederic_and_Irene_Joliot-Curie.jpg)

The Joliot-Curies in the 1940s

Irène and Frédéric hyphenated their surnames to Joliot-Curie after they married in 1926. The Joliot-Curies had two children, [Hélène](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hélène_Langevin-Joliot "Hélène Langevin-Joliot"), born eleven months after they were married, and [Pierre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Joliot "Pierre Joliot"), born in 1932.

Between 1941 and 1943, during [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II"), Joliot-Curie contracted [tuberculosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuberculosis "Tuberculosis") and was forced to spend time convalescing in Switzerland. Concern for her own health, together with the anguish of her husband's being in the resistance against the German troops and her children in occupied France, was hard to bear. She did make several dangerous visits back to France, enduring detention by German troops at the Swiss border on more than one occasion. Finally, in 1944, Joliot-Curie judged it too dangerous for her family to remain in France and she took her children back to Switzerland. Later in September 1944, after not hearing from Frédéric for months, Irene and her children were finally able to rejoin him.

Irène fought through these struggles to advocate for her own personal views. She was a passionate member of the feminist movement, especially regarding the sciences, and also advocated for peace. She continually applied to the [French Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Academy_of_Sciences "French Academy of Sciences"), an elite scientific organization, knowing that she would be denied. She did so to draw attention to the fact they did not accept women in the organization. Irène was also involved in many speaking functions, such as the International Women's Day conference. She also played a big role for the French contingent at the [World Congress of Intellectuals for Peace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Congress_of_Intellectuals_in_Defense_of_Peace "World Congress of Intellectuals in Defense of Peace"), which promoted the World Peace movement. In 1948, during a strike involving coal miners, Joliot-Curie reached out to Paris Newsletters to convince families to temporarily adopt the children of the coal miners during the strike. The Joliot-Curies adopted two girls during that time.

### Death

In 1956, after a final convalescent period in the French Alps, Joliot-Curie was admitted to the Curie Hospital in Paris, where she died on 17 March at the age of 58 from leukemia, possibly due to radiation from [polonium-210](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polonium-210 "Polonium-210"). Frédéric's health was also declining, and he died in 1958 from liver disease, which too was said to be the result of overexposure to radiation.

Joliot-Curie was an atheist and anti-war. When the French government held a national funeral in her honor, Irène's family asked to have the religious and military portions of the funeral omitted. Frédéric was also given a national funeral by the French government.

Joliot-Curie's daughter, [Hélène Langevin-Joliot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hélène_Langevin-Joliot "Hélène Langevin-Joliot"), went on to become a [nuclear physicist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_physicist "Nuclear physicist") and professor at the [University of Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Paris "University of Paris"). Joliot-Curie's son, [Pierre Joliot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Joliot "Pierre Joliot"), went on to become a [biochemist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biochemist "Biochemist") at the [Centre National de la Recherche Scientifique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centre_National_de_la_Recherche_Scientifique "Centre National de la Recherche Scientifique").

## Honours and awards

- Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1935 for the discovery of artificial radioactivity with Frédéric Joliot-Curie.
- Barnard Gold Medal for Meritorious Service to Science in 1940 with Frédéric Joliot-Curie.
- Officer of the Legion of Honor.
- The [Prix Joliot-Curie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prix_Joliot-Curie "Prix Joliot-Curie") was named in honour of Joliot-Curie by the [Société Française de Physique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Société_Française_de_Physique "Société Française de Physique") in 1956.

Her name was added to the *[Monument to the X-ray and Radium Martyrs of All Nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monument_to_the_X-ray_and_Radium_Martyrs_of_All_Nations "Monument to the X-ray and Radium Martyrs of All Nations")* erected in [Hamburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamburg "Hamburg"), Germany.

In 2026, Joliot-Curie and her mother were announced as among the 72 historical women in STEM whose names have been proposed to be added to the [72 men](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_the_72_names_on_the_Eiffel_Tower "List of the 72 names on the Eiffel Tower") already celebrated on the [Eiffel Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eiffel_Tower "Eiffel Tower"). Her mother was also on the list. The plan was announced by the Mayor of Paris, [Anne Hidalgo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anne_Hidalgo "Anne Hidalgo") following the recommendations of a committee led by [Isabelle Vauglin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isabelle_Vauglin "Isabelle Vauglin") of [Femmes et Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Femmes_et_Sciences "Femmes et Sciences") and Jean-François Martins, representing the operating company which runs the Eiffel Tower.
