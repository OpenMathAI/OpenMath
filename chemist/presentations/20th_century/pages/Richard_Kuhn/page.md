**Richard Johann Kuhn** (German pronunciation: [[ˈʁɪçaʁt ˈkuːn]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Standard_German "Help:IPA/Standard German") [ⓘ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:De-Richard_Kuhn.ogg "File:De-Richard Kuhn.ogg"); 3 December 1900 – 31 July 1967) was an Austrian-German [biochemist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biochemist "Biochemist") who was awarded the [Nobel Prize in Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Chemistry "Nobel Prize in Chemistry") in 1938 "for his work on [carotenoids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carotenoid "Carotenoid") and [vitamins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vitamin "Vitamin")".

## Biography

### Early life

Kuhn was born in [Vienna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vienna "Vienna"), [Austria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austria "Austria"), where he attended grammar school and high school. His interest in chemistry surfaced early; however he had many interests and decided late to study chemistry. Between 1910 and 1918 he was a schoolmate of [Wolfgang Pauli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolfgang_Pauli "Wolfgang Pauli"), who was awarded the [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics") for 1945. Beginning in 1918, Kuhn attended lectures at the [University of Vienna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Vienna "University of Vienna") in chemistry. He finished his chemistry studies at the [Ludwig-Maximilians-Universität München](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludwig-Maximilians-Universität_München "Ludwig-Maximilians-Universität München") (LMU Munich) and received his doctoral degree in 1922 with [Richard Willstätter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Willstätter "Richard Willstätter") for a scientific work on enzymes.

After graduating, Kuhn continued his scientific career, first in Munich, then at the [ETH Zurich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ETH_Zurich "ETH Zurich") and from 1929 onwards at the [University of Heidelberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Heidelberg "University of Heidelberg"), where he was head of the chemistry department beginning in 1937. In 1928 he married Daisy Hartmann and the couple subsequently had two sons and four daughters.

### Research

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/02/Rkuhn_grab.JPG/250px-Rkuhn_grab.JPG?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Rkuhn_grab.JPG)

Kuhn's grave in Heidelberg

Kuhn's areas of study included: investigations of theoretical problems of [organic chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_chemistry "Organic chemistry") ([stereochemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stereochemistry "Stereochemistry") of [aliphatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aliphatic_compound "Aliphatic compound") and [aromatic compounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aromaticity "Aromaticity"); syntheses of [polyenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyene "Polyene") and [cumulenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cumulene "Cumulene"); constitution and colour; the acidity of [hydrocarbons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrocarbon "Hydrocarbon")), as well as extensive fields in [biochemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biochemistry "Biochemistry") ([carotenoids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carotenoids "Carotenoids"); [flavins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flavins "Flavins"); [vitamins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vitamins "Vitamins") and [enzymes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enzymes "Enzymes")). Specifically, he carried out important work on vitamin B2 and the antidermatitis vitamin B6.

In 1929 he became Principal of the Institute for Chemistry at the newly founded [Kaiser Wilhelm Institute for Medical Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaiser_Wilhelm_Institute "Kaiser Wilhelm Institute") (which, since 1950, has been renamed the [Max Planck Institute for Medical Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Planck_Institute_for_Medical_Research "Max Planck Institute for Medical Research") in [Heidelberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heidelberg "Heidelberg")). By 1937 he also took over the administration of this Institute.

In addition to these duties he also served as of Professor of Biochemistry at the [University of Heidelberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Heidelberg "University of Heidelberg"), and for one year he was at the [University of Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Pennsylvania "University of Pennsylvania"), [Philadelphia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philadelphia "Philadelphia"), as a Visiting Research Professor for [Physiological Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biochemistry "Biochemistry").

He was subsequently awarded the [Nobel Prize in Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Chemistry "Nobel Prize in Chemistry") in 1938 for his "work on carotenoids and vitamins," but rejected the prize as [Hitler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adolf_Hitler "Adolf Hitler") had forbidden German citizens to accept it. In a handwritten letter, he even described the awarding of the prize to a German as an invitation to violate a decree of the Führer. He received the award after [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II"). Kuhn is also credited with the discovery of the deadly [nerve agent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nerve_agent "Nerve agent") [Soman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soman "Soman") in 1944.

Kuhn was editor of *[Justus Liebigs Annalen der Chemie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liebigs_Annalen "Liebigs Annalen")* from 1948.

Kuhn died in 1967 in [Heidelberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heidelberg "Heidelberg"), Germany, aged 66.

## Nazi era

Kuhn collaborated with high-ranking [Nazi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi "Nazi") officials and denounced three of his Jewish co-workers in 1936.

In 2005, the Society of German Chemists (Gesellschaft Deutscher Chemiker, GDCh) declared their intention to no longer award the Richard Kuhn Medal: "The board of the GDCh intends to discontinue awarding the Medal named after the organic chemist, Nobel Prize laureate of the year 1938 and President of the GDCh in 1964–65, Richard Kuhn. The board thereby draws the consequences out of research on Richard Kuhn's behaviour during National Socialism. Even though the question of whether Kuhn was a convinced National Socialist or just a career-oriented camp follower is not fully answered, he undisputably supported the Nazi-regime in administrative and organizational ways, especially by his scientific work. Despite his scientific achievements, Kuhn is not suitable to serve as a role model, and eponym for an important award, mainly due to his unreflected research on poison gas, but also due to his conduct towards Jewish colleagues."
