**Walter Kohn** (German pronunciation: [[ˈvaltɐ ˈkoːn]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Standard_German "Help:IPA/Standard German"); March 9, 1923 – April 19, 2016) was an Austrian-American [theoretical physicist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theoretical_physics "Theoretical physics") and [theoretical chemist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theoretical_chemistry "Theoretical chemistry").
He was awarded, with [John Pople](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Pople "John Pople"), the [Nobel Prize in Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Chemistry "Nobel Prize in Chemistry") in 1998. The award recognized their contributions to understanding the electronic properties of materials. In particular, Kohn played the leading role in the development of [density functional theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density_functional_theory "Density functional theory"), which made it possible to calculate [quantum mechanical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_mechanical "Quantum mechanical") electronic structure by equations involving the [electronic density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_density "Electronic density") (rather than the many-body [wavefunction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavefunction "Wavefunction")). This computational simplification led to more accurate calculations on complex systems as well as many new insights, and it has become an essential tool for materials science, condensed-phase physics, and the chemical physics of atoms and molecules.

## Early years in Canada

Kohn arrived in [England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/England "England") as part of the [Kindertransport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kindertransport "Kindertransport") rescue operation immediately after the [annexation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anschluss "Anschluss") of [Austria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austria "Austria") by [Hitler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adolf_Hitler "Adolf Hitler"). He was from a [Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish "Jewish") family, and has written, "My feelings towards Austria, my native land, are – and will remain – very painful. They are dominated by my vivid recollections of 1 1/2 years as a Jewish boy under the [Nazi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi "Nazi") regime, and by the subsequent murder of my parents, Salomon and Gittel Kohn, of other relatives and several teachers, during the [Holocaust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holocaust "Holocaust"). ... I want to mention that I have a strong Jewish identity and – over the years – have been involved in several Jewish projects, such as the establishment of a strong program of Judaic Studies at the University of California, San Diego.". Kohn also has identified as [Deist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deist "Deist").

Because he was an Austrian national, he was transferred to [Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canada "Canada") in July 1940 after the outbreak of [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II"). As a 17-year-old, Kohn traveled as part of a British convoy moving through U-boat-infested waters to [Quebec City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quebec_City "Quebec City") in Canada; and from there, by train, to a camp in [Trois-Rivières](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trois-Rivières "Trois-Rivières"). He was at first held in detention in a camp near [Sherbrooke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sherbrooke "Sherbrooke"), [Quebec](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quebec "Quebec"). This camp, as well as others, provided a small number of educational facilities that Kohn used to the fullest, and he finally succeeded in entering the University of Toronto. As a German national, the future Nobel Laureate in Chemistry was not allowed to enter the chemistry building, so he opted for physics and mathematics.

## Scientific career

Kohn received a war-time bachelor's degree in applied mathematics at the end of his one-year army service, having completed only 2½ out of the 4-year undergraduate program, from the [University of Toronto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Toronto "University of Toronto") in 1945; he was awarded an M.A. degree in applied mathematics by Toronto in 1946. Kohn was awarded a Ph.D. degree in physics by [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University "Harvard University") in 1948, where he worked under [Julian Schwinger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julian_Schwinger "Julian Schwinger") on the three-body scattering problem. At Harvard, he also fell under the influence of [J. H. Van Vleck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Hasbrouck_Van_Vleck "John Hasbrouck Van Vleck") and developed an interest in solid-state physics.

He moved from Harvard to [Carnegie Mellon University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carnegie_Mellon_University "Carnegie Mellon University") from 1950 to 1960, after a short stint in Copenhagen as a [National Research Council of Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Research_Council_of_Canada "National Research Council of Canada") post-doctoral fellow. At Carnegie Mellon, he did much of his seminal work on multiple-scattering band-structure work, now known as the KKR method. His association with [Bell Labs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell_Labs "Bell Labs") got him involved with semiconductor physics and produced a long and fruitful collaboration with [Luttinger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joaquin_Mazdak_Luttinger "Joaquin Mazdak Luttinger") (including, for example, development of the [Luttinger-Kohn model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luttinger-Kohn_model "Luttinger-Kohn model") of semiconductor [band structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Band_structure "Band structure")). In 1960 he moved to the newly founded [University of California, San Diego](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_San_Diego "University of California, San Diego"), held a term as the physics department chair, and remained until 1979. It was during this period, he, along with his student [Chanchal Kumar Majumdar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chanchal_Kumar_Majumdar "Chanchal Kumar Majumdar") developed the *Kohn–Majumdar theorem* related to [Fermi gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermi_gas "Fermi gas") and its bound and unbound states. He then accepted the founding director's position at the new [Institute for Theoretical Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_for_Theoretical_Physics_(Santa_Barbara) "Institute for Theoretical Physics (Santa Barbara)") in Santa Barbara. He took his position as a professor in the [Physics Department](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UC_Santa_Barbara_Physics_Department "UC Santa Barbara Physics Department") at the [University of California, Santa Barbara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Santa_Barbara "University of California, Santa Barbara") in 1984; where he worked until the end of his life.

Kohn made significant contributions to semiconductor physics, which led to his award of the [Oliver E. Buckley Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buckley_Prize "Buckley Prize") by the [American Physical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Physical_Society "American Physical Society"). He was also awarded the Feenburg medal for his contributions to the [many-body problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Many-body_problem "Many-body problem").
His work on density functional theory was initiated during a visit to the *[École Normale Supérieure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/École_Normale_Supérieure "École Normale Supérieure")* in Paris, with [Pierre Hohenberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Hohenberg "Pierre Hohenberg"), and was prompted by a consideration of alloy theory. The
[Hohenberg–Kohn theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hohenberg–Kohn_theorem "Hohenberg–Kohn theorem") was further developed, in collaboration with [Lu Jeu Sham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lu_Jeu_Sham "Lu Jeu Sham"), to produce the [Kohn-Sham equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kohn–Sham_equations "Kohn–Sham equations"). The latter is the standard workhorse of modern materials science, and even used in quantum theories of plasmas.
In 2004, a study of all citations to the *Physical Review*  journals from 1893 until 2003, found Kohn to be an author of five of the 100 papers with the "highest citation impact", including the first two.

Walter Kohn receiving an [honorary doctorate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honorary_doctorate "Honorary doctorate") at [The University of Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_University_of_Oxford "The University of Oxford")

In 1957, he relinquished his Canadian citizenship and became a [naturalized citizen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naturalized_citizen "Naturalized citizen") of the [United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States "United States").

In 1963 Kohn became a Member of the [American Academy of Arts and Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Academy_of_Arts_and_Sciences "American Academy of Arts and Sciences"), a Member of the [National Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Academy_of_Sciences "National Academy of Sciences") in 1969, and a member of the [American Philosophical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Philosophical_Society "American Philosophical Society") in 1994. In 2011, he became an honorary member of the [Austrian Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austrian_Academy_of_Sciences "Austrian Academy of Sciences") (ÖAW). He was also a Member of the [International Academy of Quantum Molecular Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Academy_of_Quantum_Molecular_Science "International Academy of Quantum Molecular Science").

## Death

Kohn died on April 19, 2016, at his home in [Santa Barbara, California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santa_Barbara,_California "Santa Barbara, California") from jaw cancer, at the age of 93.
