**Robert Floyd Curl Jr.** (August 23, 1933 – July 3, 2022) was an American chemist who was Pitzer–Schlumberger Professor of Natural Sciences and professor of chemistry at [Rice University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rice_University "Rice University"). He was awarded the [Nobel Prize in Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Chemistry "Nobel Prize in Chemistry") in 1996 for the discovery of the [nanomaterial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanomaterials "Nanomaterials") [buckminsterfullerene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buckminsterfullerene "Buckminsterfullerene"), and hence the [fullerene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fullerene "Fullerene") class of materials, along with [Richard Smalley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Smalley "Richard Smalley") (also of Rice University) and [Harold Kroto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Kroto "Harold Kroto") of the [University of Sussex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Sussex "University of Sussex").

## Early life and education

Born in [Alice, Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alice,_Texas "Alice, Texas"), United States, Curl was the son of a [Methodist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methodist "Methodist") [minister](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minister_(Christianity) "Minister (Christianity)"). Due to his father's missionary work, his family moved several times within southern and southwestern Texas, and the elder Curl was involved in starting the San Antonio Medical Center's Methodist Hospital. Curl attributes his interest in chemistry to a [chemistry set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemistry_set "Chemistry set") he received as a nine-year-old, recalling that he ruined the finish on his mother's porcelain stove when [nitric acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitric_acid "Nitric acid") boiled over onto it. He is a graduate of [Thomas Jefferson High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Jefferson_High_School_(San_Antonio,_TX) "Thomas Jefferson High School (San Antonio, TX)") in [San Antonio, Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Antonio,_Texas "San Antonio, Texas"). His high school offered only one year of chemistry instruction, but in his senior year his chemistry teacher gave him special projects to work on.

Curl received a [Bachelor of Arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_Science "Bachelor of Science") in chemistry from [Rice Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rice_University "Rice University") (now Rice University) in 1954. He was attracted to the reputation of both the school's academics and football team, and the fact that at the time it charged no tuition. He earned his [doctorate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctorate "Doctorate") in chemistry from the [University of California, Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Berkeley "University of California, Berkeley"), in 1957. At Berkeley, he worked in the laboratory of [Kenneth Pitzer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenneth_Pitzer "Kenneth Pitzer"), then dean of the college of chemistry, with whom he would become a lifelong collaborator. Curl's graduate research involved performing [infrared spectroscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infrared_spectroscopy "Infrared spectroscopy") to determine the [bond angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bond_angle "Bond angle") of [disiloxane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disiloxane "Disiloxane").

## Scientific career

Curl was a postdoctoral fellow at [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University "Harvard University") with [E. B. Wilson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edgar_Bright_Wilson "Edgar Bright Wilson"), where he used [microwave spectroscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microwave_spectroscopy "Microwave spectroscopy") to study the [bond rotation barriers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bond_rotation_barrier "Bond rotation barrier") of molecules. After that, he joined the faculty of Rice University in 1958. He inherited the equipment and graduate students of [George Bird](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Bird_(scientist)?action=edit&redlink=1 "George Bird (scientist)"), a professor who was leaving for a job at [Polaroid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polaroid_Corporation "Polaroid Corporation"). Curl's early research involved the microwave spectroscopy of [chlorine dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chlorine_dioxide "Chlorine dioxide"). His research program included both experiment and theory, mainly focused on detection and analysis of [free radicals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_radical "Free radical") using microwave spectroscopy and tunable lasers. He used these observations to develop the theory of their [fine structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fine_structure "Fine structure") and [hyperfine structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperfine_structure "Hyperfine structure"), as well as information about their structure and the kinetics of their reactions.

## Nobel Prize

Curl's research at Rice involved the fields of infrared and microwave spectroscopy. Curl's research inspired Richard Smalley to come to Rice in 1976 with the intention of collaborating with Curl. In 1985, Curl was contacted by Harold Kroto, who wanted to use a laser beam apparatus built by Smalley to simulate and study the formation of [carbon chains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyyne "Polyyne") in [red giant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_giant "Red giant") stars. Smalley and Curl had previously used this apparatus to study [semiconductors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiconductor "Semiconductor") such as [silicon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon "Silicon") and [germanium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germanium "Germanium"). They were initially reluctant to interrupt their experiments on these semiconductor materials to use their apparatus for Kroto's experiments on carbon, but eventually gave in.

They indeed found the long carbon chains they were looking for, but also found an unexpected product that had 60 carbon atoms. Over the course of 11 days, the team studied and determined its structure and named it [buckminsterfullerene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buckminsterfullerene "Buckminsterfullerene") after noting its similarity to the [geodesic domes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geodesic_dome "Geodesic dome") for which the architect [Buckminster Fuller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buckminster_Fuller "Buckminster Fuller") was known. This discovery was based solely on the single prominent peak on the [mass spectrograph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_spectroscopy "Mass spectroscopy"), implying a chemically inert substance that was geometrically closed with no [dangling bonds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dangling_bond "Dangling bond"). Curl was responsible for determining the optimal conditions of the carbon vapor in the apparatus, and examining the spectrograph. Curl noted that [James R. Heath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_R._Heath "James R. Heath") and [Sean C. O'Brien](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sean_C._O'Brien?action=edit&redlink=1 "Sean C. O'Brien") deserve equal recognition in the work to Smalley and Kroto. The existence of this type of molecule had earlier been theorized by others, but Curl and his colleagues were at the time unaware of this. Later experiments confirmed their proposed structure, and the team moved on to synthesize [endohedral fullerenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endohedral_fullerene "Endohedral fullerene") that had a metal atom inside the hollow carbon shell. The [fullerenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fullerene "Fullerene"), a class of molecules of which buckminsterfullerene was the first member discovered, are now considered to have potential applications in [nanomaterials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanomaterials "Nanomaterials") and [molecular scale electronics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_scale_electronics "Molecular scale electronics"). Robert Curl's 1985 paper entitled "C60: Buckminsterfullerine", published with colleagues H. Kroto, J. R. Heath, S. C. O’Brien, and R. E. Smalley, was honored by a Citation for Chemical Breakthrough Award from the Division of History of Chemistry of the American Chemical Society, presented to Rice University in 2015. The discovery of fullerenes was recognized in 2010 by the designation of a [National Historic Chemical Landmark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Historic_Chemical_Landmarks "National Historic Chemical Landmarks") by the [American Chemical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Chemical_Society "American Chemical Society") at the Richard E. Smalley Institute for Nanoscale Science and Technology at Rice University in Houston, Texas.

After winning the Nobel Prize in 1996, Curl took a quieter path than Smalley, who became an outspoken advocate of nanotechnology, and Kroto, who used his fame to further his interest in science education, saying, "After winning a Nobel, you can either become a scientific pontificator, or you can have some idea for a new science project and you can use your newfound notoriety to get the resources to do it. Or you can say, 'Well, I enjoy what I was doing, and I want to keep doing that.'" True to that humility, when asked by the President of Rice what he would like, following the Nobel announcement, he asked that a [bike rack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bicycle_parking_rack "Bicycle parking rack") be installed closer to his office and laboratory.

## Later research

Curl's later research interests involved [physical chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_chemistry "Physical chemistry"), developing [DNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA "DNA") genotyping and sequencing instrumentation, and creating [photoacoustic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photoacoustic_spectroscopy "Photoacoustic spectroscopy") sensors for [trace gases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trace_gas "Trace gas") using [quantum cascade lasers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_cascade_laser "Quantum cascade laser"). He is known in the residential college life at Rice University for being the first master of [Lovett College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lovett_College "Lovett College").

Curl retired in 2008 at the age of 74, becoming a University [Professor Emeritus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emeritus_professor "Emeritus professor"), Pitzer-Schlumberger Professor of Natural Sciences Emeritus, and Professor of Chemistry Emeritus at Rice University.

## Personal life

Curl married Jonel Whipple in 1955, with whom he had two children. He [cycled](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bicycle_commuting "Bicycle commuting") to his office and lab and every week played bridge with the Rice Bridge Brigade. Curl died in [Houston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Houston "Houston") on July 3, 2022, at the age of 88.
