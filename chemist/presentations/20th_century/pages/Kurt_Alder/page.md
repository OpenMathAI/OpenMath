**Kurt Alder** (German pronunciation: [[ˈkʊʁt ˈaldɐ]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Standard_German "Help:IPA/Standard German") [ⓘ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:De-Kurt_Alder.ogg "File:De-Kurt Alder.ogg"); 10 July 1902 – 20 June 1958) was a German [chemist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemist "Chemist") and [Nobel laureate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_laureate "Nobel laureate").

## Biography

Alder was born, as a teacher's son, in the industrial area of [Königshütte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Königshütte_(Silesia) "Königshütte (Silesia)"), [Silesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silesia_Province "Silesia Province") (modern day [Chorzów](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chorzów "Chorzów"), [Upper Silesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_Silesia "Upper Silesia"), [Poland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poland "Poland")), where he received his early schooling. Alder left the area when Königshütte became part of Poland in 1922. He studied chemistry at the [University of Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Berlin "University of Berlin") from 1922, and later at the [University of Kiel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Kiel "University of Kiel") where his [PhD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Philosophy "Doctor of Philosophy") was awarded in 1926 for work supervised by [Otto Paul Hermann Diels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otto_Diels "Otto Diels").

In 1930 Alder was appointed reader for chemistry at [Kiel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kiel "Kiel"), and promoted to lecturer in 1934. In 1936 he left Kiel to join [I G Farben Industrie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IG_Farben "IG Farben") at [Leverkusen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leverkusen "Leverkusen"), where he worked on [synthetic rubber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synthetic_rubber "Synthetic rubber"). Then in 1940 he was appointed Professor of Experimental Chemistry and Chemical Technology at the [University of Cologne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Cologne "University of Cologne") and Director of the Institute of Chemistry there. Throughout this time and despite the many obstacles to original research in Europe at the time, he continued a systematic program of investigations of his particular interests in the [synthesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_synthesis "Organic synthesis") of [organic compounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_compound "Organic compound"). In all he published more than 151 papers in this field.

In 1945 he worked closely with the inventor of [EDTA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EDTA "EDTA"), [Ferdinand Münz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferdinand_Münz "Ferdinand Münz"). In 1949 they published a paper together on [diene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diene "Diene") synthesis and additions

Alder received several honorary degrees and other awards, such as the 1950 Nobel Prize in Chemistry which he shared with his teacher Diels for their work on the [Diels–Alder reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diels–Alder_reaction "Diels–Alder reaction"). The [lunar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moon "Moon") [crater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impact_crater "Impact crater") [Alder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alder_(crater) "Alder (crater)") is named in his honour. The insecticide [aldrin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aldrin "Aldrin"), created through a Diels–Alder reaction, is also named after the scientist.

Alder died in June 1958, aged 55. The cause of his death is unknown, however his body was found in his apartment in [Cologne, Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cologne,_Germany "Cologne, Germany") after two weeks. Just before his death, he had written in a letter to the Standing Committee for the Meetings of Nobel Laureates: "The relentless and ever-increasing demands placed on active German university professors by constantly new tasks have, in my case, after years of depleting my strength, led to exhaustion, prompting urgent medical advice that I should, for the time being, take complete rest."
