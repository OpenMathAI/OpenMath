**George Charles de Hevesy** (born **György Bischitz**; [Hungarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungarian_language "Hungarian language"): *Hevesy György Károly*; [German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_language "German language"): *Georg Karl von Hevesy*; 1 August 1885 – 5 July 1966) was a Hungarian [radiochemist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiochemistry "Radiochemistry") and [Nobel Prize in Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Chemistry "Nobel Prize in Chemistry") laureate, recognized in 1943 for his key role in the development of [radioactive tracers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radioactive_tracer "Radioactive tracer") to study chemical processes such as in the [metabolism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metabolism "Metabolism") of animals. He also co-discovered the element [hafnium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hafnium "Hafnium").

## Biography

### Early years

Hevesy György was born in [Budapest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Budapest "Budapest"), Hungary, to a wealthy and ennobled family of [Hungarian-Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungarian-Jewish "Hungarian-Jewish") descent, the fifth of eight children to his parents Lajos Bischitz and Baroness Eugénia (Jenny) Schossberger (ennobled as "De Tornya"). Grandparents from both sides of the family had provided the presidents of the Jewish community of [Pest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pest,_Hungary "Pest, Hungary"). George grew up in Budapest and graduated high school in 1903 from [Piarist Gimnázium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piarists "Piarists"). The family's name in 1904 was Hevesy-Bischitz, and Hevesy later changed his own.

De Hevesy began his studies in chemistry at the [University of Budapest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Budapest "University of Budapest") for one year, and at the [Technische Hochschule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technische_Hochschule "Technische Hochschule") in Charlottenburg (now [Technische Universität Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technische_Universität_Berlin "Technische Universität Berlin")) for several months, but transferred to the [University of Freiburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Ludwigs_University_of_Freiburg "Albert Ludwigs University of Freiburg"). There he met [Ludwig Gattermann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludwig_Gattermann "Ludwig Gattermann"). In 1906, he started his Ph.D. thesis with Georg Franz Julius Meyer, acquiring his doctorate in physics in 1908. In 1908, Hevesy was offered a position at the [ETH Zürich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ETH_Zürich "ETH Zürich"), Switzerland, yet being independently wealthy, he was able to choose his research environment. He worked first with [Fritz Haber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fritz_Haber "Fritz Haber") in [Karlsruhe, Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karlsruhe,_Germany "Karlsruhe, Germany"), then with [Ernest Rutherford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_Rutherford "Ernest Rutherford") in [Manchester, England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manchester,_England "Manchester, England"), where he also met [Niels Bohr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niels_Bohr "Niels Bohr"). Back at home in [Budapest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Budapest "Budapest"), he was appointed professor in [physical chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_chemistry "Physical chemistry") in 1918. In 1920, he settled in Copenhagen.

### Research

In 1922, de Hevesy co-discovered (with [Dirk Coster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirk_Coster "Dirk Coster")) the element [hafnium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hafnium "Hafnium") (72Hf) ([Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin "Latin") *Hafnia* for "[Copenhagen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copenhagen "Copenhagen")", the home town of [Niels Bohr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niels_Bohr "Niels Bohr")). [Mendeleev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dmitri_Mendeleev "Dmitri Mendeleev")'s 1869 [periodic table](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periodic_table "Periodic table") arranged the chemical elements into a logical system, but a chemical element with 72 protons was missing. Hevesy determined to look for that element on the basis of Bohr's atomic model. The mineralogical museum of Norway and Greenland in Copenhagen furnished the material for the research. Characteristic [X-ray spectra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray_spectrum "X-ray spectrum") recordings made of the sample indicated that a new element was present. The accepted account has been disputed by Mansel Davies and [Eric Scerri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eric_Scerri "Eric Scerri") who attribute the prediction that element 72 would be a [transition element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transition_element "Transition element") to the chemist Charles Bury.

Supported financially by the [Rockefeller Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rockefeller_Foundation "Rockefeller Foundation"), Hevesy had a very productive year. He developed the [X-ray fluorescence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray_fluorescence "X-ray fluorescence") analytical method, and discovered the [samarium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samarium "Samarium") alpha-ray. It was here he began the use of [radioactive isotopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radioactive_isotope "Radioactive isotope") in studying the metabolic processes of plants and animals, by tracing chemicals in the body by replacing part of stable isotopes with small quantities of the radioactive isotopes. In 1923, Hevesy published the first study on the use of the naturally radioactive 212Pb as radioactive tracer to follow the absorption and [translocation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Translocation_(botany) "Translocation (botany)") in the roots, stems and leaves of *[Vicia faba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vicia_faba "Vicia faba")*, also known as the broad bean. Later, in 1943, the work on radioactive tracing would earn Hevesy the [Nobel Prize in Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Chemistry "Nobel Prize in Chemistry").

In 1924, Hevesy returned to Freiburg as Professor of Physical Chemistry. In 1930, he went to [Cornell University, Ithaca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornell_University,_Ithaca "Cornell University, Ithaca") as Baker Lecturer. In 1934, after the Nazis came to power in Germany, he returned to Niels Bohr's Institute at the University of Copenhagen. In 1936, he invented [Neutron Activation Analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron_activation_analysis "Neutron activation analysis"). In 1943 he fled to Stockholm (Sweden being neutral during the war), where was he an associate of the Institute of Research in Organic Chemistry. In 1949 he was elected Franqui Professor in the [University of Ghent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Ghent "University of Ghent"). In his retirement, he remained an active scientific associate of the [Stockholm University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stockholm_University "Stockholm University").

## World War II and beyond

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/6f/Georg_und_Pia_von_Hevesy_Stolpersteine.jpg/250px-Georg_und_Pia_von_Hevesy_Stolpersteine.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Georg_und_Pia_von_Hevesy_Stolpersteine.jpg)

[Stolpersteine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stolperstein "Stolperstein") memorials for Georg and his wife Pia de Hevesy in Freiburg

Prior to the onset of World War II, [Max von Laue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_von_Laue "Max von Laue") and [James Franck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Franck "James Franck") had sent their gold [Nobel Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize "Nobel Prize") medals to [Denmark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denmark "Denmark") to keep them from being confiscated by the Nazis. After the [Nazi invasion of Denmark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denmark_in_World_War_II "Denmark in World War II") this placed them in danger; it was illegal at the time to send gold out of Germany, and were it discovered that Laue and Franck had done so, they could have faced prosecution. To prevent this, de Hevesy concealed the medals by dissolving them in [aqua regia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aqua_regia "Aqua regia") and placing the resulting solution on a shelf in his laboratory at the [Niels Bohr Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niels_Bohr_Institute "Niels Bohr Institute") in Copenhagen. After the war, he returned to find the solution undisturbed and precipitated the gold out of the acid. The Nobel Society then recast the medals using the recovered gold and returned them to the two laureates.

By 1943, Copenhagen was no longer safe for a Jewish scientist and de Hevesy fled to Sweden, where he worked at the [Stockholm University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stockholm_University "Stockholm University") until 1961. In Stockholm, de Hevesy was received at the department of chemistry by the German-born Swedish professor and Nobel Prize winner [Hans von Euler-Chelpin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_von_Euler-Chelpin "Hans von Euler-Chelpin"), who remained strongly pro-Germany throughout the war. Despite this, de Hevesy and von Euler-Chelpin collaborated on many scientific papers during and after the war.

While in Stockholm, de Hevesy received the Nobel Prize in chemistry. He was later inducted into the [Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Swedish_Academy_of_Sciences "Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences") and received the [Copley Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copley_Medal "Copley Medal"), of which he was particularly proud. De Hevesy stated: "The public thinks the Nobel Prize in chemistry for the highest honor that a scientist can receive, but it is not so. Forty or fifty have received Nobel chemistry prizes, but there are only ten foreign members of the Royal Swedish Academy, and only two have received a Copley." (Bohr was the other one.) He received the [Atoms for Peace Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atoms_for_Peace_Award "Atoms for Peace Award") in 1958 for his peaceful use of [radioactive isotopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radioactive_isotope "Radioactive isotope").

## Family life and death

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b5/Hevesy_Gy%C3%B6rgy_s%C3%ADrja.jpg/250px-Hevesy_Gy%C3%B6rgy_s%C3%ADrja.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Hevesy_György_sírja.jpg)

George de Hevesy's grave in Budapest. Cemetery Kerepesi: 27 Hungarian Academy of Sciences.

De Hevesy married Pia Riis in 1924. They had one son and three daughters together, one of whom (Eugenie) married a grandson of the Swedish Nobel laureate [Svante Arrhenius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Svante_Arrhenius "Svante Arrhenius"). De Hevesy died in 1966 at the age of eighty and was buried in Freiburg. At his family's request, his remains were interred at his birthplace in Budapest at the [Kerepesi Cemetery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerepesi_Cemetery "Kerepesi Cemetery") in 2001. He had published a total of 397 scientific documents, one of which was the Becquerel-Curie Memorial Lecture, in which he had reminisced about the careers of pioneers of radiochemistry.

On 10 May 2005 the Hevesy Laboratory was founded at [Risø National Laboratory for Sustainable Energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Risø_DTU_National_Laboratory_for_Sustainable_Energy "Risø DTU National Laboratory for Sustainable Energy"), now [Technical University of Denmark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technical_University_of_Denmark "Technical University of Denmark"), DTU Nutech. It was named after George de Hevesy as the father of the isotope tracer principle under the initiative of the lab's first director, Prof. Mikael Jensen.
