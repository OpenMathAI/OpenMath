**Harold Clayton Urey** [ForMemRS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_Member_of_the_Royal_Society "Foreign Member of the Royal Society") ([/ˈjʊəri/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English") [*YOOR-ee*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:Pronunciation_respelling_key "Help:Pronunciation respelling key"); April 29, 1893 – January 5, 1981) was an American [physical chemist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_chemist "Physical chemist") who conducted pioneering work on [isotopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isotope "Isotope"). He earned the 1934 [Nobel Prize in Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Chemistry "Nobel Prize in Chemistry") "for his discovery of  [heavy hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deuterium "Deuterium")." He played a significant role in the development of the [atom bomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_weapon "Nuclear weapon"), as well as contributing to theories on the [development of organic life from non-living matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miller–Urey_experiment "Miller–Urey experiment").

Born in [Walkerton, Indiana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walkerton,_Indiana "Walkerton, Indiana"), Urey studied [thermodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermodynamics "Thermodynamics") under [Gilbert N. Lewis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gilbert_N._Lewis "Gilbert N. Lewis") at the [University of California, Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Berkeley "University of California, Berkeley"). After he received his [PhD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PhD "PhD") in 1923, he was awarded a fellowship by [the American-Scandinavian Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_American-Scandinavian_Foundation "The American-Scandinavian Foundation") to study at the [Niels Bohr Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niels_Bohr_Institute "Niels Bohr Institute") in [Copenhagen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copenhagen "Copenhagen"). He was a research associate at [Johns Hopkins University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johns_Hopkins_University "Johns Hopkins University") from 1924 to 1929, before becoming an associate professor of chemistry at [Columbia University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia_University "Columbia University"). In 1931, he began work with the separation of isotopes that resulted in the discovery of deuterium.

During World War II, Urey turned his knowledge of isotope separation to the problem of [uranium enrichment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium_enrichment "Uranium enrichment"). He headed the group located at Columbia University that developed isotope separation using [gaseous diffusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaseous_diffusion "Gaseous diffusion"). The method was successfully developed, becoming the sole method used in the early post-war period. After the war, Urey became professor of chemistry at the [Institute for Nuclear Studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_for_Nuclear_Studies "Institute for Nuclear Studies"), and later Ryerson professor of chemistry at the [University of Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Chicago "University of Chicago").

Urey speculated the early terrestrial [atmosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_atmosphere "Earth's atmosphere") was composed of [ammonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ammonia "Ammonia"), [methane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methane "Methane"), and hydrogen. One of his Chicago graduate students was [Stanley L. Miller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanley_L._Miller "Stanley L. Miller"), who showed in the [Miller–Urey experiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miller–Urey_experiment "Miller–Urey experiment") that if such a mixture were exposed to electric sparks and water, it can interact to produce [amino acids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amino_acids "Amino acids"), commonly considered the building blocks of life. Work with [isotopes of oxygen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isotopes_of_oxygen "Isotopes of oxygen") led to pioneering the new field of [paleoclimatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paleoclimatology "Paleoclimatology") research. In 1958, he accepted a post as a professor at large at the new [University of California, San Diego](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_San_Diego "University of California, San Diego") (UCSD), where he helped create the science faculty. He was one of the founding members of UCSD's school of chemistry created in 1960. He became increasingly interested in space science, and when [Apollo 11](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_11 "Apollo 11") returned [Moon rock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moon_rock "Moon rock") samples from the Moon, Urey examined them at the [Lunar Receiving Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_Receiving_Laboratory "Lunar Receiving Laboratory"). Lunar astronaut [Harrison Schmitt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harrison_Schmitt "Harrison Schmitt") said Urey approached him as a volunteer for a one-way mission to the Moon, stating "I will go, and I don't care if I don't come back."

## Early life

Harold Clayton Urey was born on April 29, 1893, in [Walkerton, Indiana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walkerton,_Indiana "Walkerton, Indiana"), the son of Samuel Clayton Urey, a school teacher and a minister in the [Church of the Brethren](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_the_Brethren "Church of the Brethren"), and his wife, Cora Rebecca née Reinoehl. Of mostly German ancestry, the family name had English origins. He had a younger brother, Clarence, and a younger sister, Martha. The family moved to [Glendora, California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glendora,_California "Glendora, California") after Samuel became seriously ill with [tuberculosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuberculosis "Tuberculosis"), hoping the climate would improve his health. When it became clear he would die, the family moved back to Indiana to live with Cora's widowed mother. Samuel died when Harold was six years old.

Urey was educated in an [Amish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amish "Amish") grade school, from which he graduated at the age of 14. He then attended high school in [Kendallville, Indiana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kendallville,_Indiana "Kendallville, Indiana"). After graduating in 1911, he obtained a teacher's certificate from [Earlham College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earlham_College "Earlham College"), and taught in a small school house in Indiana. He later moved to Montana, where his mother was then living, and continued to teach there. Urey entered the [University of Montana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Montana "University of Montana") in [Missoula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Missoula "Missoula") in the autumn of 1914. Unlike Eastern universities of the time, the University of Montana was co-educational in both students and teachers. Urey earned a [Bachelor of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_Science "Bachelor of Science") (BS) degree in [zoology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoology "Zoology") there in 1917.

As a result of the [United States entry into World War I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_entry_into_World_War_I "United States entry into World War I") that same year, there was strong pressure to support the war effort. Urey was raised in a religious sect that opposed war. One of his professors suggested he support the wartime effort by working as a chemist. Urey took a job with the [Barrett Chemical Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AlliedSignal "AlliedSignal") in [Philadelphia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philadelphia "Philadelphia"), making [TNT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TNT "TNT") rather than joining the army as a soldier. After the war, he returned to the University of Montana as an instructor in [chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemistry "Chemistry").

An academic career required a doctorate, so in 1921 Urey enrolled in a [PhD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PhD "PhD") program at the [University of California, Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Berkeley "University of California, Berkeley"), where he studied [thermodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermodynamics "Thermodynamics") under [Gilbert N. Lewis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gilbert_N._Lewis "Gilbert N. Lewis"). His initial attempt at a thesis was on the [ionization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionization "Ionization") of [cesium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cesium "Cesium") vapor. He ran into difficulties, and [Meghnad Saha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meghnad_Saha "Meghnad Saha") published a better paper on the same subject. Urey then wrote his thesis on the ionization states of an [ideal gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ideal_gas "Ideal gas"), which was subsequently published in the *[Astrophysical Journal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrophysical_Journal "Astrophysical Journal")*. After he received his PhD in 1923, Urey was awarded a fellowship by [the American-Scandinavian Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_American-Scandinavian_Foundation "The American-Scandinavian Foundation") to study at the [Niels Bohr Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niels_Bohr_Institute "Niels Bohr Institute") in [Copenhagen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copenhagen "Copenhagen"), where he met [Werner Heisenberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Werner_Heisenberg "Werner Heisenberg"), [Hans Kramers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Kramers "Hans Kramers"), [Wolfgang Pauli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolfgang_Pauli "Wolfgang Pauli"), [Georg von Hevesy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georg_von_Hevesy "Georg von Hevesy"), and [John Slater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_C._Slater "John C. Slater"). At the conclusion of his stay, he traveled to Germany, where he met [Albert Einstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Einstein "Albert Einstein") and [James Franck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Franck "James Franck").

On returning to the United States, Urey received an offer of a [National Research Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_National_Research_Council "United States National Research Council") fellowship to [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University "Harvard University"), and also received an offer to be a research associate at [Johns Hopkins University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johns_Hopkins_University "Johns Hopkins University"). He chose the latter. Before taking up the job, he traveled to [Seattle, Washington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seattle,_Washington "Seattle, Washington") to visit his mother. On the way, he stopped by [Everett, Washington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Everett,_Washington "Everett, Washington"), where he knew Dr. Kate Daum, a colleague from the University of Montana. Dr. Daum introduced Urey to her sister Frieda. Urey and Frieda soon became engaged. They were married at her father's house in [Lawrence, Kansas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawrence,_Kansas "Lawrence, Kansas") in 1926. The couple had four children: [Gertrude Bessie (Elizabeth)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_Baranger "Elizabeth Baranger"), born in 1927; Frieda Rebecca, born in 1929; Mary Alice, born in 1934; and John Clayton Urey, born in 1939.

At Johns Hopkins, Urey and [Arthur Ruark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Ruark "Arthur Ruark") wrote *Atoms, Quanta and Molecules* (1930), one of the first English texts on [quantum mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_mechanics "Quantum mechanics") and its applications to atomic and molecular systems. In 1929, Urey became an associate professor of chemistry at [Columbia University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia_University "Columbia University"), where his colleagues included [Rudolph Schoenheimer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudolph_Schoenheimer "Rudolph Schoenheimer"), [David Rittenberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Rittenberg "David Rittenberg"), and [T. I. Taylor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T._I._Taylor?action=edit&redlink=1 "T. I. Taylor").

## Deuterium

In the 1920s, [William Giauque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Giauque "William Giauque") and [Herrick L. Johnston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herrick_L._Johnston "Herrick L. Johnston") discovered the stable [isotopes of oxygen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isotopes_of_oxygen "Isotopes of oxygen"). Isotopes were not well understood at the time; [James Chadwick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Chadwick "James Chadwick") would not discover the [neutron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron "Neutron") until 1932. Two systems were used for classifying them based on their chemical and physical properties. The latter was determined using the [mass spectrograph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_spectrograph "Mass spectrograph"). Since it was known the [atomic weight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_weight "Atomic weight") of oxygen was almost exactly 16 times as heavy as hydrogen, [Raymond Birge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raymond_Thayer_Birge "Raymond Thayer Birge") and [Donald Menzel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Menzel "Donald Menzel") hypothesized hydrogen had more than one isotope as well. Based upon the difference between the results of the two methods, they predicted only one hydrogen atom in 4,500 was of the heavy isotope.

In 1931, Urey set out to find it. Urey and George M. Murphy (1903–1968), calculated from the [Balmer series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balmer_series "Balmer series") that the heavy isotope should have lines [blueshifted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blueshift "Blueshift") (correspondingly the light isotope [redshifted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redshift "Redshift")) by 1.1 to 1.8 [ångströms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ångström "Ångström") (1.1×10−10 to 1.8×10−10 [metres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metre "Metre")). Urey had access to a 21-foot (6.4 m) [grating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffraction_grating "Diffraction grating") [spectrograph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectrograph "Spectrograph"), a sensitive device recently installed at Columbia and capable of resolving the Balmer series. With a resolution of 1 Å per millimetre, the machine should have produced a difference of about 1 millimetre. However, since only one atom in 4,500 was heavy, the line on the spectrograph was very faint. Urey therefore decided to delay publishing their results until he had more conclusive evidence it was heavy hydrogen.

Urey and Murphy calculated from the [Debye model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Debye_model "Debye model") the heavy isotope would have a slightly higher boiling point than the light one. By carefully warming liquid hydrogen, 5 litres of liquid hydrogen could be distilled to 1 millilitre, which would be enriched in the heavy isotope by 100 to 200 times. To obtain five litres of [liquid hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid_hydrogen "Liquid hydrogen"), they traveled to the cryogenics laboratory at the [National Bureau of Standards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Bureau_of_Standards "National Bureau of Standards") in Washington, D.C., where they obtained the help of [Ferdinand Brickwedde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferdinand_Brickwedde "Ferdinand Brickwedde"), whom Urey had known at Johns Hopkins.

The first sample Brickwedde sent was evaporated at 20 K (−253.2 °C; −423.7 °F) at a pressure of 1 standard atmosphere (100 kPa). To their surprise, this showed no evidence of enrichment. Brickwedde then prepared a second sample evaporated at 14 K (−259.1 °C; −434.5 °F) at a pressure of 53 mmHg (7.1 kPa). On this sample, the Balmer lines for heavy hydrogen were seven times as intense. The paper announcing the discovery of heavy hydrogen, later named [deuterium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deuterium "Deuterium"), was jointly published by Urey, Murphy, and Brickwedde in 1932. Urey was awarded the [Nobel Prize in Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Chemistry "Nobel Prize in Chemistry") in 1934 "for his discovery of heavy hydrogen". He declined to attend the ceremony in Stockholm so he could be present at the birth of his daughter Mary Alice. He was elected to both the [American Philosophical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Philosophical_Society "American Philosophical Society") and the United States [National Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Academy_of_Sciences "National Academy of Sciences") the following year. Urey also received the [Willard Gibbs Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Willard_Gibbs_Award "Willard Gibbs Award") in 1934, and the [Davy Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Davy_Medal "Davy Medal") in 1940.

Working with [Edward W. Washburn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Wight_Washburn "Edward Wight Washburn") from the Bureau of Standards, Urey subsequently discovered the cause of the anomalous sample. Brickwedde's hydrogen had been separated from water by [electrolysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrolysis "Electrolysis"), resulting in a depleted sample. Moreover, [Francis William Aston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_William_Aston "Francis William Aston") had reported his calculated value for the atomic weight of hydrogen was wrong, thereby invalidating Birge and Menzel's original reasoning. The discovery of deuterium stood, however.

Urey and Washburn attempted to use electrolysis to create pure [heavy water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heavy_water "Heavy water"). Their technique was sound, but they were beaten to it in 1933 by Lewis, who had the resources of the University of California at his disposal. Using the [Born–Oppenheimer approximation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Born–Oppenheimer_approximation "Born–Oppenheimer approximation"), Urey and [David Rittenberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Rittenberg "David Rittenberg") calculated the properties of gases containing hydrogen and deuterium. They extended this to enriching compounds of carbon, nitrogen, and oxygen. These could be used as tracers in [biochemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biochemistry "Biochemistry"), resulting in a whole new way of examining chemical reactions. He founded the *[Journal of Chemical Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Journal_of_Chemical_Physics "Journal of Chemical Physics")* in 1932, and was its first editor, serving in that capacity until 1940.

At Columbia, Urey chaired the University Federation for Democracy and Intellectual Freedom. He supported [Atlanticist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlanticism "Atlanticism") [Clarence Streit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clarence_Streit "Clarence Streit")'s proposal for a federal union of the world's major [democracies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democracies "Democracies"), and the [republican](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republican_faction_(Spanish_Civil_War) "Republican faction (Spanish Civil War)") cause during the [Spanish Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_Civil_War "Spanish Civil War"). He was an early opponent of German [Nazism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazism "Nazism") and assisted refugee scientists, including [Enrico Fermi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enrico_Fermi "Enrico Fermi"), by helping them find work in the United States, and to adjust to life in a new country.

## Manhattan Project

By the time World War II broke out in Europe in 1939, Urey was recognized as a world expert on isotope separation. Thus far, separation had involved only the light elements. In 1939 and 1940, Urey published two papers on the separation of heavier isotopes in which he proposed centrifugal separation. This assumed great importance due to Niels Bohr speculating [uranium 235](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium_235 "Uranium 235") was [fissile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fissile "Fissile"). Because it was considered "very doubtful whether a chain reaction can be established without separating 235 from the rest of the uranium," Urey began intensive studies of how uranium enrichment might be achieved. Apart from centrifugal separation, [George Kistiakowsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Kistiakowsky "George Kistiakowsky") suggested [gaseous diffusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaseous_diffusion "Gaseous diffusion") might be a possible method. A third possibility was [thermal diffusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium_enrichment#Thermal_diffusion "Uranium enrichment"). Urey coordinated all isotope separation research efforts, including the effort to produce heavy water, which could be used as a [neutron moderator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron_moderator "Neutron moderator") in [nuclear reactors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_reactor "Nuclear reactor").

The [S-1 Executive Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S-1_Executive_Committee "S-1 Executive Committee") at [Bohemian Grove](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bohemian_Grove "Bohemian Grove"), September 13, 1942. From left to right are Urey, [Ernest O. Lawrence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_O._Lawrence "Ernest O. Lawrence"), [James B. Conant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_B._Conant "James B. Conant"), [Lyman J. Briggs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyman_J._Briggs "Lyman J. Briggs"), [Eger V. Murphree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eger_V._Murphree "Eger V. Murphree"), and [Arthur H. Compton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_H._Compton "Arthur H. Compton").

In May 1941, Urey was appointed to the [Committee on Uranium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S-1_Executive_Committee "S-1 Executive Committee"), which oversaw the uranium project as part of the [National Defense Research Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Defense_Research_Committee "National Defense Research Committee") (NDRC). In 1941, Urey and [George B. Pegram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_B._Pegram "George B. Pegram") led a diplomatic mission to England to establish co-operation on development of the atomic bomb. The British were optimistic about gaseous diffusion, but it was clear both gaseous and centrifugal methods faced formidable technical obstacles. In May 1943, as the [Manhattan Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manhattan_Project "Manhattan Project") gained momentum, Urey became head of the wartime Substitute Alloy Materials Laboratories ([SAM Laboratories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SAM_Laboratories "SAM Laboratories")) at Columbia. SAM was responsible for the heavy water and all the isotope enrichment processes except [Ernest Lawrence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_Lawrence "Ernest Lawrence")'s electromagnetic process.

Early reports on the centrifugal method indicated it was not as efficient as predicted. Urey suggested that a more efficient but technically more complicated countercurrent system be used instead of the previous flow-through method. By November 1941, technical obstacles seemed formidable enough for the process to be abandoned. Countercurrent centrifuges were developed after the war, and today are the favored method in many countries.

The gaseous diffusion process remained more encouraging, although it too had technical obstacles to overcome. By the end of 1943, Urey had over 700 people working for him on gaseous diffusion. The process involved hundreds of cascades, in which corrosive [uranium hexafluoride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium_hexafluoride "Uranium hexafluoride") diffused through gaseous barriers, becoming progressively more enriched at every stage. A major problem was finding proper seals for the pumps, but by far the greatest difficulty lay in constructing an appropriate diffusion barrier. Construction of the huge [K-25](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K-25 "K-25") gaseous diffusion plant was well under way before a suitable barrier became available in quantity in 1944. As a backup, Urey championed thermal diffusion.

Worn out by the effort, Urey left the project in February 1945, handing over his responsibilities to R. H. Crist. The K-25 plant commenced operation in March 1945, and as the bugs were worked out, the plant operated with remarkable efficiency and economy. For a time, uranium was fed into the [S50 liquid thermal diffusion plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S-50_(Manhattan_Project) "S-50 (Manhattan Project)"), then the K-25 gaseous plant, and finally the [Y-12 electromagnetic separation plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Y-12_National_Security_Complex "Y-12 National Security Complex"); but soon after the war ended the thermal and electromagnetic separation plants were closed down, and separation was performed by K-25 alone. Along with its twin K-27, constructed in 1946, it became the principal isotope separation plant in the early post-war period. For his work on the Manhattan Project, Urey was awarded the [Medal for Merit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medal_for_Merit "Medal for Merit") by the Project director, [Major General](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Major_General_(United_States) "Major General (United States)") [Leslie R. Groves, Jr.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leslie_R._Groves,_Jr. "Leslie R. Groves, Jr.")

## Post-war years

After the war, Urey became professor of chemistry at the [Institute for Nuclear Studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_for_Nuclear_Studies "Institute for Nuclear Studies"), and then became Ryerson professor of chemistry at the [University of Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Chicago "University of Chicago") in 1952. He did not continue his pre-war research with isotopes. However, applying the knowledge gained with hydrogen to oxygen, he realized the [fractionation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fractionation "Fractionation") between [carbonate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbonate "Carbonate") and water for [oxygen-18](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxygen-18 "Oxygen-18") and [oxygen-16](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxygen-16 "Oxygen-16") would decrease by a factor of 1.04 between 0 and 25 °C (32 and 77 °F). The ratio of the isotopes could then be used to determine average temperatures, assuming the measurement equipment was sufficiently sensitive. The team included his colleague [Ralph Buchsbaum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ralph_Buchsbaum "Ralph Buchsbaum"). Examination of a 100-million-year-old [belemnite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belemnite "Belemnite") then indicated the summer and winter temperatures it had lived through over a period of four years. For this pioneering [paleoclimatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paleoclimatology "Paleoclimatology") research, Urey was awarded the [Arthur L. Day Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_L._Day_Medal "Arthur L. Day Medal") by the [Geological Society of America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geological_Society_of_America "Geological Society of America"), and the [V. M. Goldschmidt Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V._M._Goldschmidt_Award "V. M. Goldschmidt Award") of the [Geochemical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geochemical_Society "Geochemical Society"). At the University of Chicago, Urey contributed to the [Urey–Bigeleisen–Mayer equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urey–Bigeleisen–Mayer_equation "Urey–Bigeleisen–Mayer equation"), a model of stable isotope fractionation.

Miller–Urey experiment

Urey actively campaigned against the 1946 [May-Johnson bill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_Energy_Act_of_1946 "Atomic Energy Act of 1946") because he feared it would lead to military control of nuclear energy, but supported and fought for the McMahon bill that replaced it, and ultimately created the [Atomic Energy Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Atomic_Energy_Commission "United States Atomic Energy Commission"). Urey's commitment to the ideal of [world government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_government "World government") dated from before the war, but the possibility of [nuclear war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_war "Nuclear war") made it only more urgent in his mind. He went on lecture tours against war, and became involved in Congressional debates regarding nuclear issues. He argued publicly on behalf of [Ethel and Julius Rosenberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethel_and_Julius_Rosenberg "Ethel and Julius Rosenberg"), and was called before the [House Committee on Un-American Activities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_Un-American_Activities_Committee "House Un-American Activities Committee").

## Cosmochemistry and the Miller–Urey experiment

In later life, Urey helped develop the field of [cosmochemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmochemistry "Cosmochemistry") and is credited with coining the term. His work on oxygen-18 led him to develop theories about the [abundance of the chemical elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abundance_of_the_chemical_elements "Abundance of the chemical elements") on Earth, and of their abundance and  [evolution in the stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stellar_nucleosynthesis "Stellar nucleosynthesis"). Urey summarized his work in *The Planets: Their Origin and Development* (1952). Urey speculated the early terrestrial [atmosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_atmosphere "Earth's atmosphere") was composed of [ammonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ammonia "Ammonia"), [methane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methane "Methane"), and hydrogen. One of his Chicago graduate students, [Stanley L. Miller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanley_L._Miller "Stanley L. Miller"), showed in the *[Miller–Urey experiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miller–Urey_experiment "Miller–Urey experiment")* that if such a mixture is exposed to electric sparks and water, it can interact to produce [amino acids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amino_acids "Amino acids"), commonly considered the building blocks of life.

Urey also researched the [isotopic abundance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_abundance "Natural abundance") of cosmic bodies, particularly the contribution of radioisotopes to [internal heating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internal_heating "Internal heating"). In tribute to his contributions, scientists refer to the ratio of a planet's internal heat generation to surface heat flux as the *Urey ratio*. Urey spent a year in the United Kingdom as a visiting professor at [Oxford University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford_University "Oxford University") in 1956 and 1957. In 1958, he reached the University of Chicago's retirement age of 65, but he accepted a post as a professor at large at the new [University of California, San Diego](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_San_Diego "University of California, San Diego") (UCSD), and moved to [La Jolla, California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Jolla,_California "La Jolla, California"). He was subsequently made a [professor emeritus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Professor_emeritus "Professor emeritus") there from 1970 to 1981. Urey helped build up the science faculty there. He was one of the founding members of UCSD's school of chemistry, which was created in 1960, along with Stanley Miller, [Hans Suess](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Suess "Hans Suess"), and [Jim Arnold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_R._Arnold "James R. Arnold").

In the late 1950s and early 1960s, space science became a topic of research in the wake of the launch of [Sputnik 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sputnik_1 "Sputnik 1"). Urey helped persuade [NASA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NASA "NASA") to make uncrewed probes to the Moon a priority. When [Apollo 11](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_11 "Apollo 11") returned [Moon rock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moon_rock "Moon rock") samples from the Moon, Urey examined them at the [Lunar Receiving Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_Receiving_Laboratory "Lunar Receiving Laboratory"). The samples supported Urey's contention that the Moon and the Earth shared a common origin. While at UCSD, Urey published 105 scientific papers, 47 of them about lunar topics. When asked why he continued to work so hard, he joked, "Well, you know I'm not on tenure anymore."

## Death and legacy

Urey enjoyed gardening and raising [cattleya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cattleya "Cattleya"), [cymbidium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cymbidium "Cymbidium") and other [orchids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orchids "Orchids"). He died at La Jolla, California on 5 January 1981, and is buried at the Fairfield Cemetery in [DeKalb County, Indiana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DeKalb_County,_Indiana "DeKalb County, Indiana").

Apart from his Nobel Prize, he also won the [Franklin Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franklin_Medal "Franklin Medal") in 1943, the [J. Lawrence Smith Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._Lawrence_Smith_Medal "J. Lawrence Smith Medal") in 1962, the [Gold Medal of the Royal Astronomical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold_Medal_of_the_Royal_Astronomical_Society "Gold Medal of the Royal Astronomical Society") in 1966, the Golden Plate Award of the [American Academy of Achievement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academy_of_Achievement "Academy of Achievement") in 1966, and the [Priestley Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Priestley_Medal "Priestley Medal") of the [American Chemical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Chemical_Society "American Chemical Society") in 1973. In 1964 he received the [National Medal of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Medal_of_Science "National Medal of Science"). He became a [Fellow of the Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow_of_the_Royal_Society "Fellow of the Royal Society") in 1947. Named after him are [lunar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moon "Moon") [impact crater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impact_crater "Impact crater") [Urey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urey_(crater) "Urey (crater)"), asteroid [4716 Urey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/4716_Urey "4716 Urey"), and the [H. C. Urey Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H._C._Urey_Prize "H. C. Urey Prize"), awarded for achievement in [planetary sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planetary_science "Planetary science") by the [American Astronomical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Astronomical_Society "American Astronomical Society"). The Harold C. Urey Middle School in Walkerton, Indiana, is also named for him, as is [Urey Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urey_Hall "Urey Hall"), the chemistry building at [Revelle College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revelle_College "Revelle College"), UCSD, in La Jolla, and the Harold C. Urey Lecture Hall at the University of Montana. UCSD has also established a Harold C. Urey chair whose first holder was James Arnold.

Urey's daughter [Elizabeth Baranger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_Baranger "Elizabeth Baranger") also became a notable physicist.
