**Eduard Buchner** (German: [[ˈeːduaʁt ˈbuːxnɐ]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Standard_German "Help:IPA/Standard German") [ⓘ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:De-Eduard_Buchner.ogg "File:De-Eduard Buchner.ogg"); 20 May 1860 – 13 August 1917) was a German
[chemist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemist "Chemist")
and expert on fermentation (sometimes called a
[zymologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zymurgy "Zymurgy")), awarded the 1907
[Nobel Prize in Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Chemistry "Nobel Prize in Chemistry") for his work on
[fermentation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermentation_(biochemistry) "Fermentation (biochemistry)").

## Biography

### Early years

Buchner was born in
[Munich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Munich "Munich") to a physician and Doctor Extraordinary of Forensic Medicine. His older brother was the bacteriologist
[Hans Ernst August Buchner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Ernst_August_Buchner "Hans Ernst August Buchner"). In 1884, he began studies of chemistry with [Adolf von Baeyer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adolf_von_Baeyer "Adolf von Baeyer") and of botany with [Carl Nägeli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Nägeli "Carl Nägeli"), at the Botanic Institute in Munich. After a period working with Otto Fischer (cousin of [Emil Fischer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emil_Fischer "Emil Fischer")) at the [University of Erlangen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Erlangen–Nuremberg "University of Erlangen–Nuremberg"), Buchner was awarded a doctorate from the [Ludwig-Maximilians-Universität München](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludwig-Maximilians-Universität_München "Ludwig-Maximilians-Universität München") in 1888 under [Theodor Curtius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodor_Curtius "Theodor Curtius").

### Academics

Buchner was appointed assistant lecturer in the organic laboratory of
[Adolf von Baeyer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adolf_von_Baeyer "Adolf von Baeyer") in 1889 at the Ludwig-Maximilians-Universität München. In 1891, he was promoted to lecturer at the same university.

In the autumn of 1893, Buchner moved to
[Kiel University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kiel_University "Kiel University") and appointed professor in 1895. In the next year, he was appointed Professor Extraordinary for Analytical and Pharmaceutical Chemistry in the chemical laboratory of H. von Pechmann at the
[University of Tübingen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Tübingen "University of Tübingen").

In October 1898, he was appointed to the Chair of General Chemistry in the
[Agricultural University of Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agricultural_University_of_Berlin "Agricultural University of Berlin"), fully training his assistants by himself, and received his habilitation in 1900.

In 1909, he was transferred to the
[University of Breslau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Breslau "University of Breslau") (reorganised to be
[University of Wrocław](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Wrocław "University of Wrocław") in 1945), and in 1911, he moved to [University of Würzburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Würzburg "University of Würzburg").

#### The Nobel Prize

Buchner received the
[Nobel Prize in Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Chemistry "Nobel Prize in Chemistry")
in 1907. The experiment for which Buchner won the Nobel Prize consisted of producing a
[cell-free](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell-free_system "Cell-free system") extract of
[yeast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yeast "Yeast") cells and showing that this "press juice" could ferment sugar. This dealt yet another blow to
[vitalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vitalism "Vitalism") by showing that the presence of living yeast cells was not needed for fermentation. The cell-free extract was produced by combining dry yeast cells,
[quartz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quartz "Quartz") and
[kieselguhr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kieselguhr "Kieselguhr") and then pulverizing the yeast cells with a pestle and mortar. This mixture would then become moist as the yeast cells' contents would come out of the cells. Once this step was done, the moist mixture would be put through a press and the resulting "press juice" had
[glucose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glucose "Glucose"),
[fructose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fructose "Fructose"),
or [maltose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maltose "Maltose") added and
[carbon dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide "Carbon dioxide") was seen to evolve, sometimes for days. Microscopic investigation revealed no living yeast cells in the extract. Buchner hypothesized that yeast cells secrete proteins into their environment in order to ferment sugars, but it was later found that fermentation occurs inside the yeast cells.
[Maria Manasseina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maria_Manasseina "Maria Manasseina") claimed to have discovered free-cell fermentation a generation earlier than Buchner, but Buchner and Rapp considered that she was subjectively convinced of the existence of an enzyme of fermentation, and that her experimental evidence was unconvincing.

### Personal life

Buchner married Lotte Stahl in 1900. At the outbreak of the
[First World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I "World War I"), he volunteered in the
[Imperial German Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_German_Army "Imperial German Army") and rose to the rank of *[Major](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Major_(Germany) "Major (Germany)")*, commanding a munition-transport unit on the
[Western](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Front_(World_War_I) "Western Front (World War I)") and then
[Eastern Front](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Front_(World_War_I) "Eastern Front (World War I)"). In March 1916, he returned to the University of Würzburg. In April 1917, he volunteered again. On 11 August 1917, while stationed at
[Focșani, Romania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Focșani "Focșani"), he was hit by a shell fragment in the left thigh and died in a field hospital two days later. He died in the
[Battle of Mărășești](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Mărășești "Battle of Mărășești") and is buried in the cemetery of German soldiers in Focșani.

Though it is believed by some that the
[Büchner flask](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Büchner_flask "Büchner flask") and the
[Büchner funnel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Büchner_funnel "Büchner funnel") are named for him, they are actually named for the industrial chemist
[Ernst Büchner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernst_Büchner "Ernst Büchner").
