**Elias James Corey** (born July 12, 1928) is an American [organic chemist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_chemistry "Organic chemistry"). In 1990, he won the [Nobel Prize in Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Chemistry "Nobel Prize in Chemistry") "for his development of the theory and methodology of [organic synthesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_synthesis "Organic synthesis")", specifically [retrosynthetic analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retrosynthetic_analysis "Retrosynthetic analysis").

## Biography

E.J. Corey (the surname was anglicized from [Levantine Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levantine_Arabic "Levantine Arabic") *[Khoury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khoury "Khoury")*, meaning *priest*) was born to [Lebanese Greek Orthodox Christian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebanese_Greek_Orthodox_Christians "Lebanese Greek Orthodox Christians") immigrants Fatima (née Hasham) and Elias Corey in [Methuen, Massachusetts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methuen,_Massachusetts "Methuen, Massachusetts"), 50 km (31 mi) north of Boston. His mother changed his name from William to "Elias" to honor his father, who died eighteen months after Corey's birth. His widowed mother, brother, two sisters, aunt and uncle all lived together in a spacious house, struggling through the [Great Depression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Depression "Great Depression"). As a young boy, Corey was independent and enjoyed sports such as baseball, football, and hiking. He attended a Catholic elementary school and [Lawrence High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawrence_High_School_(Massachusetts) "Lawrence High School (Massachusetts)") in [Lawrence, Massachusetts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawrence,_Massachusetts "Lawrence, Massachusetts").

At the age of 16 Corey entered [MIT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MIT "MIT"), where he earned both a [bachelor's degree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor's_degree "Bachelor's degree") in 1948 and a [Ph.D.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Philosophy "Doctor of Philosophy") under Professor [John C. Sheehan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_C._Sheehan "John C. Sheehan") in 1951. Upon entering MIT, Corey's only experience with science was in mathematics, and he began his college career pursuing a degree in engineering. After his first chemistry class in his sophomore year he began rethinking his long-term career plans and graduated with a bachelor's degree in chemistry. Immediately thereafter, at the invitation of Professor John C. Sheehan, Corey remained at MIT for his Ph.D. After his graduate career he was offered an appointment at the [University of Illinois at Urbana–Champaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Illinois_at_Urbana–Champaign "University of Illinois at Urbana–Champaign"), where he became a full professor of chemistry in 1956 at the age of 27. He was initiated as a member of the Zeta chapter of [Alpha Chi Sigma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpha_Chi_Sigma "Alpha Chi Sigma") at the University of Illinois in 1952. In 1959, he moved to [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University "Harvard University"), and in 1967 he was appointed the Sheldon Emory Professor and became a Guggenheim Fellow (1968-69). During his time at Harvard, Corey synthesised around 100 molecules, which previously had been only found in nature. At Harvard, Corey is currently an emeritus professor of organic chemistry; his group maintains an active research program. He chose to work in organic chemistry because of "its intrinsic beauty and its great relevance to human health". He has also been an advisor to Pfizer for more than 50 years.

Among numerous honors, Corey was awarded the [National Medal of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Medal_of_Science "National Medal of Science") in 1988, the [Nobel Prize in Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Chemistry "Nobel Prize in Chemistry") in 1990, and the [American Chemical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Chemical_Society "American Chemical Society")'s greatest honor, the [Priestley Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Priestley_Medal "Priestley Medal"), in 2004.

## Major contributions

### Reagents

Corey has developed several new synthetic reagents:

- [Pyridinium chlorochromate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyridinium_chlorochromate "Pyridinium chlorochromate") (PCC), also known as the Corey–Suggs reagent, is widely used for the oxidation of [alcohols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alcohol_(chemistry) "Alcohol (chemistry)") to corresponding [ketones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ketone "Ketone") and [aldehydes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aldehyde "Aldehyde"). PCC has several advantages over other commercial oxidants. An air-stable yellow solid, it is only slightly hygroscopic. Unlike other oxidizing agents, PCC requires only about 1.5 equivalents  to complete a single oxidation (scheme 1).

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b9/PCC_mechanism.png/960px-PCC_mechanism.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:PCC_mechanism.png)

In the reaction, the alcohol nucleophilically displaces chlorine from the electropositive [chromium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromium "Chromium")(VI) metal. The [chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloride "Chloride") [anion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anion "Anion") then acts as a base to afford the aldehyde product and chromium(IV).

The slightly acidic character of PCC makes it useful for cyclization reactions with alcohols and [alkenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkene "Alkene") (Scheme 2).

[![reactivity of PCC under acidic conditions](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/27/PCC_under_acidic_conditions2.png/960px-PCC_under_acidic_conditions2.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:PCC_under_acidic_conditions2.png "reactivity of PCC under acidic conditions")

reactivity of PCC under acidic conditions

The initial oxidation yields the corresponding aldehyde, which can then undergo a [Prins reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prins_reaction "Prins reaction") with the neighboring [alkene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkene "Alkene"). After elimination and further oxidation, the product is a cyclic [ketone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ketone "Ketone"). Conversely, powdered [sodium acetate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium_acetate "Sodium acetate") co-reagent inhibits reaction after formation of the aldehyde.

PCC's oxidative robustness has also rendered it useful in the realm of total synthesis (Scheme 3). This example illustrates the [Babler oxidation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babler_oxidation "Babler oxidation") reaction, in which tertiary [allylic alcohols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allylic_alcohol "Allylic alcohol") undergo a [3,3]-[sigmatropic rearrangement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigmatropic_rearrangement "Sigmatropic rearrangement").

[![[3,3] rearrangement with PCC](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/87/PCC_rearrangement3.png/960px-PCC_rearrangement3.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:PCC_rearrangement3.png "[3,3] rearrangement with PCC")

[3,3] rearrangement with PCC

- *t*-Butyldimethylsilyl ether (TBS), triisopropylsilyl ether (TIPS), and methoxyethoxymethyl (MEM) are popular alcohol [protecting groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protecting_group "Protecting group"). The development of these protecting groups allowed the synthesis of several [natural products](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_product "Natural product") whose [functional groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functional_group "Functional group") could not withstand standard chemical transformations. Although the synthetic community attempts to minimize the use of protecting groups, it is still rare that a published natural-product synthesis omits them entirely.

Since 1972 the TBS group has become the most popular [silicon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon "Silicon") protecting group (Scheme 4). TBS is stable to [chromatography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromatography "Chromatography") and labile enough to cleave under basic and acidic conditions. More importantly, TBS [ethers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ether "Ether") are stable to such carbon nucleophiles as Grignard reagents and enolates.

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/40/TBS_primary_deprotection4.png/500px-TBS_primary_deprotection4.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:TBS_primary_deprotection4.png)

[CSA (Camphorsulfonic acid)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camphorsulfonic_acid "Camphorsulfonic acid") selectively removes a primary TBS ether in the presence of TIPS and tertiary TBS ethers. Other TBS deprotection methods include acids (also Lewis acids), and [fluorides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluoride "Fluoride").

TIPS protecting groups provide increased selectivity of primary over secondary and tertiary alcohol protection. Their ethers are more stable under acidic and basic conditions than TBS ethers, but less labile for deprotection. The most common cleavage reagents employ the same conditions as TBS ether, but longer reaction times.

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f8/Primary_TIPS_deprotection5.png/960px-Primary_TIPS_deprotection5.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Primary_TIPS_deprotection5.png)

Usually TBAF severs TBS ethers, but the hindered TBS ether above survives primary TIPS removal (scheme 5).

The MEM protecting group was first described by Corey in 1976. This protecting group is similar in reactivity and stability to other alkoxy methyl ethers under acidic conditions. Acidic conditions usually accomplish cleavage of MEM protecting groups, but coordination with metal halides greatly enhances lability (scheme 6).

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e8/MEM_Zn_deprotection6.png/960px-MEM_Zn_deprotection6.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:MEM_Zn_deprotection6.png)

- 1,3-[Dithianes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dithiane "Dithiane") are a temporary modification of a [carbonyl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbonyl "Carbonyl") group that reverses their reactivity in displacement and addition reactions. Dithianation introduced *[umpolung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umpolung "Umpolung")* chemistry, now a key concept in organic synthesis.

The formations of dithianes can be accomplished with a Lewis acid (scheme 7) or directly from carbonyl compounds.

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/0a/Dithiane_formation7.png/500px-Dithiane_formation7.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Dithiane_formation7.png)

The pKa of dithianes is approximately 30, allowing deprotonation with an alkyl lithium reagent, typically [n-butyllithium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/N-butyllithium "N-butyllithium").

The reaction between dithianes and aldehydes is now known as the [Corey-Seebach reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corey-Seebach_reaction "Corey-Seebach reaction"). The dithiane, once deprotonated, serves as an acyl anion, attacking incoming [electrophiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrophile "Electrophile"). Dithiane deprotection, usually with HgO, constructs a ketone product.

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a1/1%2C2-dithiane_addition8.png/960px-1%2C2-dithiane_addition8.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:1,2-dithiane_addition8.png)

- Corey also commenced detailed studies on cationic polyolefin cyclizations utilized in enzymatic production of [cholesterol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cholesterol "Cholesterol") from simpler plant terpenes. Corey established the details of the remarkable cyclization process by first studying the biological synthesis of sterols from squalene.

### Methodology

Several reactions developed in Corey's lab have become commonplace in modern synthetic organic chemistry. At least 302 methods have been developed in the Corey group since 1950. Several reactions have been named after him:
[Corey-Itsuno reduction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corey-Itsuno_reduction "Corey-Itsuno reduction"), also known as the Corey-Bakshi-Shibata reduction, is an enantioselective reduction of ketones to alcohols through an oxazaborolidine [catalyst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catalysis "Catalysis"), with various boranes as the stoichiometric reductant.

The Corey group first demonstrated [the catalyst's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CBS_catalyst "CBS catalyst") synthesis using borane and the chiral amino acid [proline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proline "Proline") (scheme 9).

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e3/CBS_formation9.png/960px-CBS_formation9.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:CBS_formation9.png)

Later, Corey demonstrated that substituted boranes were easier to prepare and much more stable.

The reduction mechanism begins with the oxazaborolidine, only slightly basic at [nitrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen "Nitrogen"), coordinating to a borane reductant (scheme 10). Poor donation from the nitrogen to the boron leaves the Lewis acidity mostly intact, allowing coordination to the ketone substrate. The complexation of the substrate occurs from the most accessible lone pair of the [oxygen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxygen "Oxygen"), restricting rotation around the B-O bond due to the sterically neighboring phenyl group.

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/03/CBS_mechanism10.png/500px-CBS_mechanism10.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:CBS_mechanism10.png)

Migration of the hydride from borane to the electrophilic ketone center occurs via a 6-membered ring transition state, leading to a four-membered ring intermediate, ultimately providing the chiral product and regeneration of the catalyst.

The reaction has also been of great use to natural products chemists (scheme 11). The synthesis of dysidiolide by Corey and co-workers was achieved via an enantioselective CBS reduction using a borane-dimethylsulfide complex.

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/45/CBS_total_synthesis11.png/960px-CBS_total_synthesis11.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:CBS_total_synthesis11.png)

[Corey-Fuchs alkyne synthesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corey-Fuchs_reaction "Corey-Fuchs reaction") is the synthesis of terminal [alkynes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkyne "Alkyne") through a one-carbon homologation of aldehydes using [triphenylphosphine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triphenylphosphine "Triphenylphosphine") and carbon tetrabromide. The mechanism is similar to that of a combined [Wittig reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wittig_reaction "Wittig reaction") and [Appel reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appel_reaction "Appel reaction"). Reacting a phosphorus ylide formed *in situ* with the aldehyde substrate yields a dibromoolefin.

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/cc/Corey-fuch_reaction12.png/960px-Corey-fuch_reaction12.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Corey-fuch_reaction12.png)

On treatment with two equivalents of *n*-butyllithium, lithium halogen exchange and deprotonation yields a lithium acetylide species that undergoes hydrolysis to yield the terminal alkyne product (scheme 12).

More recent developments include a modified procedure for one-pot synthesis.

This synthetic transformation has been proven successful in the total synthesis (+)-taylorione by W.J. Kerr and co-workers (scheme 13).

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/ba/Corey-fuch_total_synthesis13.png/960px-Corey-fuch_total_synthesis13.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Corey-fuch_total_synthesis13.png)

The [Corey–Kim oxidation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corey–Kim_oxidation "Corey–Kim oxidation") was a new conversion of alcohols into corresponding aldehydes and ketones. This combination of *N*-chlorosuccinimidosulfonium chloride (NCS), dimethylsulfide (DMS), and [triethylamine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triethylamine "Triethylamine") (TEA) offers a less toxic alternative to chromium-based oxidations.

The Corey-Kim reagent is formed in *situ* when the succinimide and sulfide react to form a dimethylsuccinimidosulfonium chloride species (scheme 14).

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c0/Corey_kim_ox14.png/960px-Corey_kim_ox14.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Corey_kim_ox14.png)

Triethylamine deprotonates the alkoxysulfonium salt at the α position to afford the oxidized product. The reaction accommodates a wide array of functional groups, but allylic and benzylic alcohols are typically transformed into chlorides instead.

Its application in synthesis is based on the mild protocol conditions and functional and protecting group compatibility. In the total synthesis of ingenol, Kuwajima and co-workers exploited the Corey-Kim oxidation by selectively oxidizing the less hindered secondary alcohol(scheme 15).

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/2b/Corey_kim_ox_synthesis_example15.png/960px-Corey_kim_ox_synthesis_example15.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Corey_kim_ox_synthesis_example15.png)

[Corey-Winter olefination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corey-Winter_Olefination "Corey-Winter Olefination") is a stereospecific transformation of 1,2-diols to alkenes involving the diol substrate, thiocarbonyldiimidazole, and excess trialkylphosphite.

The exact mechanism is unknown, but has been narrowed down to two possible pathways. The thionocarbonate and trialkylphosphite either form a phosphorus ylide or carbenoid intermediate.

The reaction is stereospecific for most substrates unless the product would lead to an exceedingly strained structure, as discovered when Corey *et al* attempted to form sterically hindered *trans* alkenes in certain 7-membered rings.

Stereospecfic alkenes are present in several natural products as the method continues to be exploited to yield a series of complex substrates. Professor T.K.M Shing *et al* used the Corey-Winter olefination reaction to synthesize (+)-Boesenoxide (scheme 16).

[![total synthesis example of corey winter olefination](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7b/Corey-winter_olefination_example16.png/960px-Corey-winter_olefination_example16.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Corey-winter_olefination_example16.png "total synthesis example of corey winter olefination")

total synthesis example of corey winter olefination

CBS-type enantioselective [Diels–Alder reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diels–Alder_reaction "Diels–Alder reaction") has been developed using a similar scaffold to the enantioselective CBS reduction. After the development of this reaction the CBS reagent proved to be a very versatile reagent for a series of several powerful synthetic transformations. The use of a chiral Lewis acid such as the CBS catalyst includes a broad range of unsaturated [enones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enone "Enone") substrates.

The reaction likely proceeds via a highly organized 6-membered ring pre-transition state to deliver highly enantio-enriched products (scheme 17).

[![enantioslective diels-alder transition state](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e6/Diels_alder_TS17.png/500px-Diels_alder_TS17.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Diels_alder_TS17.png "enantioslective diels-alder transition state")

enantioslective diels-alder transition state

This transition state likely occurs because of favorable pi-stacking with the phenyl substituent. The enantioselectivity of the process is facilitated from the diene approaching the dienophile from the opposite face of the phenyl substituent.

The Diels-Alder reaction is one of the most powerful transformations in synthetic chemistry. The synthesis of natural products using the Diels-Alder reaction as a transform has been applied especially to the formation of six-membered rings(scheme 18).

[![enantioslective diels-alder in total synthesis](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/ac/Diels_alder_example_enantioselective18.png/960px-Diels_alder_example_enantioselective18.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Diels_alder_example_enantioselective18.png "enantioslective diels-alder in total synthesis")

enantioslective diels-alder in total synthesis

[Corey-Nicolaou macrolactonization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corey-Nicolaou_macrolactonization "Corey-Nicolaou macrolactonization") provides the first method for preparing medium-to-large-size [lactones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lactone "Lactone"). Previously, intermolecular outcompeted intramolecular lactonization even at low concentrations. One big advantage of this reaction is that it is performed under neutral conditions allowing the presence of acid and base-labile functional groups. As of 2016, rings of 7–44 members have been successfully synthesized using this method.

[![mechanism of Corey-Nicolaou macrolactonization](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/93/Corey-nicolaou_macrolactonization19.png/960px-Corey-nicolaou_macrolactonization19.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Corey-nicolaou_macrolactonization19.png "mechanism of Corey-Nicolaou macrolactonization")

mechanism of Corey-Nicolaou macrolactonization

The reaction occurs in the presence of 2,2'-dipyridyl disulfide and triphenylphosphine with reflux of a nonpolar solvent such as [benzene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benzene "Benzene"). The mechanism begins with formation of the 2-pyridinethiol ester (scheme 19). Proton-transfer provides a dipolar intermediate in which the alkoxide [nucleophile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nucleophile "Nucleophile") attacks the electrophilic carbonyl center, providing a tetrahedral intermediate that yields the macrolactone product.

One of the first examples of this protocol was applied to the total synthesis of [zearalenone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zearalenone "Zearalenone") (scheme 20).

[![macrolactonization total synthesis example](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/80/Macrolactonization_example20.png/960px-Macrolactonization_example20.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Macrolactonization_example20.png "macrolactonization total synthesis example")

macrolactonization total synthesis example

The [Johnson-Corey-Chaykovsky reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johnson-Corey-Chaykovsky_reaction "Johnson-Corey-Chaykovsky reaction") synthesizes [epoxides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epoxide "Epoxide") and [cyclopropanes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclopropane "Cyclopropane"). The reaction forms a sulfur ylide in situ that reacts with enones, ketones, aldehydes, and [imines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imine "Imine") to form corresponding epoxides, cyclopropanes, and [aziridines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aziridines "Aziridines"). Two sulfur ylide variants have been employed that give different chemoselective products (scheme 21).The dimethylsulfoxonium methylide provides epoxides from ketones, but yields the cyclopropanes when enones are employed. Dimethylsulfonium methylide transforms ketones and enones to the corresponding epoxides. Dimethylsulfonium methylide is much more reactive and less stable than dimethylsulfoxonium methylide, so it is generated at low temperatures.

[![corey-chaykovsky selectivity](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/70/Corey-chakovsky_reaction21.png/960px-Corey-chakovsky_reaction21.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Corey-chakovsky_reaction21.png "corey-chaykovsky selectivity")

corey-chaykovsky selectivity

Based on their reactivity, another distinct advantage of these two variants is that kinetically they provide a difference in diastereoselectivity. The reaction is very well established, and enantioselective variants (catalytic and stoichiometric) have also been achieved. From a retrosynthetic analysis standpoint, this reaction provides a reasonable alternative to conventional epoxidation reactions with alkenes (scheme 22). Danishefsky utilized this methodology for the synthesis of taxol. Diastereoselectivity is established by 1,3 interactions in the transition state required for epoxide closure.

[![corey-chaykovsky total synthesis example](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/21/Corey-chakovsky_example22.png/960px-Corey-chakovsky_example22.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Corey-chakovsky_example22.png "corey-chaykovsky total synthesis example")

### Total syntheses

E. J. Corey and his research group have completed many [total syntheses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_synthesis "Total synthesis"). At least 265 natural compounds have been synthesized in the Corey group since 1950.

His 1969 total syntheses of several [prostaglandins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prostaglandin "Prostaglandin") are considered classics. Specifically the synthesis of Prostaglandin F2α presents several challenges. The presence of both *cis* and *trans* olefins as well as five asymmetric carbon atoms renders the molecule a desirable challenge for organic chemists. Corey's retrosynthetic analysis outlines a few key disconnections that lead to simplified precursors (scheme 23).

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/eb/Prostaglandin_retro23.png/960px-Prostaglandin_retro23.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Prostaglandin_retro23.png)

Molecular simplification began first by disconnecting both carbon chains with a Wittig reaction and Horner-Wadsworth Emmons modification. The Wittig reaction affords the *cis* product, while the Horner-Wadsworth Emmons produces the *trans* olefin. The published synthesis reveals a 1:1 diastereomeric mixture of the carbonyl reduction using zinc borohydride. However, years later Corey and co-workers established the CBS reduction. One of the examples that exemplified this protocol was an intermediate in the prostaglandin synthesis revealing a 9:1 mixture of the desired diastereomer (scheme 24).

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/5f/Prostoglandin_CBS24.png/500px-Prostoglandin_CBS24.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Prostoglandin_CBS24.png)

The iodolactonization transform affords an allylic alcohol leading to a key Baeyer-Villiger intermediate. This oxidation regioselectively inserts an oxygen atom between the ketone and the most electron-rich site. The pivotal intermediate leads to a straightforward conversion to the Diels-Alder structural goal, which provides the carbon framework for the functionalized cyclopentane ring. Later Corey developed an asymmetric Diels-Alder reaction employing a chiral oxazoborolidine, greatly simplifying the synthetic route to the prostaglandins.

Other notable syntheses:

- [Longifolene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longifolene "Longifolene")

- [Lactacystin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lactacystin "Lactacystin")

### Computer programs

Corey and his research group created [LHASA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LHASA "LHASA"), a program that uses [artificial intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence "Artificial intelligence") to discover sequences of reaction which may lead to total synthesis. The program was one of the first to use a graphical interface to input and display chemical structures.

## Altom suicide

[Jason Altom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jason_Altom "Jason Altom"), one of Corey's students, committed suicide in 1998. Altom's suicide was well-known because of his suicide notes, which called for protection of students from "abusive research advisors," which he stated were a part of the reason he killed himself. One of the letters was addressed to Corey. Altom's suicide note also contained explicit instructions on how to reform the relationship between students and their supervisors.

Corey was reportedly devastated and bewildered by his student's death. Corey said, "That letter doesn't make sense. At the end, Jason must have been delusional or irrational in the extreme." Corey also claimed he never questioned Altom's intellectual contributions. "I did my best to guide Jason as a mountain guide would to guide someone climbing a mountain. I did my best every step of the way," Corey states. "My conscience is clear. Everything Jason did came out of our partnership. We never had the slightest disagreement." Students and professors said Altom actually retained Corey's support even as Altom struggled to make progress.

## Woodward–Hoffmann rules

When awarded the Priestley Medal in 2004, E. J. Corey created a controversy with his claim to have inspired [Robert Burns Woodward](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Burns_Woodward "Robert Burns Woodward") prior to the development of the [Woodward–Hoffmann rules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woodward–Hoffmann_rules "Woodward–Hoffmann rules"). Corey wrote:

*"On May 4, 1964, I suggested to my colleague R. B. Woodward a simple explanation involving the symmetry of the perturbed (HOMO) molecular orbitals for the stereoselective cyclobutene → 1,3-butadiene and 1,3,5-hexatriene → cyclohexadiene conversions that provided the basis for the further development of these ideas into what became known as the Woodward–Hoffmann rules."*

This was Corey's first public statement on his claim that starting on May 5, 1964, Woodward put forth Corey's explanation as his own thought with no mention of Corey and the conversation of May 4. Corey had discussed his claim privately with Hoffmann and close colleagues since 1964. Corey mentions that he made the Priestley statement *"so the historical record would be correct"*.

Corey's claim and contribution were publicly rebutted by [Roald Hoffmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roald_Hoffmann "Roald Hoffmann") in the journal *[Angewandte Chemie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angewandte_Chemie "Angewandte Chemie")*. In the rebuttal, Hoffmann states that he asked Corey over the course of their long discussion of the matter why Corey did not make the issue public. Corey responded that he thought such a public disagreement would hurt Harvard and that he would not "consider doing anything against Harvard, to which I was and am so devoted." Corey also hoped that Woodward himself would correct the historical record "as he grew older, more considerate, and more sensitive to his own conscience." Woodward died suddenly of a heart attack in his sleep in 1979.
