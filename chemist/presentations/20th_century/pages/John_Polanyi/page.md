**John Charles Polanyi** ([Hungarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungarian_language "Hungarian language"): *Polányi János Károly*; born 23 January 1929) is a German-born Canadian [chemist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemist "Chemist"). He was awarded the 1986 [Nobel Prize in Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Chemistry "Nobel Prize in Chemistry") for his research in [chemical reaction dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reaction_dynamics "Reaction dynamics").

Polanyi was born into the prominent Hungarian Polányi (Pollacsek) family in [Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berlin "Berlin"), Germany, prior to his family emigrating in 1933 to the United Kingdom where he was subsequently educated at the [University of Manchester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Manchester "University of Manchester"), achieving his Ph.D in 1952, and did [postdoctoral research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postdoctoral_research "Postdoctoral research") at the [National Research Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Research_Council_(Canada) "National Research Council (Canada)") in Canada (1952-1954) and [Princeton University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University "Princeton University") in [New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Jersey "New Jersey") (1954-1956). Polanyi's first academic appointment was at the [University of Toronto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Toronto "University of Toronto") in 1956, and he remains there as of 2025 as University Professor Emeritus.

In addition to the Nobel Prize, Polanyi has received numerous other awards, including 33 honorary degrees, the [Wolf Prize in Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolf_Prize_in_Chemistry "Wolf Prize in Chemistry") and the [Gerhard Herzberg Canada Gold Medal for Science and Engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerhard_Herzberg_Canada_Gold_Medal_for_Science_and_Engineering "Gerhard Herzberg Canada Gold Medal for Science and Engineering"). Outside his scientific pursuits, Polanyi is active in public policy discussion, especially concerning science and nuclear weapons. According to [György Marx](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/György_Marx "György Marx"), he was one of "[The Martians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Martians_(scientists) "The Martians (scientists)")", a group of prominent Hungarian scientists who emigrated to the United States in the first half of the 20th century.

## Early years

His father, Mihály ([Michael](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Polanyi "Michael Polanyi")), was a chemist, philosopher of science, and economist; his uncle Károly ([Karl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Polanyi "Karl Polanyi")) was an economic historian. Michael was born Jewish and converted to Catholicism. He moved from Germany to Britain in 1933, partly as a result of the persecution of [Jews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jews "Jews") under [Adolf Hitler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adolf_Hitler "Adolf Hitler"). During [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II"), Polanyi's father sent him to Canada for three years when he was 11, so he would be safe from [German bombings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Blitz "The Blitz"). While living in Toronto, he attended the [University of Toronto Schools](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Toronto_Schools "University of Toronto Schools"). After returning to Britain, Polanyi finished high school and attended university at [Manchester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manchester_University "Manchester University"), where he received his undergraduate degree in 1949 and his [PhD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctorate "Doctorate") in 1952. Although his university education was focused in science, he was not convinced it was his calling after finishing high school, when he briefly considered a career as a poet. His father, [Michael Polanyi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Polanyi "Michael Polanyi"), was a professor in the chemistry department during his first year of university, before transferring to a newly created position in the [social studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_studies "Social studies") department. Polanyi's supervisor during his graduate studies was Ernest Warhurst, a former student of his father's. After completing his PhD studies, Polanyi did postdoctoral research at the [National Research Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Research_Council_(Canada) "National Research Council (Canada)") in Ottawa, [Ontario](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ontario "Ontario") from 1952 until 1954, where he worked with [Edgar William Richard Steacie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edgar_William_Richard_Steacie "Edgar William Richard Steacie"). From 1954 until 1956, he was a research associate at [Princeton University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University "Princeton University").

## Academic posts

John Polanyi started at the [University of Toronto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Toronto "University of Toronto") as a lecturer in 1956. He moved up the ranks quickly at the university, being promoted to assistant professor in 1957, associate professor in 1960 and becoming a full professor in 1962. In 1974, he was named University Professor, an honorary title he has retained since.

## Research interests

Polanyi's PhD studies at Manchester University focused on measuring the strengths of [chemical bonds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_bonds "Chemical bonds") using thermal dissociation, building on Warhurst's graduate studies using a sodium flame apparatus to determine the likelihood that a collision between a sodium atom and another molecule would lead to a [chemical reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_reaction "Chemical reaction"). For the majority of his career, Polanyi's research has focused on chemical dynamics, attempting to determine the mechanics of a [chemical reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_reaction "Chemical reaction"), and the properties of chemical species in the [transition state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transition_state "Transition state"). While at the [National Research Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Research_Council_(Canada) "National Research Council (Canada)") (NRC), Polanyi evaluated [transition state theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transition_state_theory "Transition state theory") for its predictive powers, coming to the conclusion that the theory was flawed, largely due to a lack of knowledge about the forces at play in the transition state. Near the end of his stay at NRC, Polanyi worked in [Gerhard Herzberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerhard_Herzberg "Gerhard Herzberg")'s lab, using [spectroscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectroscopy "Spectroscopy") to examine vibrational and rotational excitation in iodine molecules. During Polanyi's time at [Princeton University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University "Princeton University"), he worked with Sir [Hugh Taylor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugh_Stott_Taylor "Hugh Stott Taylor") and his colleagues, Michael Boudart and David Garvin. He was influenced by studies conducted at Princeton looking at the vibrationally excited reaction products between atomic [hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen "Hydrogen") and [ozone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ozone "Ozone").

When Polanyi moved to the [University of Toronto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Toronto "University of Toronto"), his first graduate students were looking for enhanced reaction rates with vibrationally excited hydrogen, as well as looking for the presence of vibrationally excited hydrogen chloride during the [exothermic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exothermic "Exothermic") reaction of molecular chlorine with atomic hydrogen. Graduate student Kenneth Cashion was working with Polanyi when they made their first discoveries about [chemiluminescence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemiluminescence "Chemiluminescence"), the light emitted by an atom molecule when it is in an [excited state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Excited_state "Excited state"). This work was first published in 1958.

In 2009, Polanyi and his colleagues published a paper in *[Nature Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nature_Chemistry "Nature Chemistry")*, entitled "Cooperative molecular dynamics in surface reactions." This more recent research could be influential in [nanotechnology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanotechnology "Nanotechnology"), building devices from single atoms and molecules. Polanyi's work still focuses on the basic workings of chemical reactions, but since his Nobel Prize win in 1986, his methods have changed. While in [Sweden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sweden "Sweden") for the award ceremony, he encountered the three scientists who were awarded the 1986 [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics"), who were honoured for their work in [electron optics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_optics "Electron optics") and [scanning tunneling microscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scanning_tunneling_microscope "Scanning tunneling microscope"). This technology allowed Polanyi and his colleagues to monitor chemical reactions on a very small scale, rather than observing the energy being released using infrared technology. His lab at the University of Toronto currently has 4 scanning tunneling microscopes, valued at approximately $750,000 each.

In addition to his scientific pursuits, Polanyi has also always been keenly aware of the world at large. As a student, he edited a newspaper and displayed an interest in politics. Although his father was a scientist, he did not demonstrate an immediate affinity for chemistry. Beginning in the 1950s, Polanyi became involved in public affairs, especially concerning [nuclear weapons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_weapons "Nuclear weapons"). He founded Canada's [Pugwash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pugwash_Conferences_on_Science_and_World_Affairs "Pugwash Conferences on Science and World Affairs") group in 1960, and served as the chairman for the group from its inception until 1978. Pugwash is a global movement that received the [Nobel Peace Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Peace_Prize "Nobel Peace Prize") in 1995. Their goal is to reduce armed conflict and seek solutions to global problems. He has also been a supporter of "pure" science, and a critic of government policies that do not support such research. He is also a supporter of the [Campaign for the Establishment of a United Nations Parliamentary Assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Campaign_for_the_Establishment_of_a_United_Nations_Parliamentary_Assembly "Campaign for the Establishment of a United Nations Parliamentary Assembly"), an organisation which campaigns for democratic reformation of the United Nations, and the creation of a more accountable international political system. Polanyi often accepts speaking engagements to discuss issues relating to social justice, peace and nuclear proliferation, despite his busy research schedule. He frequently comments on science and public policy issues via the *Letters to the Editor* and *Opinion* sections of *[The Globe and Mail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Globe_and_Mail "The Globe and Mail")* newspaper. He currently serves on the National Advisory Board of the Center for Arms Control and Non-Proliferation, the research arm of [Council for a Livable World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_for_a_Livable_World "Council for a Livable World").

## Awards and honours

### Nobel Prize

Polanyi was awarded the 1986 [Nobel Prize in Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Chemistry "Nobel Prize in Chemistry") for his work in [chemical reaction dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reaction_dynamics "Reaction dynamics"). He shared the prize with [Dudley Herschbach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dudley_Herschbach "Dudley Herschbach") of [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University "Harvard University") and [Yuan T. Lee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yuan_T._Lee "Yuan T. Lee") of the [University of California, Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Berkeley "University of California, Berkeley"). The trio were honoured for "their contributions concerning the dynamics of chemical elementary processes." Polanyi's contributions were centred around the work he did developing the technique of [infrared chemiluminescence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemiluminescence#Infrared_chemiluminescence "Chemiluminescence"). This technique was used to measure weak infrared emissions from a newly formed molecule in order to examine energy disposal during a chemical reaction. Polanyi's Nobel lecture upon receipt of the award was entitled "Some Concepts in Reaction Dynamics."

Polanyi had mixed feelings about the impact of the Nobel Prize on his research, feeling that his name on research proposals and papers often brought additional scrutiny, and also had people questioning his dedication to science after the honour. Polanyi said, "There is a very reasonable suspicion that you are so busy doing the things that Nobel Prize winners do that you are actually only giving half your mind to science."

His Nobel victory also signaled a change in his research direction. The 1986 [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics") was awarded to [Ernst Ruska](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernst_Ruska "Ernst Ruska"), [Gerd Binnig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerd_Binnig "Gerd Binnig") and [Heinrich Rohrer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heinrich_Rohrer "Heinrich Rohrer") for their work in [electron microscopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_microscopes "Electron microscopes") and [scanning tunnelling microscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scanning_tunnelling_microscopy "Scanning tunnelling microscopy") (STM). This research piqued Polanyi's interest while he was in Sweden for the ceremony. After returning to Toronto, Polanyi and his colleagues looked into the technique and now have four STMs, which they use to picture chemical reactions at the molecular level, rather than using infrared detection and chemiluminescence.

Polanyi's Nobel medal is on display at [Massey College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massey_College "Massey College") (University of Toronto) where he is also a Senior Fellow.

### Additional awards

He was elected a [Fellow of the Royal Society (FRS) in 1971](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Fellows_of_the_Royal_Society_elected_in_1971 "List of Fellows of the Royal Society elected in 1971"). In 1974, Polanyi was made an Officer of the [Order of Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_Canada "Order of Canada"). In 1979, he was promoted to Companion. He has received many other awards throughout his career, including the Marlow Medal of the [Faraday Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faraday_Society "Faraday Society") in 1962, Centenary Medal of the [British Chemical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Chemical_Society "British Chemical Society") in 1965, the [Steacie Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steacie_Prize "Steacie Prize") for Natural Sciences in 1965 (shared), the Noranda Award of the Chemical Institute of Canada in 1967, the [Henry Marshall Tory Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Marshall_Tory_Medal "Henry Marshall Tory Medal") of the [Royal Society of Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society_of_Canada "Royal Society of Canada") in 1977, the [Wolf Prize in Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolf_Prize_in_Chemistry "Wolf Prize in Chemistry") in 1982 (shared), the [Izaak Walton Killam Memorial Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Izaak_Walton_Killam_Memorial_Prize "Izaak Walton Killam Memorial Prize") in 1988, the [Royal Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Medal "Royal Medal") of the [Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society "Royal Society") in 1989, and the John C. Polanyi Lecture Award of the [Canadian Society for Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canadian_Society_for_Chemistry "Canadian Society for Chemistry") in 1992. In 2007, Polanyi was awarded the [Gerhard Herzberg Canada Gold Medal for Science and Engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerhard_Herzberg_Canada_Gold_Medal_for_Science_and_Engineering "Gerhard Herzberg Canada Gold Medal for Science and Engineering"). The [Royal Society of Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society_of_Chemistry "Royal Society of Chemistry") honoured Polanyi as their 2010 [Faraday Lectureship Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faraday_Lectureship_Prize "Faraday Lectureship Prize").

Polanyi has received many honorary degrees from over 30 institutions, including [Waterloo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Waterloo "University of Waterloo") in 1970, [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University "Harvard University") in 1982, [Ottawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Ottawa "University of Ottawa") in 1987, and [Queen's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen's_University_at_Kingston "Queen's University at Kingston") in 1992. He is a fellow of the [Royal Society of Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society_of_Canada "Royal Society of Canada"), the [Royal Society of London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society_of_London "Royal Society of London"), the [Royal Society of Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society_of_Edinburgh "Royal Society of Edinburgh"), and a member of the [American Academy of Arts and Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Academy_of_Arts_and_Sciences "American Academy of Arts and Sciences"), the [U.S. National Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._National_Academy_of_Sciences "U.S. National Academy of Sciences"), the [Pontifical Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pontifical_Academy_of_Sciences "Pontifical Academy of Sciences"), and an Honorary Fellow of the [Royal Society of Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society_of_Chemistry "Royal Society of Chemistry") of the United Kingdom and the [Chemical Institute of Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_Institute_of_Canada "Chemical Institute of Canada").

Polanyi was pictured on a [Canada Post](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canada_Post "Canada Post") first class postage stamp on 3 October 2011, issued to salute the [International Year of Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Year_of_Chemistry "International Year of Chemistry"). In 1992, Polanyi was appointed to the Queen's Privy Council of Canada.

Polanyi was awarded the 2022 Andrei Sakharov Prize. The award cites Polanyi's seven decades of activism for a nuclear-weapons-free world, for upholding human rights and freedom of speech globally, for public education on the essential role of science in society, and for a visionary approach to bringing about a hopeful, peaceful future.

### Legacy

In honour of Polanyi's Nobel Prize win, the Ontario government established the "John Charles Polanyi Prizes". These prizes are each worth $20,000, and are awarded to young researchers in the province in a postdoctoral fellowship or who have recently started a faculty appointment at an Ontario university. The prizes are awarded in similar categories to the Nobel Prizes, broadly defined as: [Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics "Physics"), [Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemistry "Chemistry"), [Physiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physiology "Physiology") or Medicine, Economics and Literature.

Canada's [Natural Sciences and Engineering Research Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_Sciences_and_Engineering_Research_Council "Natural Sciences and Engineering Research Council") (NSERC) created the John C. Polanyi award to recognize a researcher or researchers whose work in an NSERC-supported field has led to an outstanding advance in the field. The research must have been conducted in Canada, and have been at least partially supported by NSERC funding. The award consists partially of a $250,000 grant for the winner. The inaugural winner of the John C. Polanyi Award was the [Sudbury Neutrino Observatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sudbury_Neutrino_Observatory "Sudbury Neutrino Observatory"). In 2011, the award was presented to [Victoria M. Kaspi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victoria_M._Kaspi "Victoria M. Kaspi"), an [astrophysicist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrophysics "Astrophysics") at [McGill University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McGill_University "McGill University").

Polanyi started publishing his scientific research in 1953. As of 2010, he has published over 250 scientific papers. His writing is not limited to his scientific interests, as he has published over 100 articles on policy, the impact of science on society and armament control. In 1970, he produced a film entitled *Concepts in Reaction Dynamics*, and he co-edited a book called *The Dangers of Nuclear War*.

In 2010, the [Toronto District School Board](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toronto_District_School_Board "Toronto District School Board") voted to change the name of [Sir Sandford Fleming Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_Sandford_Fleming_Academy "Sir Sandford Fleming Academy") to the [John Polanyi Collegiate Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Polanyi_Collegiate_Institute "John Polanyi Collegiate Institute") to coincide with a move to a new location. The new school opened in September 2011.

## Personal life

Polanyi was born in 1929 to [Michael](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Polanyi "Michael Polanyi") and Magda Elizabeth Kemény Polanyi in [Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berlin "Berlin"), Germany. His father was born in 1891, in [Hungary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungary "Hungary"). His uncle [Karl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Polanyi "Karl Polanyi") was an economist, noted for his criticism of [market capitalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Market_capitalism "Market capitalism"). His brother George was noted for his defence of market capitalism. His paternal grandfather, Mihaly Pollacsek, built railways. Mihaly Pollacsek [Magyarised](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungarian_language "Hungarian language") the family's name to Polanyi, but did not change his own name. The Polanyi's were non-observant [Jews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Jews_in_Hungary "History of the Jews in Hungary"), although Michael Polanyi became a [Christian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian "Christian").

In 1958, Polanyi married Anne Ferrar Davidson (1929–2013). He has two children – a daughter, Margaret, born in 1961 and a son, Michael, born in 1963. His daughter is a journalist, and his son is a political scientist who started his career as a physicist. Polanyi is currently married to portrait artist [Brenda Bury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brenda_Bury "Brenda Bury"). Outside his scientific and policy endeavours, Polanyi's interests include art, literature and poetry. He was an avid white water canoeist in his younger days, but has replaced that with walking and skiing.
