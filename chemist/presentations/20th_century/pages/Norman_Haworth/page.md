**Sir Walter Norman Haworth** [FRS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow_of_the_Royal_Society "Fellow of the Royal Society") (19 March 1883 – 19 March 1950) was a British [chemist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemist "Chemist") best known for his groundbreaking work on [ascorbic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ascorbic_acid "Ascorbic acid") ([vitamin C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vitamin_C "Vitamin C")) while working at the [University of Birmingham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Birmingham "University of Birmingham"). He received the 1937 [Nobel Prize in Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Chemistry "Nobel Prize in Chemistry") "for his investigations on [carbohydrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbohydrate "Carbohydrate") and vitamin C". The prize was shared with Swiss chemist [Paul Karrer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Karrer "Paul Karrer") for his work on other [vitamins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vitamin "Vitamin").

Haworth worked out the correct structure of a number of sugars, and is known among organic chemists for his development of the [Haworth projection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haworth_projection "Haworth projection") that translates three-dimensional sugar structures into convenient two-dimensional graphical form.

## Academic career

Having worked for some time from the age of fourteen in the local Ryland's [linoleum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linoleum "Linoleum") factory managed by his father, he studied for and successfully passed the entrance examination to the [University of Manchester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Manchester "University of Manchester") in 1903 to study chemistry. He made this pursuit in spite of active discouragement by his parents. He gained his first-class honours degree in 1906. After gaining his master's degree under [William Henry Perkin Jr.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Henry_Perkin_Jr. "William Henry Perkin Jr."), he was awarded an [1851 Research Fellowship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1851_Research_Fellowship "1851 Research Fellowship") from the [Royal Commission for the Exhibition of 1851](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Commission_for_the_Exhibition_of_1851 "Royal Commission for the Exhibition of 1851") and studied at the [University of Göttingen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Göttingen "University of Göttingen") earning his PhD in [Otto Wallach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otto_Wallach "Otto Wallach")'s laboratory after only one year of study. A [DSc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DSc "DSc") from the University of Manchester followed in 1911, after which he served a short time at the Imperial College of Science and Technology as Senior Demonstrator in Chemistry.

In 1912 Haworth became a lecturer at United College of [University of St Andrews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_St_Andrews "University of St Andrews") in Scotland and became interested in [carbohydrate chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbohydrate_chemistry "Carbohydrate chemistry"), which was being investigated at St Andrews by Thomas Purdie (1843–1916) and [James Irvine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_James_Irvine "Sir James Irvine") (1877–1952). Haworth began his work on simple sugars in 1915 and developed a new method for the preparation of the methyl [ethers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ether "Ether") of sugars using [methyl sulfate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methyl_sulfate "Methyl sulfate") and alkali (now called [Haworth methylation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haworth_methylation "Haworth methylation")). He then began studies on the structural features of the [disaccharides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disaccharide "Disaccharide"). Haworth organised the laboratories at St Andrews University for the production of chemicals and drugs for the [British government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Her_Majesty's_Government "Her Majesty's Government") during [World War I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I "World War I") (1914–1918).

He was appointed Professor of Organic Chemistry at the Armstrong College ([Newcastle upon Tyne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newcastle_upon_Tyne "Newcastle upon Tyne")) of [Durham University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Durham_University "Durham University") in 1920. The next year Haworth was appointed Head of the Chemistry Department at the college. It was during his time in the North East of England that he married Violet Chilton Dobbie.

In 1925 he was appointed Mason Professor of Chemistry at the [University of Birmingham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Birmingham "University of Birmingham") (a position he held until 1948). Among his lasting contributions to science was the confirmation of a number of structures of optically active sugars: by 1928, he had deduced and confirmed, among others, the structures of [maltose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maltose "Maltose"), [cellobiose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cellobiose "Cellobiose"), [lactose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lactose "Lactose"), [gentiobiose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gentiobiose "Gentiobiose"), [melibiose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melibiose "Melibiose"), gentianose, [raffinose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raffinose "Raffinose"), as well as the [glucoside](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glucoside "Glucoside") ring tautomeric structure of aldose sugars. He published a classic text in 1929, *The Constitution of Sugars*.

In 1933, working with the then Assistant Director of Research (later Sir) [Edmund Hirst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmund_Hirst "Edmund Hirst") and a team led by post-doctoral student [Maurice Stacey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maurice_Stacey "Maurice Stacey") (who in 1956 rose to the same Mason Chair), having properly deduced the correct structure and optical-isomeric nature of vitamin C, Haworth reported the synthesis of the vitamin. Haworth had been given his initial reference sample of "water-soluble vitamin C" or "hexuronic acid" (the previous name for the compound as extracted from natural products) by Hungarian physiologist [Albert Szent-Györgyi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Szent-Györgyi "Szent-Györgyi"), who had codiscovered its vitamin properties along with [Charles Glen King](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Glen_King "Charles Glen King"), and had more recently discovered that it could be extracted in bulk from Hungarian [paprika](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paprika "Paprika"). In honour of the compound's antiscorbutic properties, Haworth and [Szent-Györgyi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Szent-Györgyi "Szent-Györgyi") now proposed the new name of "a-scorbic acid" for the molecule, with [L-ascorbic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L-ascorbic_acid "L-ascorbic acid") as its formal chemical name. During World War II, he was a member of the [MAUD Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MAUD_Committee "MAUD Committee") which oversaw research on the British atomic bomb project.

## Recognition

Haworth is commemorated at the [University of Birmingham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Birmingham "University of Birmingham") in the Haworth Building, which houses most of the University of Birmingham School of Chemistry. The School has a Haworth Chair of Chemistry, held by Professor Nigel Simpkins from 2007 until his retirement in 2017, and by Professor [Neil Champness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neil_Champness "Neil Champness") since 2021.

In 1977 the [Royal Mail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Mail "Royal Mail") issued a postage stamp (one of a series of four) featuring Haworth's achievement in synthesising vitamin C and his Nobel prize.

He also developed a simple method of representing on paper the three-dimensional structure of sugars. The representation, using perspective, now known as a [Haworth projection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haworth_projection "Haworth projection"), is still widely used in biochemistry.

## Personal life

In 1922 he married Violet Chilton Dobbie, daughter of Sir James Johnston Dobbie. They had two sons, James and David.

He was elected a [Fellow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow_of_the_Royal_Society "Fellow of the Royal Society") of the [Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society "Royal Society") (FRS) in 1928.

He was [knighted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knight_Bachelor "Knight Bachelor") in the [1947 New Years Honours list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1947_New_Year_Honours "1947 New Year Honours").

He died suddenly from a heart attack on 19 March 1950, his 67th birthday.
