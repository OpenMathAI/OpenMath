**Robert Huber** (German pronunciation: [[ˈʁoːbɛʁt ˈhuːbɐ]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Standard_German "Help:IPA/Standard German") [ⓘ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:De-Robert_Huber.ogg "File:De-Robert Huber.ogg"); born 20 February 1937) is a German [biochemist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biochemist "Biochemist") and [Nobel laureate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_laureate "Nobel laureate"). known for his work crystallizing an intra[membrane protein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Membrane_protein "Membrane protein") important in [photosynthesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photosynthesis "Photosynthesis") and subsequently applying X-ray crystallography to elucidate the protein's structure.

## Early life and education

He was born on 20 February 1937 in [Munich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Munich "Munich"), where his father, Sebastian, was a bank cashier. He was educated at the Humanistisches Karls-Gymnasium from 1947 to 1956 and then studied [chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemistry "Chemistry") at the [Technische Hochschule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technical_University_of_Munich "Technical University of Munich"), receiving his diploma in 1960. He stayed and did research into using [crystallography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystallography "Crystallography") to elucidate the structure of [organic compounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_compounds "Organic compounds").

## Career

In 1971, he became a director at the [Max Planck Institute for Biochemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Planck_Institute_for_Biochemistry "Max Planck Institute for Biochemistry"), where his team developed methods for the crystallography of [proteins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protein "Protein").

In 1988, he received the [Nobel Prize for Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_for_Chemistry "Nobel Prize for Chemistry") jointly with [Johann Deisenhofer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Deisenhofer "Johann Deisenhofer") and [Hartmut Michel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hartmut_Michel "Hartmut Michel"). The trio were recognized for their work in first crystallizing an intramembrane protein important in photosynthesis in [purple bacteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purple_bacteria "Purple bacteria"), and subsequently applying X-ray crystallography to elucidate the protein's structure. The information provided the first insight into the structural bodies that performed the integral function of photosynthesis. This insight could be translated to understand the more complex analogue of photosynthesis in [cyanobacteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyanobacteria "Cyanobacteria") which is essentially the same as that in [chloroplasts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloroplasts "Chloroplasts") of higher plants.

In 2006, he took up a post at the [Cardiff University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_University "Cardiff University") to spearhead the development of Structural Biology at the university on a part-time basis.

Since 2005, he has been doing research at the Centre for Medical Biotechnology of the [University of Duisburg-Essen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Duisburg-Essen "University of Duisburg-Essen").

Huber was one of the original editors of the [Encyclopedia of Analytical Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encyclopedia_of_Analytical_Chemistry "Encyclopedia of Analytical Chemistry").

## Awards and honours

In 1977 Huber was awarded the [Otto Warburg Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otto_Warburg_Medal "Otto Warburg Medal"). In 1988 he was awarded the Nobel Prize and in 1992 the [Sir Hans Krebs Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_Hans_Krebs_Medal "Sir Hans Krebs Medal"). Huber was elected a member of [Pour le Mérite for Sciences and Arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pour_le_Mérite_for_Sciences_and_Arts "Pour le Mérite for Sciences and Arts"), in 1993 and [Foreign Member of the Royal Society (ForMemRS) in 1999](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Fellows_of_the_Royal_Society_elected_in_1999 "List of Fellows of the Royal Society elected in 1999"). His certificate of election reads:

> Huber has built up, led and still leads the most productive protein crystallography laboratory in Europe. His own contributions to crystallography, made over a period of some 25 years, are prodigious. For his PhD thesis he solved the chemical formula of the important insect hormone edtyson which had eluded the chemists. He then demonstrated that the tertiary fold of the polypeptide chain in the haemoglobin of the fly [larva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Larva "Larva") [chironomus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chironomus "Chironomus") closely resembled that in Kendrew's sperm whale [myoglobin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myoglobin "Myoglobin"), indicating for the first time that this fold had been preserved throughout evolution.
>
> Huber's next achievement was the solution of the structure of [trypsin inhibitor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trypsin_inhibitor "Trypsin inhibitor") and the demonstration that in its complex with trypsin it mimicked the tetrahedral transition state of the enzyme's substrate. Since then he has determined the structures of many other proteinases, their inactive precursors and their inhibitors, and has established himself as the world authority in this field. Outstanding structures are those of [procarboxypeptidase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carboxypeptidase "Carboxypeptidase"), which led to the discovery of the remarkable activation mechanism of this enzyme, and of the complex of [thrombin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thrombin "Thrombin") with [hirudin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hirudin "Hirudin"), which showed the molecular mechanism of inhibition of blood clotting by this leech toxin.
>
> In parallel with this work, Huber solved the structures of several [immunoglobulin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immunoglobulin "Immunoglobulin") fragments. He was the first to determine the structure of the complement-activating F-fragment, which was also the first variable and the first constant domains in Fab-fragments.
>
> Huber's structure of [citrate synthase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citrate_synthase "Citrate synthase") revealed a striking example of a conformational change undergone by an enzyme on combination with its substrate by a process of induced fit.
> Huber shared the Nobel Prize for Chemistry in 1988 with Michel and Deisenhofer for their determination of the remarkable and supremely important structures of the photochemical reaction centre of Rhodopseudomonas viridis and of [phycocyanin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phycocyanin "Phycocyanin"), the light harvesting protein of the [blue-green alga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyanobacteria "Cyanobacteria") Mastiglocadus laminosus. This protein binds linear [tetrapyrroles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetrapyrrole "Tetrapyrrole") in a [tertiary fold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protein_tertiary_structure "Protein tertiary structure") reminiscent of the [globins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Globin "Globin"), which brought Huber back full circle to his first structure, [erythrocruerin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erythrocruorin "Erythrocruorin"),
> Huber has also determined the structures of several copper-containing [electron-transfer proteins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_transport_chain "Electron transport chain"), including that of [ascorbate oxidase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L-ascorbate_oxidase "L-ascorbate oxidase"), and of other metallo-enzymes. These studies have thrown new light on electron-transfer systems and on zinc coordination in proteins. He has also solved the structure of an important class of calcium binding proteins – the annexins. Finally his very accurate structures have provided important insights into the different degrees of mobility within protein molecules.
>
> Huber has published some 400 papers.
