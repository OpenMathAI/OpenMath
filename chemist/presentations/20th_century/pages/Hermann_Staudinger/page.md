**Hermann Staudinger** (German: [[ˈhɛʁman ˈʃtaʊ̯dɪŋɐ]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Standard_German "Help:IPA/Standard German") [ⓘ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:De-Hermann_Staudinger.ogg "File:De-Hermann Staudinger.ogg"); 23 March 1881 – 8 September 1965) was a German [organic chemist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_chemist "Organic chemist") who demonstrated the existence of [macromolecules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macromolecule "Macromolecule"), which he characterized as [polymers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polymer "Polymer"). For this work he received the 1953 [Nobel Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize "Nobel Prize") in Chemistry.

He is also known for his discovery of [ketenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ketene "Ketene") and of the [Staudinger reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Staudinger_reaction "Staudinger reaction"). Staudinger, together with [Leopold Ružička](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leopold_Ružička "Leopold Ružička"), also elucidated the molecular structures of [pyrethrin I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyrethrin_I "Pyrethrin I") and II in the 1920s, enabling the development of [pyrethroid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyrethroid "Pyrethroid") [insecticides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insecticides "Insecticides") in the 1960s and 1970s.

## Early work

Staudinger was born in 1881 in [Worms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worms,_Germany "Worms, Germany"). Staudinger, who initially wanted to become a botanist, studied chemistry at the [University of Halle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Luther_University_of_Halle-Wittenberg "Martin Luther University of Halle-Wittenberg"), at [Technische Hochschule Darmstadt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technische_Hochschule_Darmstadt "Technische Hochschule Darmstadt") and at the [Ludwig-Maximilians-Universität München](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludwig-Maximilians-Universität_München "Ludwig-Maximilians-Universität München"). He received his "Verbandsexamen" (comparable to master's degree) from the Technische Hochschule Darmstadt. After receiving his Ph.D. from the [University of Halle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Halle "University of Halle") in 1903, Staudinger qualified as an academic lecturer at the [University of Strasbourg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Strasbourg "University of Strasbourg") in 1907. He was supported in his work by his new wife [Dora Staudinger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dora_Staudinger "Dora Staudinger") who wrote up his lectures.

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/69/Ketenes.png/120px-Ketenes.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Ketenes.png)

**Figure 1.** The general structure of a ketene. R is any group.

It was here that he discovered the [ketenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ketene "Ketene"), a family of molecules characterized by the general form depicted in **Figure 1**. Ketenes would prove a synthetically important intermediate for the production of yet-to-be-discovered antibiotics such as [penicillin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penicillin "Penicillin") and [amoxicillin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amoxicillin "Amoxicillin").

In 1907, Staudinger began an assistant professorship at the [Technical University of Karlsruhe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Karlsruhe "University of Karlsruhe"). Here, he successfully isolated a number of useful organic compounds (including a synthetic coffee flavoring) as more completely reviewed by Rolf Mülhaupt. Here too he guided future Nobel laureates [Leopold Ružička](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leopold_Ružička "Leopold Ružička") (1910) and [Tadeusz Reichstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tadeusz_Reichstein "Tadeusz Reichstein") to their doctorates.

## The Staudinger reaction

In 1912, Staudinger took on a new position at the [Swiss Federal Institute of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ETH_Zurich "ETH Zurich") in [Zurich, Switzerland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zurich,_Switzerland "Zurich, Switzerland"). One of his earliest discoveries came in 1919, when he and colleague Meyer reported that [organic azides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_azide "Organic azide") react with [triphenylphosphine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triphenylphosphine "Triphenylphosphine") to form an [iminophosphorane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iminophosphorane "Iminophosphorane") (**Figure 2**). This reaction, commonly referred to as the [Staudinger reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Staudinger_reaction "Staudinger reaction"), typically produces a high yield of the iminophosphorane.

## World War I

While in autumn 1914 German professors joined the widespread public support of the war, Staudinger refused to sign [Manifesto of the Ninety-Three](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manifesto_of_the_Ninety-Three "Manifesto of the Ninety-Three") and joined the few exceptions like [Max Born](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Born "Max Born"), [Otto Buek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otto_Buek "Otto Buek") and [Albert Einstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Einstein "Albert Einstein") in condemning it. In 1917 he authored an essay predicting the defeat of Germany due to industrial superiority of [the *Entente*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allies_of_World_War_I "Allies of World War I") and called for a peaceful settlement as soon as possible, and after the [entrance of the US](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_in_World_War_I "United States in World War I") he repeated the call in a long letter to the German military leadership. [Fritz Haber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fritz_Haber "Fritz Haber") attacked him for his essay, accusing him of harming Germany, and Staudinger in turn criticized Haber for his role in the German chemical weapons program.

## Polymer chemistry

While at Karlsruhe and later, Zurich, Staudinger began research in the chemistry of [rubber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rubber "Rubber"), for which very high [molecular weights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_mass "Molecular mass") had been measured by the physical methods of [Raoult](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/François-Marie_Raoult "François-Marie Raoult") and [van 't Hoff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobus_Henricus_van_'t_Hoff "Jacobus Henricus van 't Hoff"). Contrary to prevailing ideas (see below), Staudinger proposed in a landmark paper published in 1920 that [rubber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rubber "Rubber") and other [polymers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polymer "Polymer") such as [starch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starch "Starch"), [cellulose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cellulose "Cellulose") and [proteins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proteins "Proteins") are long chains of short repeating molecular units linked by [covalent bonds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covalent_bonds "Covalent bonds"). In other words, polymers are like chains of paper clips, made up of small constituent parts linked from end to end (**Figure 3**).

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/86/Clipspla.jpg/250px-Clipspla.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Clipspla.jpg)

**Figure 3.** A chain of paper clips *(above)* is a good model for a polymer such as [polylactic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polylactic_acid "Polylactic acid") *(below)*. The polymer chain is composed of small pieces linked together in a head-to-tail fashion.

At the time, leading organic chemists such as [Emil Fischer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann_Emil_Fischer "Hermann Emil Fischer") and [Heinrich Wieland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heinrich_Wieland "Heinrich Wieland") believed that the measured high molecular weights were only apparent values caused by the aggregation of small molecules into [colloids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colloids "Colloids"). At first, the majority of Staudinger's colleagues refused to accept the possibility that small molecules could link together covalently to form high-molecular weight compounds. As Mülhaupt aptly notes, this is due in part to the fact that molecular structure and bonding theory were not fully understood in the early 20th century.

In 1926, he was appointed lecturer of chemistry at the [University of Freiburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Freiburg "University of Freiburg") at Freiburg im Breisgau (Germany), where he spent the rest of his career. Further evidence to support his polymer hypothesis emerged in the 1930s. High molecular weights of polymers were confirmed by [membrane osmometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osmotic_pressure "Osmotic pressure"), and also by Staudinger's measurements of [viscosity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viscosity "Viscosity") in solution. The [X-ray diffraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray_diffraction "X-ray diffraction") studies of polymers by [Herman Mark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herman_Mark "Herman Mark") provided direct evidence for long chains of repeating molecular units. And the synthetic work led by [Carothers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wallace_Carothers "Wallace Carothers") demonstrated that polymers such as [nylon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nylon "Nylon") and [polyester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyester "Polyester") could be prepared by well-understood organic reactions. His theory opened up the subject to further development, and helped place polymer science on a sound basis.

## Private life

He married in 1906 to Dora Förster and they remained together until their divorce in 1926. They had four children including Eva Lezzi (1907–1993) and Klar (Klara) Kaufmann who were active in resisting the rise of fascism. Dora married again and became a leading peace activist.

In 1927, he married the Latvian botanist, [Magda Voita](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magda_Staudinger "Magda Staudinger") (also shown as; [German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_language "German language"): *Magda Woit*), who was a collaborator with him until his death and whose contributions he acknowledged in his Nobel Prize acceptance.

In 1935 Staudinger became a [Patron Member](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Förderndes_Mitglied_der_SS "Förderndes Mitglied der SS") of the [SS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schutzstaffel "Schutzstaffel").

## Legacy

Staudinger's groundbreaking elucidation of the nature of the high-molecular weight compounds he termed *Makromoleküle* paved the way for the birth of the field of polymer chemistry. Staudinger himself saw the potential for this science long before it was fully realized. "It is not improbable," Staudinger commented in 1936, "that sooner or later a way will be discovered to prepare artificial fibers from synthetic high-molecular products, because the strength and elasticity of natural fibers depend exclusively on their macro-molecular structure – i.e., on their long thread-shaped molecules." Staudinger founded the first polymer chemistry journal in 1940, and in 1953 received the [Nobel Prize in Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Chemistry "Nobel Prize in Chemistry") for "his discoveries in the field of macromolecular chemistry." In 1999, the [American Chemical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Chemical_Society "American Chemical Society") and the [German Chemical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Chemical_Society "German Chemical Society") designated Staudinger's work as an [International Historic Chemical Landmark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Historic_Chemical_Landmarks "National Historic Chemical Landmarks"). His pioneering research has afforded the world myriad plastics, textiles, and other polymeric materials which make consumer products more affordable, attractive and enjoyable, while helping engineers develop lighter and more durable structures. The [German Chemical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Chemical_Society "German Chemical Society") started the [Hermann Staudinger Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann_Staudinger_Prize "Hermann Staudinger Prize") in 1971 to recognize fundamental contributions in [polymer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polymer_science "Polymer science").
