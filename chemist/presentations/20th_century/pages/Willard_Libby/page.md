**Willard Frank Libby** (December 17, 1908 – September 8, 1980) was an American [physical chemist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_chemistry "Physical chemistry") noted for his role in the 1949 development of [radiocarbon dating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiocarbon_dating "Radiocarbon dating"), a process which revolutionized [archaeology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaeology "Archaeology") and [palaeontology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palaeontology "Palaeontology"). For his contributions to the team that developed this process, Libby was awarded the [Nobel Prize in Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Chemistry "Nobel Prize in Chemistry") in 1960.

A 1931 chemistry graduate of the [University of California, Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Berkeley "University of California, Berkeley"), from which he received his doctorate in 1933, he studied radioactive elements and developed sensitive [Geiger counters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geiger_counter "Geiger counter") to measure weak natural and artificial radioactivity. During [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II") he worked in the [Manhattan Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manhattan_Project "Manhattan Project")'s Substitute Alloy Materials (SAM) Laboratories at [Columbia University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia_University "Columbia University"), developing the [gaseous diffusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaseous_diffusion "Gaseous diffusion") process for [uranium enrichment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium_enrichment "Uranium enrichment").

After the war, Libby accepted a professorship at the [University of Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Chicago "University of Chicago")'s [Institute for Nuclear Studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_for_Nuclear_Studies "Institute for Nuclear Studies"), where he developed the technique for dating organic compounds using [carbon-14](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon-14 "Carbon-14"). He also discovered that [tritium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tritium "Tritium") similarly could be used for dating water, and therefore wine. In 1950, he became a member of the General Advisory Committee (GAC) of the [Atomic Energy Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Atomic_Energy_Commission "United States Atomic Energy Commission") (AEC). He was appointed a commissioner in 1954, becoming its sole scientist. He sided with [Edward Teller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Teller "Edward Teller") on pursuing a crash program to develop the [hydrogen bomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_bomb "Hydrogen bomb"), participated in the [Atoms for Peace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atoms_for_Peace "Atoms for Peace") program, and defended the administration's atmospheric [nuclear testing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_testing "Nuclear testing").

Libby resigned from the AEC in 1959 to become professor of chemistry at [University of California, Los Angeles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Los_Angeles "University of California, Los Angeles") (UCLA), a position he held until his retirement in 1976. In 1962, he became the director of the [University of California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California "University of California") statewide Institute of [Geophysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geophysics "Geophysics") and Planetary Physics (IGPP). He started the first Environmental Engineering program at UCLA in 1972, and as a member of the [California Air Resources Board](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California_Air_Resources_Board "California Air Resources Board"), he worked to develop and improve California's air pollution standards.

## Early life and career

Willard Frank Libby was born in Parachute, Colorado, on December 17, 1908, the son of farmers Ora Edward Libby and his wife Eva May (née Rivers). He had two brothers, Elmer and Raymond, and two sisters, Eva and Evelyn. Libby began his education in a two-room Colorado schoolhouse. When he was five, Libby's parents moved to [Santa Rosa, California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santa_Rosa,_California "Santa Rosa, California"). He attended [Analy High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analy_High_School "Analy High School"), in [Sebastopol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sebastopol,_California "Sebastopol, California"), from which he graduated in 1926. Libby, who grew to be 6 feet 2 inches (188 cm) tall, played [tackle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tackle_(gridiron_football_position) "Tackle (gridiron football position)") on the high school [football](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gridiron_football "Gridiron football") team.

In 1927 he entered the University of California, Berkeley, where he received his [BS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_Science "Bachelor of Science") in 1931, and his [PhD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PhD "PhD") in 1933, writing his doctoral thesis on the "Radioactivity of ordinary elements, especially samarium and neodymium: method of detection" under the supervision of [Wendell Mitchell Latimer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wendell_Mitchell_Latimer "Wendell Mitchell Latimer"). Independently of the work of [George de Hevesy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_de_Hevesy "George de Hevesy") and Max Pahl, he discovered that the natural long-lived isotopes of [samarium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samarium "Samarium") primarily decay by emission of [alpha particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpha_particle "Alpha particle").

Libby was appointed Instructor in the department of chemistry at the University of California, Berkeley, in 1933. He became an [assistant professor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assistant_professor "Assistant professor") of chemistry there in 1938. He spent the 1930s building sensitive [Geiger counters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geiger_counter "Geiger counter") to measure weak natural and artificial radioactivity. He joined Berkeley's chapter of [Alpha Chi Sigma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpha_Chi_Sigma "Alpha Chi Sigma") in 1941. That year he was awarded a [Guggenheim Fellowship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guggenheim_Fellowship "Guggenheim Fellowship"), and elected to work at [Princeton University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University "Princeton University").

## Manhattan Project

On December 8, 1941, the day after the [Japanese attack on Pearl Harbor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_attack_on_Pearl_Harbor "Japanese attack on Pearl Harbor") brought the United States into [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II"), Libby volunteered his services to Nobel Prize laureate [Harold Urey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Urey "Harold Urey"). Urey arranged for Libby to be given leave from the University of California, Berkeley and to join him at [Columbia University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia_University "Columbia University") to work on the [Manhattan Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manhattan_Project "Manhattan Project"), the wartime project to develop [atomic bombs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_bomb "Atomic bomb"), at what became its Substitute Alloy Materials (SAM) Laboratories. During his time in the New York City area, Libby was a resident of [Leonia, New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonia,_New_Jersey "Leonia, New Jersey").

Over the next three years, Libby worked on the [gaseous diffusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaseous_diffusion "Gaseous diffusion") process for [uranium enrichment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium_enrichment "Uranium enrichment"). An atomic bomb required [fissile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fissile "Fissile") material, and the fissile [uranium-235](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium-235 "Uranium-235") made up only 0.7 percent of natural uranium. The SAM Laboratories therefore had to find a way of separating kilograms of it from the more abundant [uranium-238](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium-238 "Uranium-238"). Gaseous diffusion worked on the principle that a lighter gas diffuses through a barrier faster than a heavier one at a rate inversely proportional to its molecular weight. But the only known gas containing uranium was the highly corrosive [uranium hexafluoride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium_hexafluoride "Uranium hexafluoride"), and a suitable barrier was hard to find.

Through 1942, Libby and his team studied different barriers and the means to protect them from corrosion from the uranium hexafluoride. The most promising type was a barrier made of powdered [nickel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nickel "Nickel") developed by Edward O. Norris of the Jelliff Manufacturing Corporation and Edward Adler from the [City College of New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_College_of_New_York "City College of New York"), which became known as the "Norris-Adler" barrier by late 1942.

In addition to developing a suitable barrier, the SAM Laboratories also had to assist in the design of a gaseous separation plant, which became known as [K-25](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K-25 "K-25"). Libby helped with the engineers from [Kellex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kellex "Kellex") to produce a workable design for a pilot plant. Libby conducted a series of tests that indicated that the Norris-Adler barrier would work, and he remained confident that with an all-out effort, the remaining problems with it could be solved. Although doubts remained, construction work began on the K-25 full-scale production plant in September 1943.

As 1943 gave way to 1944, many problems remained. Tests began on the machinery at K-25 in April 1944 without a barrier. Attention turned to a new process developed by Kellex. Finally, in July 1944, Kellex barriers began to be installed in K-25. K-25 commenced operation in February 1945, and as cascade after cascade came online, the quality of the product increased. By April 1945, K-25 had attained a 1.1% enrichment. Uranium partially enriched in K-25 was fed into the [calutrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calutron "Calutron") at [Y-12](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Y-12_National_Security_Complex "Y-12 National Security Complex") to complete the enrichment process.

Construction of the upper stages of the K-25 plant was cancelled, and Kellex was directed to instead design and build a 540-stage side feed unit, which became known as K-27. The last of K-25's 2,892 stages commenced operation in August 1945. On August 5, K-25 starting producing feed enriched to 23 percent uranium-235. K-25 and K-27 achieved their full potential only in the early postwar period, when they eclipsed the other production plants and became the prototypes for a new generation of plants. Enriched uranium was used in the [Little Boy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_Boy "Little Boy") bomb employed in the [bombing of Hiroshima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bombing_of_Hiroshima "Bombing of Hiroshima") on August 6, 1945. Libby brought home a stack of newspapers and told his wife, "This is what I've been doing."

## Radiocarbon dating

After the war, Libby accepted an offer from the [University of Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Chicago "University of Chicago") of a professorship in the chemistry department at the new [Institute for Nuclear Studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_for_Nuclear_Studies "Institute for Nuclear Studies"). He returned to his pre-war studies of radioactivity. In 1939, [Serge Korff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serge_Korff "Serge Korff") had discovered that [cosmic rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmic_rays "Cosmic rays") generated [neutrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron "Neutron") in the upper atmosphere. These interact with [nitrogen-14](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrogen-14 "Nitrogen-14") in the air to produce [carbon-14](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon-14 "Carbon-14"):

:   1n + 14N → 14C + 1p

The [half-life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half-life "Half-life") of carbon-14 is 5,730±40 years. Libby realized that when plants and animals die they cease to ingest fresh carbon-14, thereby giving any organic compound a built-in nuclear clock. He published his theory in 1946, and expanded on it in his monograph *Radiocarbon Dating* in 1955. He also developed sensitive radiation detectors that could make the measurements required by the technique. Tests against [sequoia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sequoia_(genus) "Sequoia (genus)") with known dates from their tree rings showed [radiocarbon dating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiocarbon_dating "Radiocarbon dating") to be reliable and accurate. The technique revolutionised [archaeology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaeology "Archaeology"), [palaeontology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palaeontology "Palaeontology") and other disciplines that dealt with ancient artefacts. In 1960, he was awarded the [Nobel Prize in Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Chemistry "Nobel Prize in Chemistry") "for his method to use carbon-14 for age determination in archaeology, geology, geophysics, and other branches of science". He also discovered that [tritium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tritium "Tritium") similarly could be used for dating water, and therefore wine.

## Atomic Energy Commission

[Atomic Energy Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Atomic_Energy_Commission "United States Atomic Energy Commission") (AEC) Chairman [Gordon Dean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gordon_Dean_(lawyer) "Gordon Dean (lawyer)") appointed Libby to its influential General Advisory Committee (GAC) in 1950. In 1954, he was appointed an AEC commissioner by [President](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_of_the_United_States "President of the United States") [Dwight D. Eisenhower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dwight_D._Eisenhower "Dwight D. Eisenhower") on the recommendation of Dean's successor, [Lewis Strauss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lewis_Strauss "Lewis Strauss"). Libby and his family moved from Chicago to [Washington, D.C.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington,_D.C. "Washington, D.C.") He brought with him a truckload of scientific equipment, which he used to establish a laboratory at the [Carnegie Institution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carnegie_Institution_for_Science "Carnegie Institution for Science") there to continue his studies of [amino acids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amino_acid "Amino acid"). Staunchly conservative politically, he was one of the few scientists who sided with [Edward Teller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Teller "Edward Teller") rather than [Robert Oppenheimer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Oppenheimer "Robert Oppenheimer") during the debate on whether it was wise to pursue a crash program to develop the [hydrogen bomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_bomb "Hydrogen bomb"). As a commissioner, Libby played an important role in promoting Eisenhower's [Atoms for Peace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atoms_for_Peace "Atoms for Peace") program, and was part of the United States delegation at the Geneva Conferences on Peaceful Uses of Atomic Energy in 1955 and 1958.

As the only scientist among the five AEC commissioners, it fell to Libby to defend the Eisenhower administration's stance on atmospheric [nuclear testing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_testing "Nuclear testing"). He argued that the dangers of radiation from nuclear tests were less than that from chest X-rays, and therefore less important than the risk of having an inadequate nuclear arsenal, but his arguments failed to convince the scientific community or reassure the public. In January 1956, he publicly revealed the existence of [Project Sunshine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Project_SUNSHINE "Project SUNSHINE"), a series of secret research studies to ascertain the impact of radioactive fallout on the world's population that he had initiated in 1953 while serving on the GAC. The project caused controversy after it was revealed to the public and with the revelation it was found out that much of the research involved stealing the bodies of dead children without the parents' consent and doing radioactive experiments on them. Many of the 1,500 sample cadavers were babies and young children, and were taken from countries from Australia to Europe, often without their parents' consent or knowledge. By 1958, even Libby and Teller were supporting limits on atmospheric nuclear testing.

## UCLA

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/2d/Willard_Libby_in_Lab.jpg/250px-Willard_Libby_in_Lab.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Willard_Libby_in_Lab.jpg)

Libby in the lab, c. 1960s

Libby resigned from the AEC in 1959, and he became professor of chemistry at University of California, Los Angeles, a position he held until his retirement in 1976. He taught honors freshman chemistry. In 1962, he became the director of the University of California statewide Institute of [Geophysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geophysics "Geophysics") and Planetary Physics (IGPP), a position he also held until 1976. His time as director encompassed the [Apollo space program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_space_program "Apollo space program") and the lunar landings.

Libby started the first Environmental Engineering program at UCLA in 1972. As a member of the [California Air Resources Board](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California_Air_Resources_Board "California Air Resources Board"), he worked to develop and improve California's air pollution standards. He established a research program to investigate [heterogeneous catalysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heterogeneous_catalysis "Heterogeneous catalysis") with the idea of reducing emissions from motor vehicles through more complete fuel combustion. The election of [Richard Nixon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Nixon "Richard Nixon") as president in 1968 generated speculation that Libby might be appointed as Presidential Science Advisor. There was a storm of protest from scientists who felt that Libby was too conservative, and the offer was not made.

Although Libby retired and became a [professor emeritus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Professor_emeritus "Professor emeritus") in 1976, he remained professionally active until his death in 1980.

## Awards and honors

Libby was an elected member of the [National Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Academy_of_Sciences "National Academy of Sciences"), the [American Academy of Arts and Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Academy_of_Arts_and_Sciences "American Academy of Arts and Sciences"), and the [American Philosophical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Philosophical_Society "American Philosophical Society"). In addition to the Nobel Prize, he received numerous honors and awards, including Columbia University's Chandler Medal in 1954, the Remsen Memorial Lecture Award in 1955, the Bicentennial Lecture Award from the [City College of New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_College_of_New_York "City College of New York") and the Nuclear Applications in Chemistry Award in 1956, the [Franklin Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franklin_Institute "Franklin Institute")'s [Elliott Cresson Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliott_Cresson_Medal "Elliott Cresson Medal") in 1957, the [American Chemical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Chemical_Society "American Chemical Society")'s [Willard Gibbs Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Willard_Gibbs_Award "Willard Gibbs Award") in 1958, the [Joseph Priestley Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Priestley_Award?action=edit&redlink=1 "Joseph Priestley Award") from [Dickinson College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dickinson_College "Dickinson College") and the [Albert Einstein Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Einstein_Medal "Albert Einstein Medal") in 1959, the [Geological Society of America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geological_Society_of_America "Geological Society of America")'s [Arthur L. Day Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_L._Day_Medal "Arthur L. Day Medal") in 1961, the Golden Plate Award of the [American Academy of Achievement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academy_of_Achievement "Academy of Achievement") in 1961, the [Gold Medal of the American Institute of Chemists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold_Medal_of_the_American_Institute_of_Chemists "Gold Medal of the American Institute of Chemists") in 1970, and the Lehman Award from the [New York Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_Academy_of_Sciences "New York Academy of Sciences") in 1971. He was elected a member of the [National Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Academy_of_Sciences "National Academy of Sciences") in 1950. [Analy High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analy_High_School "Analy High School") library has a mural of Libby, and a Sebastopol city park and a nearby highway are named in his honor. His 1947 paper on radiocarbon dating was honored by a Citation for Chemical Breakthrough Award from the Division of History of Chemistry of the American Chemical Society presented to the University of Chicago in 2016.

## Personal life

In 1940, Libby married Leonor Hickey, a [physical education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_education "Physical education") teacher. They had twin daughters, Janet Eva and Susan Charlotte, who were born in 1945.

In 1966 Libby divorced Leonor and married [Leona Woods Marshall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leona_Woods "Leona Woods"), a distinguished [nuclear physicist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_physicist "Nuclear physicist") who was one of the original builders of [Chicago Pile-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicago_Pile-1 "Chicago Pile-1"), the world's first [nuclear reactor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_reactor "Nuclear reactor"). She joined him at UCLA as a professor of [environmental engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_engineering "Environmental engineering") in 1973. Through this second marriage he acquired two stepsons, the children of her first marriage.

Libby died at the [UCLA Medical Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UCLA_Medical_Center "UCLA Medical Center") in [Los Angeles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Los_Angeles "Los Angeles") on September 8, 1980, from a blood clot in his lung complicated by [pneumonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pneumonia "Pneumonia"). His papers are in the [Charles E. Young Research Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_E._Young_Research_Library "Charles E. Young Research Library") at UCLA. Seven volumes of his papers were edited by Leona and Rainer Berger and published in 1981.
