**Alfred Werner** (12 December 1866 – 15 November 1919) was a Swiss chemist who was a student at [ETH Zurich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ETH_Zurich "ETH Zurich") and a professor at the [University of Zurich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Zurich "University of Zurich"). He won the [Nobel Prize in Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Chemistry "Nobel Prize in Chemistry") in 1913 for proposing the [octahedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octahedral_molecular_geometry "Octahedral molecular geometry") configuration of [transition metal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transition_metal "Transition metal") complexes. Werner developed the basis for modern [coordination chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordination_chemistry "Coordination chemistry"). He was the first [inorganic chemist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inorganic_chemistry "Inorganic chemistry") to win the Nobel Prize, and the only one prior to 1973.

## Biography

Werner was born in 1866 in [Mulhouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mulhouse "Mulhouse"), [Alsace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alsace "Alsace") (which was then part of France, but which was annexed by Germany in 1871). He was raised as Roman Catholic. He was the fourth and last child of Jean-Adam Werner, a foundry worker, and his second wife, Salomé Jeannette Werner, who originated from a wealthy family. He went to Switzerland to study chemistry at the [Swiss Federal Institute (polytechnikum) in Zurich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ETH_Zurich "ETH Zurich"). Still, since this institute was not empowered to grant doctorates until 1909, Werner received a doctorate formally from the University of Zürich in 1890. After postdoctoral study in Paris, he returned to the Swiss Federal Institute to teach (1892). In 1893 he moved to the [University of Zurich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Zurich "University of Zurich"), where he became a professor in 1895. In 1894 he became a Swiss citizen.

In his last year, he suffered from a general, progressive, degenerative [arteriosclerosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arteriosclerosis "Arteriosclerosis"), especially of the brain, aggravated by years of excessive drinking and overwork. He died in a psychiatric hospital in Zürich on 15 November 1919 of arteriosclerosis at the age of 52.

## Research

### Coordination chemistry

In 1893, Werner was the first to propose correct structures for coordination compounds containing [complex ions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_(chemistry) "Complex (chemistry)"), in which a central transition metal atom is surrounded by neutral or anionic [ligands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ligands "Ligands").

For example, it was known that cobalt forms a "complex" hexamine cobalt (III) chloride, with formula CoCl3•6NH3, but the nature of the association indicated by the dot was mysterious. Werner proposed the structure [[Co(NH3)6]Cl3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cobalt(III)_hexammine_chloride "Cobalt(III) hexammine chloride"), with the Co3+ ion surrounded by six NH3 at the vertices of an octahedron. The three Cl− are dissociated as free ions, which Werner confirmed by measuring the [conductivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conductivity_(electrolytic) "Conductivity (electrolytic)") of the compound in an aqueous solution, and also by chloride anion analysis using precipitation with [silver nitrate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silver_nitrate "Silver nitrate"). Later, [magnetic susceptibility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_susceptibility "Magnetic susceptibility") analysis was also used to confirm Werner's proposal for the chemical nature of CoCl3•6NH3.

*trans*-[Co(NH3)4 Cl2]+

For complexes with more than one type of ligand, Werner succeeded in explaining the number of [isomers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomers "Isomers") observed. For example, he explained the existence of two tetramine isomers, "Co(NH3)4Cl3", one green and one purple. Werner proposed that these are two [geometric isomers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octahedral_molecular_geometry#Isomerism "Octahedral molecular geometry") of formula [Co(NH3)4Cl2]Cl, with one Cl− ion dissociated as confirmed by conductivity measurements. The Co atom is surrounded by four NH3 and two Cl ligands at the vertices of an octahedron. The green isomer is "trans" with the two Cl ligands at opposite vertices, and the purple is "cis" with the two Cl at adjacent vertices.

Werner also prepared complexes with [optical isomers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_isomers "Optical isomers"), and in 1914 he reported the first synthetic [chiral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chirality_(chemistry) "Chirality (chemistry)") compound lacking carbon, known as [hexol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hexol "Hexol") with formula [Co(Co(NH3)4(OH)2)3]Br6.

### Nature of valence

Before Werner, chemists defined the [valence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valence_(chemistry) "Valence (chemistry)") of an element as the number of its bonds without distinguishing different types of bonds. However, in complexes such as [Co(NH3)6]Cl3 for example, Werner considered that the Co-Cl bonds correspond to a "primary" valence of 3 at long distance, while the Co-NH3 bonds which correspond to a "secondary" or weaker valence of 6 at shorter length. This secondary valence of 6 he referred to as the [coordination number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordination_number "Coordination number") which he defined as the number of molecules (here of NH3) directly linked to the central metal atom. In other complexes, he found coordination numbers of 4 or 8.

On these views, and other similar views, in 1904 [Richard Abegg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Abegg "Richard Abegg") formulated what is now known as [Abegg's rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abegg's_rule "Abegg's rule") which states that the difference between the maximum positive and negative [valence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valence_(chemistry) "Valence (chemistry)") of an [element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_element "Chemical element") is frequently eight. This rule was used later in 1916 when [Gilbert N. Lewis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gilbert_N._Lewis "Gilbert N. Lewis") formulated the "[octet rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octet_rule "Octet rule")" in his [cubical atom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cubical_atom "Cubical atom") theory.

In modern terminology, Werner's primary valence corresponds to the [oxidation state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxidation_state "Oxidation state"), and his secondary valence is called [coordination number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordination_number "Coordination number"). The Co-Cl bonds (in the above example) are now classed as ionic, and each Co-N bond is a [coordinate covalent bond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate_covalent_bond "Coordinate covalent bond") between the [Lewis acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lewis_acid "Lewis acid") Co3+ and the [Lewis base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lewis_base "Lewis base") NH3.
