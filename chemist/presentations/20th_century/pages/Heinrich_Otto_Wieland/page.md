**Heinrich Otto Wieland** (German pronunciation: [[ˈhaɪnʁɪç ˈviːlant]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Standard_German "Help:IPA/Standard German") [ⓘ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:De-Heinrich_Wieland.ogg "File:De-Heinrich Wieland.ogg"); 4 June 1877 – 5 August 1957) was a German [chemist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemist "Chemist"). He won the 1927 [Nobel Prize in Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Chemistry "Nobel Prize in Chemistry") for his research into the [bile acids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bile_acid "Bile acid").

## Career

In 1901, Wieland received his doctorate at the [Ludwig-Maximilians-Universität München](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludwig-Maximilians-Universität_München "Ludwig-Maximilians-Universität München") while studying under [Johannes Thiele](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johannes_Thiele_(chemist) "Johannes Thiele (chemist)"). In 1904 he completed his [habilitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habilitation "Habilitation"), then continued to teach at the university and starting in 1907 was a consultant for [Boehringer Ingelheim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boehringer_Ingelheim "Boehringer Ingelheim"). In 1914, he became associate professor for special topics in [organic chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_chemistry "Organic chemistry"), and director of the Organic Division of the State Laboratory in [Munich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Munich "Munich"). From 1917 to 1918, Wieland worked in the service of the (KWI) [Kaiser Wilhelm Institute for Physical Chemistry and Electrochemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaiser_Wilhelm_Institute_for_Physical_Chemistry_and_Electrochemistry "Kaiser Wilhelm Institute for Physical Chemistry and Electrochemistry") in [Dahlem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dahlem_(Berlin) "Dahlem (Berlin)") then led by [Fritz Haber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fritz_Haber "Fritz Haber") as an alternative to regular military service. There he was involved in weapons research for instance finding new synthetic routes for [mustard gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mustard_gas "Mustard gas"). He is also credited with the first synthesis of [Adamsite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adamsite "Adamsite").

From 1913 to 1921, he was Professor at the [Technical University of Munich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technical_University_of_Munich "Technical University of Munich"). He then moved to the [University of Freiburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Freiburg "University of Freiburg") as successor of [Ludwig Gattermann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludwig_Gattermann "Ludwig Gattermann") (he also assumed responsibility for Gattermann's famous cookbook). In Freiburg he started working on toad poisons and bile acids. In association with Boehringer Ingelheim he worked on synthetic alkaloids such as [morphine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morphine "Morphine") and [strychnine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strychnine "Strychnine").

In 1925, Wieland succeeded [Richard Willstätter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Willstätter "Richard Willstätter") as Chemistry Professor at the [Ludwig-Maximilians-Universität München](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludwig-Maximilians-Universität_München "Ludwig-Maximilians-Universität München").

In 1941, Wieland isolated the toxin [alpha-amanitin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpha-amanitin "Alpha-amanitin"), the principal active agent of one of the world's most poisonous mushrooms *[Amanita phalloides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amanita_phalloides "Amanita phalloides")*.

Wieland tried successfully to protect people, especially [Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jew "Jew") students, who were "racially burdened" after the [Nuremberg Laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuremberg_Laws "Nuremberg Laws"). Students who were expelled because they were "racially burdened" could stay in Heinrich Wieland's group as chemists or as "Gäste des Geheimrats" (guests of the privy councillor). [Hans Conrad Leipelt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Conrad_Leipelt "Hans Conrad Leipelt"), a student of Wieland, was sentenced to death after collecting money for [Kurt Huber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurt_Huber "Kurt Huber")'s widow Clara Huber.

## Family

Heinrich's father, Theodor Wieland (1846–1928) was a pharmacist with a doctorate in chemistry. He owned a gold and silver refinery in Pforzheim. Heinrich Wieland was a cousin of Helene Boehringer, the wife of Albert Boehringer, who was the founder of the [Boehringer Ingelheim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boehringer_Ingelheim "Boehringer Ingelheim") pharmaceutical company. He worked for the company from 1915 to 1920 and established the company's scientific department.

Eva Wieland, Heinrich Wieland's daughter, was married to [Feodor Lynen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feodor_Felix_Konrad_Lynen "Feodor Felix Konrad Lynen") on 14 May 1937.

## Heinrich Wieland Prize

Since 1964, the [Heinrich Wieland Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heinrich_Wieland_Prize "Heinrich Wieland Prize") has been awarded annually. First to promote research on chemistry, [biochemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biochemistry "Biochemistry"), [physiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physiology "Physiology") and [clinical medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clinical_medicine "Clinical medicine") of lipids and related substances, nowadays the prize is awarded for outstanding research on biologically active molecules and systems in the fields of chemistry, biochemistry, and physiology as well as on their clinical importance. The prize is among the most treasured international science awards and has a successful history of over 50 years. The Heinrich Wieland Prize has been sponsored by Boehringer Ingelheim from 2000 to 2010. From 2011, it has been awarded by the Boehringer Ingelheim Foundation. The awardees have always been selected by an independent Board of Trustees. Since 2014, it has been endowed with 100,000 euros.
