**Frederik "Frits" Zernike** (Dutch: [[frɪt ˈsɛrnikə]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Dutch "Help:IPA/Dutch"); 16 July 1888 – 10 March 1966) was a Dutch [physicist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physicist "Physicist") who received the [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics") in 1953 for his invention of the [phase-contrast microscope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase-contrast_microscope "Phase-contrast microscope").

## Early life and education

Frederik Zernike was born on 16 July 1888 in [Amsterdam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amsterdam "Amsterdam"), the second son of Carl Frederik August Zernike and Antje Dieperink. Both parents were teachers of mathematics, and he especially shared his father's passion for physics. In 1905, he enrolled at the [University of Amsterdam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Amsterdam "University of Amsterdam"), studying chemistry (his major), mathematics, and physics. He received his [B.Sc.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B.Sc. "B.Sc.") in Chemistry in 1912 and his [Ph.D.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ph.D. "Ph.D.") in Physics in 1915.

## Academic career

In 1912, Zernike was awarded a prize for his doctoral work on [opalescence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opalescence "Opalescence") in gases. In 1913, he became assistant to [Jacobus Kapteyn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobus_Kapteyn "Jacobus Kapteyn") in the astronomical laboratory at the [University of Groningen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Groningen "University of Groningen"). In 1914, Zernike and [Leonard Ornstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonard_Ornstein "Leonard Ornstein") were jointly responsible for the derivation of the [Ornstein–Zernike equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ornstein–Zernike_equation "Ornstein–Zernike equation") in critical-point theory. In 1915, he became [lector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lecturer "Lecturer") in [theoretical mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theoretical_mechanics "Theoretical mechanics") and [mathematical physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_physics "Mathematical physics") at the same university and in 1920 he was promoted to Professor of Mathematical Physics.

In 1930, Zernike was conducting research into [spectral lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectral_line "Spectral line") when he discovered that the so-called *ghost lines* that occur to the left and right of each primary line in [spectra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_spectrum "Electromagnetic spectrum") created by means of a [diffraction grating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffraction_grating "Diffraction grating"), have their phase shifted from that of the primary line by 90 degrees. It was at a Physical and Medical Congress in [Wageningen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wageningen "Wageningen") in 1933, that Zernike first described his phase contrast technique in microscopy. He extended his method to test the figure of concave mirrors. His discovery lay at the base of the first phase contrast microscope, built during World War II. The phase-contrast microscope became particularly important in biology because it made colorless, transparent [living cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_(biology) "Cell (biology)") visible without staining them.

He also made another contribution in the field of optics, relating to the efficient description of the imaging defects or [aberrations of optical imaging systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_aberration "Optical aberration") like [microscopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microscope "Microscope") and [telescopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telescope "Telescope"). The representation of aberrations was originally based on the theory developed by [Ludwig Seidel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philipp_Ludwig_von_Seidel "Philipp Ludwig von Seidel") in the middle of the nineteenth century. [Seidel's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philipp_Ludwig_von_Seidel "Philipp Ludwig von Seidel") representation was based on [power series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_series "Power series") expansions and did not allow a clear separation between various types and orders of aberrations. [Zernike's orthogonal circle polynomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zernike's_orthogonal_circle_polynomials "Zernike's orthogonal circle polynomials") provided a solution to the long-standing problem of the optimum 'balancing' of the various aberrations of an optical instrument. Since the 1960s, [Zernike's circle polynomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zernike's_circle_polynomials "Zernike's circle polynomials") are widely used in [optical design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_design "Optical design"), optical [metrology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metrology "Metrology") and [image analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Image_analysis "Image analysis").

Zernike's work helped awaken interest in [coherence theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coherence_theory_(optics) "Coherence theory (optics)"), the study of partially coherent light sources. In 1938 he published a simpler derivation of Van Cittert's 1934 theorem on the coherence of radiation from distant sources, now known as the [Van Cittert–Zernike theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Van_Cittert–Zernike_theorem "Van Cittert–Zernike theorem").

## Personal life and death

Zernike was married to Dora van Bommel van Vloten, with whom he had one son. Dora died in 1945. He married his second wife Lena Koperberg-Baanders in 1954. After his retirement, they moved to [Naarden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naarden "Naarden").

Zernike died in hospital in [Amersfoort](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amersfoort "Amersfoort") on 10 March 1966 after suffering illness the last years of his life.

His granddaughter is the journalist [Kate Zernike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kate_Zernike "Kate Zernike"). His great-nephew [Gerard 't Hooft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerard_'t_Hooft "Gerard 't Hooft") won the [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics") in 1999.

## Commemoration

The university complex ([Zernike Campus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Groningen#Campus "University of Groningen")) to the north of the city of [Groningen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groningen "Groningen") is named after him, as is the crater [Zernike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zernike_(crater) "Zernike (crater)") on the Moon and the minor planet [11779 Zernike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_minor_planets:_11001–12000#779 "List of minor planets: 11001–12000").

The Oz Enterprise, a [Linux distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linux_distribution "Linux distribution"), was named after [Leonard Ornstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonard_Ornstein "Leonard Ornstein") and Frits Zernike.
