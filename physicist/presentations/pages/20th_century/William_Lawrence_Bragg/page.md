**Sir William Lawrence Bragg** (31 March 1890 – 1 July 1971) was an Australian-born British [X-ray crystallographer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray_crystallographer "X-ray crystallographer") who uniquely shared a [Nobel Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize#Specially_distinguished_laureates "Nobel Prize") with his father [William Henry Bragg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Henry_Bragg "William Henry Bragg") – the 1915 [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics") "for their services in the analysis of [crystal structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal_structure "Crystal structure") by means of [X-rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray "X-ray")," an important step in the development of [X-ray crystallography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray_crystallography "X-ray crystallography").

As of 2025, Bragg is the youngest ever [Nobel laureate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Nobel_laureates "List of Nobel laureates") in physics, or in any science category, having received the award at the age of 25. Bragg was the director of the [Cavendish Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cavendish_Laboratory "Cavendish Laboratory"), Cambridge, when [James D. Watson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_D._Watson "James D. Watson") and [Francis Crick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Crick "Francis Crick") reported the discovery of the structure of DNA in February 1953.

## Education

William Lawrence Bragg was born on 31 March 1890 in [Adelaide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adelaide "Adelaide"), South Australia, the son of [William Henry Bragg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Henry_Bragg "William Henry Bragg") (1862–1942), who was appointed Elder Professor of Mathematics and Physics at the [University of Adelaide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Adelaide "University of Adelaide") in 1885, and Gwendoline Todd (1869–1929), daughter of [Charles Todd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Todd_(pioneer) "Charles Todd (pioneer)"), government astronomer of South Australia.

In 1900, Bragg enrolled at [Queen's School, North Adelaide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen's_School,_North_Adelaide "Queen's School, North Adelaide"), followed by five years at [St Peter's College, Adelaide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Peter's_College,_Adelaide "St Peter's College, Adelaide"). He went to the University of Adelaide at the age of 16 to study mathematics, chemistry, and physics, graduating in 1908. The same year, his father accepted the Cavendish Chair of Physics at the [University of Leeds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Leeds "University of Leeds") and brought the family to England.

Bragg enrolled at the [University of Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Cambridge "University of Cambridge") as an undergraduate student of [Trinity College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College,_Cambridge "Trinity College, Cambridge"), in the autumn of 1909, receiving a major scholarship in mathematics, despite taking the exam while in bed with [pneumonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pneumonia "Pneumonia"). After initially excelling in mathematics, he transferred to the physics course in the later years of his studies, and graduated in 1912 with [First Class Honours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Class_Honours "First Class Honours") in the [Natural Sciences Tripos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_Sciences_Tripos "Natural Sciences Tripos"). In 1914, Bragg became a Fellow and Lecturer in Natural Sciences at Trinity College.

Among Bragg's other interests was [shell collecting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shell_collecting "Shell collecting"); his personal collection amounted to specimens from some 500 species; all personally collected from South Australia. He discovered a new species of [cuttlefish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuttlefish "Cuttlefish") – *[Sepia braggi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sepia_braggi "Sepia braggi")*, named for him by [Joseph Verco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Verco "Joseph Verco").

## Career and research

### X-rays and the Bragg equation

In 1912, as a first-year research student at Cambridge, W. L. Bragg, while strolling by the river, had the insight that crystals made from parallel sheets of atoms would not diffract X-ray beams that struck their surface at most angles because X-rays deflected by collisions with atoms would be out of phase, cancelling one another out. However, when the X-ray beam struck at an angle at which the distances it passed between atomic sheets in the crystal equalled the X-ray's wavelength then those deflected would be in phase and produce a spot on a nearby film. From this insight he wrote the simple [Bragg equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg's_law "Bragg's law") that relates the wavelength of the X-ray and the distance between atomic sheets in a simple crystal to the angles at which an impinging X-ray beam would be reflected.

His father built an [apparatus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray_spectroscopy "X-ray spectroscopy") in which a crystal could be rotated to precise angles while measuring the energy of reflections. This enabled father and son to measure the distances between the atomic sheets in a number of simple crystals. They calculated the spacing of the atoms from the weight of the crystal and the [Avogadro constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avogadro_constant "Avogadro constant"), which enabled them to measure the wavelengths of the X-rays produced by different metallic targets in the X-ray tubes. W. H. Bragg reported their results at meetings and in a paper, giving credit to "his son" (unnamed) for the equation, but not as a co-author, which gave his son "some heartaches", which he never overcame.

### Work on sound ranging

Bragg was commissioned early in [World War I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I "World War I") in the [Royal Horse Artillery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Horse_Artillery "Royal Horse Artillery") as a [second lieutenant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_lieutenant "Second lieutenant") of the [Leicestershire battery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leicestershire_Royal_Horse_Artillery "Leicestershire Royal Horse Artillery"). In 1915 he was seconded to the [Royal Engineers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Engineers "Royal Engineers") to develop a method to localise enemy artillery from the boom of their firing. On 2 September 1915 his brother was killed during the [Gallipoli Campaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gallipoli_Campaign "Gallipoli Campaign"). Shortly afterwards, he and his father were awarded the [Nobel Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize "Nobel Prize") in Physics. He was 25 years old and remains the youngest science laureate. The problem with [sound ranging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artillery_sound_ranging "Artillery sound ranging") was that the heavy guns boomed at too low a frequency to be detected by a microphone. After months of frustrating failure he and his group devised a hot wire air wave detector that solved the problem. In this work he was aided by [Charles Galton Darwin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Galton_Darwin "Charles Galton Darwin"), [William Sansome Tucker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Sansome_Tucker "William Sansome Tucker"), [Harold Roper Robinson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Roper_Robinson "Harold Roper Robinson"), [Edward Andrade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Andrade "Edward Andrade") and Henry Harold Hemming. British sound ranging was very effective; there was a unit in every British Army and their system was adopted by the Americans when they entered the war. For his work during the war, he was awarded the [Military Cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_Cross "Military Cross") and appointed an [Officer of the Order of the British Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Officer_of_the_Order_of_the_British_Empire "Officer of the Order of the British Empire") (OBE). He was also [mentioned in dispatches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mentioned_in_dispatches "Mentioned in dispatches") on 16 June 1916, 4 January 1917 and 7 July 1919.

Hot wire sound ranging was used in [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II") during which he served as a civilian adviser.

Between the wars, from 1919 to 1937, he worked at the [Victoria University of Manchester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victoria_University_of_Manchester "Victoria University of Manchester") as [Langworthy Professor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Langworthy_Professor "Langworthy Professor") of Physics. He became the director of the [National Physical Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Physical_Laboratory_(United_Kingdom) "National Physical Laboratory (United Kingdom)") in Teddington in 1937.

After World War II, Bragg returned to Cambridge, splitting the [Cavendish Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cavendish_Laboratory "Cavendish Laboratory") into research groups. He believed that "the ideal research unit is one of six to twelve scientists and a few assistants".

### University of Manchester

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/29/Lawrence_Bragg_Blue_Plaque_Manchester.jpg/250px-Lawrence_Bragg_Blue_Plaque_Manchester.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Lawrence_Bragg_Blue_Plaque_Manchester.jpg)

A [blue plaque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_plaque "Blue plaque") commemorating Bragg's work at the [University of Manchester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Manchester "University of Manchester").

When demobilised he returned to crystallography at Cambridge. They had agreed that father would study organic crystals, son would investigate inorganic compounds. In 1919 when [Ernest Rutherford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_Rutherford "Ernest Rutherford"), a long-time family friend, moved to Cambridge, Lawrence Bragg replaced him as Langworthy Professor of Physics at the [Victoria University of Manchester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victoria_University_of_Manchester "Victoria University of Manchester") and was elected to membership of the [Manchester Literary and Philosophical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manchester_Literary_and_Philosophical_Society "Manchester Literary and Philosophical Society") on 4.11.1919. He recruited an excellent faculty, including former sound rangers, but he believed that his knowledge of physics was weak and he had no classroom experience. The students, many veterans, were critical and rowdy. He was deeply shaken but with family support he pulled himself together and prevailed. He and R. W. James measured the absolute energy of reflected X-rays, which validated a formula derived by C. G. Darwin before the war. Now they could determine the number of electrons in the reflecting targets, and they were able to decipher the structures of more complicated crystals like silicates. It was still difficult: requiring repeated guessing and retrying. In the late 1920s they eased the analysis by using [Fourier transforms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourier_transform "Fourier transform") on the data.

In 1930, he became deeply disturbed while considering a job offer from [Imperial College London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_College_London "Imperial College London"). His family rallied around and he recovered his balance while they spent 1931 in Munich, where he did research.

### National Physical Laboratory

He became director of the [National Physical Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Physical_Laboratory_(United_Kingdom) "National Physical Laboratory (United Kingdom)") in Teddington in 1937, bringing some co-workers along. However, administration and committees took much of his time away from the workbench.

### University of Cambridge

Rutherford died and the search committee nominated Lawrence Bragg as next in the line of the [Cavendish Professors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cavendish_Professor_of_Physics "Cavendish Professor of Physics") who directed the [Cavendish Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cavendish_Laboratory "Cavendish Laboratory"). The Laboratory had an eminent history in atomic physics and certain members were wary of appointing a crystallographer, which Bragg surmounted by even-handed administration. He worked on improving the interpretation of diffraction patterns. In the small crystallography group was a refugee research student without a mentor: [Max Perutz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Perutz "Max Perutz"). He showed Bragg X-ray diffraction data from [haemoglobin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hemoglobin "Hemoglobin"), which suggested that the structure of giant biological molecules might be deciphered. Bragg appointed Perutz as his research assistant and within a few months obtained additional support with a grant from the [Rockefeller Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rockefeller_Foundation "Rockefeller Foundation"). The work was suspended during the Second World War when Perutz was interned as an [enemy alien](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enemy_alien "Enemy alien") and then worked in military research.

During the war the Cavendish offered a shortened graduate course which emphasised the electronics needed for [radar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radar "Radar"). Bragg worked on the structure of metals and consulted on sonar and sound ranging, for which the Tucker microphone was still used. Bragg was knighted and became Sir Lawrence in 1941. After his father died in 1942, Bragg served for six months as Scientific Liaison Officer to Canada. He also organised periodic conferences on X-ray analysis, which was widely used in [military research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_science "Military science").

After the war Bragg led in the formation of the [International Union of Crystallography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Union_of_Crystallography "International Union of Crystallography") and was elected its first president. He reorganised the Cavendish into units to reflect his conviction that "the ideal research unit is one of six to twelve scientists and a few assistants, helped by one or more first-class instrument mechanics and a workshop in which the general run of apparatus can be constructed." Senior members of staff now had offices, telephones, and secretarial support. The scope of the department was enlarged with a new unit on radio astronomy. Bragg's own work focused on the structure of metals, using both X-rays and the [electron microscope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_microscope "Electron microscope"). In 1947 he persuaded the [Medical Research Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_Research_Council_(United_Kingdom) "Medical Research Council (United Kingdom)") (MRC) to support what he described as the "gallant attempt" to determine protein structure as the [Laboratory of Molecular Biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laboratory_of_Molecular_Biology "Laboratory of Molecular Biology"), initially consisting of Perutz, [John Kendrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Kendrew "John Kendrew") and two assistants. Bragg worked with them and by 1960 they had resolved the structure of [myoglobin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myoglobin "Myoglobin") to the atomic level. After this Bragg was less involved; their analysis of [haemoglobin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hemoglobin "Hemoglobin") was easier after they incorporated two mercury atoms as markers in each molecule. The first monumental triumph of the MRC was decoding the structure of DNA by [James Watson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Watson "James Watson") and [Francis Crick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Crick "Francis Crick"). Bragg announced the discovery at a [Solvay conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solvay_conference "Solvay conference") on proteins in Belgium on 8 April 1953, though it went unreported by the press. He then gave a talk at [Guy's Hospital Medical School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guy's_Hospital_Medical_School "Guy's Hospital Medical School") in London on Thursday, 14 May 1953, which resulted in an article by [Ritchie Calder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ritchie_Calder "Ritchie Calder") in the *[News Chronicle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/News_Chronicle "News Chronicle")* of London on Friday, 15 May 1953, entitled "Why You Are You. Nearer Secret of Life". Bragg nominated Crick, Watson and [Maurice Wilkins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maurice_Wilkins "Maurice Wilkins") for the 1962 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine; Wilkins' share recognised the contribution of X-ray crystallographers at [King's College London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King's_College_London "King's College London"). Among them was [Rosalind Franklin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosalind_Franklin "Rosalind Franklin"), whose "[photograph 51](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photo_51 "Photo 51")" showed that DNA was a double [helix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helix "Helix"), not the triple helix that [Linus Pauling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linus_Pauling "Linus Pauling") had proposed. Franklin died before the prize (which only goes to living people) was awarded.

### Royal Institution

In 1953 the Braggs moved into the elegant flat for the Resident Professor in the [Royal Institution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Institution "Royal Institution") in London, the position his father had occupied when he died. In 1934 and 1961 Lawrence had delivered the [Royal Institution Christmas Lecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Institution_Christmas_Lectures "Royal Institution Christmas Lectures") and since 1938 he had been Professor of Natural Philosophy in the Institution, delivering an annual lecture. His father's successors had weakened the Institution, so Bragg had to rebuild it. He bolstered finances by enlisting corporate sponsors, the traditional Friday Evening Discourses were followed by a dinner party for the speaker and carefully selected possible patrons, more than 120 of them each year. "Two of these Discourses in 1965 gave him particular pleasure. On 7 May, Lady Bragg, who had been a member of the Royal Commission on Marriage and Divorce (1951–55) and was Chairman of the [National Marriage Guidance Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Marriage_Guidance_Council "National Marriage Guidance Council"), lectured on 'Changing patterns in marriage and divorce'; and on 15 November, Bragg listened with evident pride to the Discourse on 'Oscillations and noise in jet engines' given by his engineer-son [Stephen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Bragg "Stephen Bragg"), who was then Chief Scientist at Rolls-Royce Ltd and later became Vice-Chancellor of [Brunel University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brunel_University "Brunel University")." He also introduced a programme of highly regarded Schools' Lectures, enlivened by the elaborate demonstrations that were a hallmark of the Institution. He gave three of these lectures on "electricity".

He continued research in the Institution by recruiting a small group to work in the Davy-Faraday Laboratory in the basement and in the adjoining house, supported by grants he obtained. A visitor to the laboratory succeeded in inserting heavy metals into the enzyme [lysozyme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lysozyme "Lysozyme"); the structure of its crystal was solved in 1965 at the Royal Institution by [David Chilton Phillips](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Chilton_Phillips "David Chilton Phillips") and his coworkers, with the computations on the 9,040 reflections performed on the digital computer at the University of London, which greatly facilitated the work. Two of the illustrations of the positioning of amino acids in the chain were drawn by Bragg. Unlike myoglobin, in which nearly 80 per cent of the amino-acid residues are in the [alpha-helix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpha_helix "Alpha helix") conformation, in lysozyme the alpha-helix content is only about 40 per cent of the amino-acid residues found in four main stretches. Other stretches are of the [310 helix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/310_helix "310 helix"), a conformation that they had proposed earlier. In this conformation, every third peptide is hydrogen-bonded back to the first peptide, thus forming a ring containing ten atoms. They had the complete structure of an enzyme in time for Bragg's 75th birthday. He became Professor Emeritus in 1966.

X-ray analysis of protein structure flourished in subsequent years, determining the structures of scores of proteins in laboratories around the world. Twenty eight Nobel Prizes have been awarded for work using X-ray analysis. The disadvantage of the method is that it must be done on crystals, which precludes seeing changes in shape when enzymes bind substrates and the like. This problem was solved by the development of another line Bragg had initiated, using modified electron microscopes to image single frozen molecules: [cryo-electron microscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryo-electron_microscopy "Cryo-electron microscopy").

In his long association with the [Royal Institution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Institution "Royal Institution") he was:

- Professor of Natural Philosophy, 1938–1953
- [Fullerian Professor of Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fullerian_Professor_of_Chemistry "Fullerian Professor of Chemistry"), 1954–1966
- Superintendent of the House, 1954–1966
- Director of the Davy-Faraday Research Laboratory, 1954–1966
- [Director of the Royal Institution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Director_of_the_Royal_Institution "Director of the Royal Institution"), 1965–1966
- Emeritus Professor, 1966–1971

## Personal life

In 1921, Bragg married Alice Hopkinson (1899–1989), a cousin of a Cecil Hopkinson (1891–1917), who shared rooms with Bragg, and was one of his closest friends whilst they were both studying at Cambridge. Cecil was the son of [John Hopkinson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Hopkinson "John Hopkinson") who was Alice's uncle.

They had four children, the engineer [Stephen Lawrence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Bragg "Stephen Bragg") (1923–2014), David William (1926–2005), Margaret Alice (1931–2022) (who married the diplomat [Mark Heath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Heath "Mark Heath")), and Patience Mary (1935–2020) (who married David, the son of [George Paget Thomson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Paget_Thomson "George Paget Thomson") the Nobel prize winning physicist). Alice was on the staff at [Withington Girls' School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Withington_Girls'_School "Withington Girls' School") until Bragg was appointed director of the National Physical Laboratory in 1937. She was active in a number of public bodies and served as [Mayor of Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_mayors_of_Cambridge "List of mayors of Cambridge") from 1945 to 1946.

Bragg in 1967

Bragg's hobbies included drawing – family letters were illustrated with lively sketches – painting, literature and a lifelong interest in gardening. When he moved to London, he missed having a garden and so worked as a part-time gardener, unrecognised by his employer, until a guest at the house expressed surprise at seeing him there.

Bragg died on 1 July 1971 at a hospital near his home in [Waldringfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waldringfield "Waldringfield"), [Ipswich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ipswich "Ipswich"), at the age of 81. He is buried at [Trinity College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College,_Cambridge "Trinity College, Cambridge"). His son, David, is buried in the [Parish of the Ascension Burial Ground](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ascension_Parish_Burial_Ground,_Cambridge "Ascension Parish Burial Ground, Cambridge") in Cambridge, his grave is within a few paces of that of Bragg's close friend, Rudolph Cecil Hopkinson, who incurred a severe head wound in the 1914–19 war and died a few months after being invalided back to the UK.

In August 2013, Bragg's relative, the broadcaster [Melvyn Bragg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melvyn_Bragg "Melvyn Bragg"), presented a BBC Radio 4 programme ("Bragg on the Braggs") on the 1915 Nobel Prize in Physics winners.

## Commemoration

Since 1967, the [Institute of Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_of_Physics "Institute of Physics") has awarded the [Lawrence Bragg Medal and Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawrence_Bragg_Medal_and_Prize "Lawrence Bragg Medal and Prize"). Additionally since 1992, the [Australian Institute of Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_Institute_of_Physics "Australian Institute of Physics") has awarded the Bragg Gold Medal for Excellence in Physics to commemorate Lawrence Bragg (in front on the medal) and his father, William Bragg, for the best PhD thesis by a student at an [Australian university](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_universities_in_Australia "List of universities in Australia").

The [Electoral district of Bragg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electoral_district_of_Bragg "Electoral district of Bragg"), in the [South Australian House of Assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Australian_House_of_Assembly "South Australian House of Assembly"), was created in 1970, and was named after both William and Lawrence Bragg.
