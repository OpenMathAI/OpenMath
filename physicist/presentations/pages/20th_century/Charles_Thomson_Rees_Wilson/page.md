**Charles Thomson Rees Wilson** (14 February 1869 – 15 November 1959) was a British [meteorologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meteorologist "Meteorologist") and [physicist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physicist "Physicist") who shared the 1927 [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics") with [Arthur Compton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Compton "Arthur Compton") for his invention of the [cloud chamber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cloud_chamber "Cloud chamber").

## Early life and education

Charles Thomson Rees Wilson was born on 14 February 1869 in [Glencorse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glencorse "Glencorse"), Scotland, the son of John Wilson, a sheep farmer, and Annie Clark Harper. After his father died in 1873, Wilson moved with his family to [Manchester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manchester "Manchester"), England.

With financial support from his step-brother, Wilson studied biology at [Owens College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Owens_College "Owens College") (now the [University of Manchester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Manchester "University of Manchester")) with the intent of becoming a doctor. He graduated with a [B.Sc.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B.Sc. "B.Sc.") in 1887. The following year, he won a scholarship to attend [Sidney Sussex College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidney_Sussex_College,_Cambridge "Sidney Sussex College, Cambridge"), where he became interested in physics and chemistry. In 1892, he received [First Class Honours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Class_Honours "First Class Honours") in both parts of the [Natural Sciences Tripos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_Sciences_Tripos "Natural Sciences Tripos").

## Career and research

Wilson became particularly interested in [meteorology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meteorology "Meteorology"), and in 1893 he began to study clouds and their properties. Beginning in 1894, he worked for some time at the observatory on [Ben Nevis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ben_Nevis "Ben Nevis"), where he made observations of cloud formation. He was particularly fascinated by the appearance of [glories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glory_(optical_phenomenon) "Glory (optical phenomenon)"). He then tried to reproduce this effect on a smaller scale in the [Cavendish Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cavendish_Laboratory "Cavendish Laboratory") at Cambridge, expanding humid air within a sealed container.

In 1895, Wilson discovered that at a large enough expansion ratio, supersaturated water vapour condensates even without dust which he removed by previous condensations, contrary to the previous research by [John Aitken](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Aitken_(meteorologist) "John Aitken (meteorologist)"). Under [J. J. Thomson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._J._Thomson "J. J. Thomson")'s mentorship by 1896, he found out that [X-rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray "X-ray") stimulate the condensation just as well as dust.

In 1900, Wilson became a Fellow of [Sidney Sussex College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidney_Sussex_College,_Cambridge "Sidney Sussex College, Cambridge"), and was appointed University Lecturer and Demonstrator. He was known by some as a poor lecturer, due to a pronounced stutter. He taught a course on [atmospheric electricity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmospheric_electricity "Atmospheric electricity") as a visiting lecturer at [Imperial College London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_College_London "Imperial College London"). He was appointed Reader in Electrical Meteorology in 1918 and [Jacksonian Professor of Natural Philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacksonian_Professor_of_Natural_Philosophy "Jacksonian Professor of Natural Philosophy") in 1925.

In 1906, Wilson hypothesised that [cosmic radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmic_radiation "Cosmic radiation") generates the ions causing condensation without apparent reasons.

Wilson published numerous papers on meteorology and physics, on topics including X-rays, [ionisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionisation "Ionisation"), thundercloud formation, and other meteorological events. Wilson may also have observed a [sprite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sprite_(lightning) "Sprite (lightning)") in 1924, 65 years before their official discovery. Weather was a focus of his work throughout his career, from his early observations at Ben Nevis to his final paper, on thunderclouds.

## Cloud chamber

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/87/CTRwilsonCloudChamberCavendishLab2013-08-29-17-09-40.jpg/250px-CTRwilsonCloudChamberCavendishLab2013-08-29-17-09-40.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:CTRwilsonCloudChamberCavendishLab2013-08-29-17-09-40.jpg)

Wilson's original cloud chamber.

The invention of the [cloud chamber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cloud_chamber "Cloud chamber") was by far Wilson's signature accomplishment, earning him the [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics") in 1927. The [Cavendish Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cavendish_Laboratory "Cavendish Laboratory") praised him for the creation of "a novel and striking method of investigating the properties of ionised gases." The cloud chamber allowed huge experimental leaps forward in the study of [subatomic particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subatomic_particle "Subatomic particle") and the field of [particle physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_physics "Particle physics"), generally. Some have credited Wilson with making the study of particles possible at all.

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b2/Observatory_Ben_Nevis_memorial.jpg/500px-Observatory_Ben_Nevis_memorial.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Observatory_Ben_Nevis_memorial.jpg)

Commemorative plaque at [Ben Nevis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ben_Nevis "Ben Nevis") about the observatory there, and Wilson's cloud chamber.

Wilson later experimented with the creation of cloud trails in his chamber by condensation onto [ions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ion "Ion") generated by [radioactivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radioactivity "Radioactivity"). Several of his cloud chambers survive.

Retrospectively, Wilson's experimental method has received some attention from scholars.

In a period of scientific inquiry characterised by a divide between "analytical" and "morphological" scientists, Wilson's method of inquiry represented a hybrid. While some scientists believed phenomena should be observed in pure nature, others proposed laboratory-controlled experiments as the premier method for inquiry. Wilson used a combination of methods in his experiments and investigations. Wilson's work "made things visible whose properties had only previously been deduced indirectly."

Wilson has been called "almost the last of the great individual experimenters in physics." He used his cloud chamber in various ways to demonstrate the operating principles of things like subatomic particles and [X-rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray "X-ray"). But his primary interest, and the subject of the bulk of his papers, was [meteorology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meteorology "Meteorology").

## Personal life and death

In 1908, Wilson married Jessie Fraser, the daughter of a [minister](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_minister "Religious minister") from [Glasgow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glasgow "Glasgow"). They had four children. His family knew him as patient and curious, and fond of taking walks in the hills near his home.

Wilson died on 15 November 1959 at his home in [Carlops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carlops "Carlops") at the age of 90, surrounded by his family.

## Commemoration

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/01/Wilson_plaque_at_Glencorse.jpg/250px-Wilson_plaque_at_Glencorse.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Wilson_plaque_at_Glencorse.jpg)

Commemorative plaque at Flotterstone

[Wilson crater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilson_(crater) "Wilson (crater)") on the Moon is named after him, [Alexander Wilson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Wilson_(mathematician) "Alexander Wilson (mathematician)"), and [Ralph Elmer Wilson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ralph_Elmer_Wilson "Ralph Elmer Wilson"). The Wilson Condensation Cloud formations that occur after large explosions, such as [nuclear detonations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effects_of_nuclear_explosions "Effects of nuclear explosions"), are named after him. The Wilson Society—the scientific society of [Sidney Sussex College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidney_Sussex_College,_Cambridge "Sidney Sussex College, Cambridge")—is named in his honour, as is the CTR Wilson Institute for Atmospheric Electricity—the Atmospheric Electricity Special Interest Group of the [Royal Meteorological Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Meteorological_Society "Royal Meteorological Society").

The archives of C.T.R. Wilson are maintained by the [Archives of the University of Glasgow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archives_of_the_University_of_Glasgow "Archives of the University of Glasgow").

in 1996, a [blue plaque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_plaque "Blue plaque") in Wilson's honour was installed in a specially built cairn at Flotterstone, close to his birthplace at Crosshouse Farm.

In 2012, the [Royal Society of Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society_of_Edinburgh "Royal Society of Edinburgh") held a meeting in honour of Wilson, the "Great Scottish Physicist."
