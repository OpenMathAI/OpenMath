**Richard Edward Taylor** (2 November 1929 – 22 February 2018), was a Canadian physicist and [Stanford University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford_University "Stanford University") professor. He shared the 1990 [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics") with [Jerome Friedman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerome_Isaac_Friedman "Jerome Isaac Friedman") and [Henry Kendall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Way_Kendall "Henry Way Kendall") "for their pioneering investigations concerning [deep inelastic scattering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deep_inelastic_scattering "Deep inelastic scattering") of [electrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron "Electron") on [protons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proton "Proton") and bound [neutrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron "Neutron"), which have been of essential importance for the development of the [quark model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quark_model "Quark model") in particle physics."

## Early life

Taylor was born in [Medicine Hat, Alberta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medicine_Hat,_Alberta "Medicine Hat, Alberta"). He studied for his BSc (1950) and MSc (1952) degrees at the [University of Alberta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Alberta "University of Alberta") in Edmonton, Canada. Newly married, he applied to work for a PhD degree at [Stanford University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford_University "Stanford University"), where he joined the High Energy Physics Laboratory.

His PhD thesis was on an experiment using polarised [gamma rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamma_ray "Gamma ray") to study [pion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pion "Pion") production.

## Research and career

After three years at the [École Normale Supérieure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/École_Normale_Supérieure "École Normale Supérieure") in Paris and a year at the [Lawrence Berkeley Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawrence_Berkeley_Laboratory "Lawrence Berkeley Laboratory") in California, Taylor returned to Stanford. Construction of the Stanford Linear Accelerator Center (now the [SLAC National Accelerator Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SLAC_National_Accelerator_Laboratory "SLAC National Accelerator Laboratory")) was beginning. In collaboration with researchers from the [California Institute of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California_Institute_of_Technology "California Institute of Technology") and the [Massachusetts Institute of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massachusetts_Institute_of_Technology "Massachusetts Institute of Technology"), Taylor worked on the design and construction of the equipment, and was involved in many of the experiments.

In 1971, Taylor was awarded a [Guggenheim fellowship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guggenheim_fellowship "Guggenheim fellowship") that allowed him to spend a sabbatical year at [CERN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CERN "CERN").

The experiments run at SLAC in the late 1960s and early 1970s involved scattering high-energy beams of [electrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron "Electron") from [protons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proton "Proton") and [deuterons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deuteron "Deuteron") and heavier [nuclei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_nucleus "Atomic nucleus"). At lower energies, it had already been found that the electrons would only be scattered through low angles, consistent with the idea that the [nucleons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nucleon "Nucleon") had no internal structure. However, the SLAC-MIT experiments showed that higher energy electrons could be scattered through much higher angles, with the loss of some energy. These deep inelastic scattering results provided the first experimental evidence that the protons and neutrons were made up of point-like particles, later identified to be the [up](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Up_quark "Up quark") and [down](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Down_quark "Down quark") [quarks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quark "Quark") that had previously been proposed on theoretical grounds. The experiments also provided the first evidence for the existence of [gluons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gluon "Gluon"). Taylor, Friedman and Kendall were jointly awarded the Nobel Prize in 1990 for this work.

## Death

Taylor died at his home in [Stanford, California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford,_California "Stanford, California") near the campus of Stanford University on 22 February 2018 at the age of 88.

## Awards and honours

Taylor has received numerous awards and honours including:

- Alexander von Humboldt Senior Scientist Award, 1982.
- [W.K.H. Panofsky Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panofsky_Prize "Panofsky Prize"), 1989.
- [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics"), 1990.
- Golden Plate Award, [American Academy of Achievement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academy_of_Achievement "Academy of Achievement"), 1991.
- Fellow, Guggenheim Foundation, 1971 – 1972.
- Fellow, [American Physical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Physical_Society "American Physical Society"), 1986.
- Fellow, [American Association for the Advancement of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Association_for_the_Advancement_of_Science "American Association for the Advancement of Science").
- Elected a [Fellow of the Royal Society (FRS) in 1997](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Fellows_of_the_Royal_Society_elected_in_1997 "List of Fellows of the Royal Society elected in 1997")
- Fellow, [Royal Society of Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society_of_Canada "Royal Society of Canada").
- Member, [American Academy of Arts and Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Academy_of_Arts_and_Sciences "American Academy of Arts and Sciences").
- Member, Canadian Association of Physicists.
- Foreign Associate, [National Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Academy_of_Sciences "National Academy of Sciences").
- Companion of the [Order of Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_Canada "Order of Canada"), 2005.

## In popular culture

In May 2019, the announcement of the 1990 Nobel Prize for physics was featured on the season 2 finale of the TV series [Young Sheldon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Young_Sheldon "Young Sheldon"). "A Swedish Science Thing and the Equation for Toast" featured Sheldon Cooper as a child, listening to a short wave radio as the Nobel Prize was announced in Sweden.
