**John Hasbrouck Van Vleck** ([/væn vlɛk/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English"); March 13, 1899 – October 27, 1980) was an American [physicist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physicist "Physicist") and [mathematician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematician "Mathematician") who won the [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics") in 1977 for his contributions to the understanding of the behavior of [electronic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron "Electron") [magnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetism "Magnetism") in [solids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid "Solid"). He shared the Prize with [Philip W. Anderson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_W._Anderson "Philip W. Anderson") and [Nevill Mott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nevill_Mott "Nevill Mott").

## Early life and education

John Hasbrouck Van Vleck was born on March 13, 1899, to mathematician [Edward Burr Van Vleck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Burr_Van_Vleck "Edward Burr Van Vleck") and Hester L. Raymond in [Middletown, Connecticut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middletown,_Connecticut "Middletown, Connecticut"), while his father was an assistant professor at [Wesleyan University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wesleyan_University "Wesleyan University"), and where his grandfather, astronomer [John Monroe Van Vleck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Monroe_Van_Vleck "John Monroe Van Vleck"), was also a professor. He grew up in [Madison, Wisconsin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madison,_Wisconsin "Madison, Wisconsin"), and received an [A.B.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_Arts "Bachelor of Arts") [degree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academic_degree "Academic degree") from the [University of Wisconsin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Wisconsin–Madison "University of Wisconsin–Madison") in 1920, before earning his Ph.D. at [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University "Harvard University") in 1922 under the supervision of [Edwin C. Kemble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edwin_C._Kemble "Edwin C. Kemble").

## Career and research

He joined the [University of Minnesota](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Minnesota "University of Minnesota") as an assistant [professor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Professor "Professor") in 1923, then moved to the University of Wisconsin before settling at Harvard. He also earned *[Honorary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honorary_Degree "Honorary Degree") D. Sc.*, or *D. [Honoris Causa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honoris_Causa "Honoris Causa")*, degree from [Wesleyan University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wesleyan_University "Wesleyan University") in 1936.

J. H. Van Vleck established the fundamentals of the [quantum mechanical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_mechanical "Quantum mechanical") theory of [magnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetism "Magnetism"), [crystal field theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal_field_theory "Crystal field theory") and [ligand field theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ligand_field_theory "Ligand field theory") ([chemical bonding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_bonding "Chemical bonding") in [metal complexes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_(chemistry) "Complex (chemistry)")). He is regarded as the *Father of Modern [Magnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetism "Magnetism")*.

During [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II"), J. H. Van Vleck worked on [radar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radar "Radar") at the [MIT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MIT "MIT") [Radiation Lab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiation_Lab "Radiation Lab"). He was half time at the [Radiation Lab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiation_Lab "Radiation Lab") and half time on the staff at [Harvard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University "Harvard University"). He showed that at about 1.25-centimeter [wavelength](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavelength "Wavelength") [water molecules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_molecule "Water molecule") in the [atmosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmosphere "Atmosphere") would lead to troublesome [absorption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absorption_(electromagnetic_radiation) "Absorption (electromagnetic radiation)") and that at 0.5-centimeter [wavelength](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavelength "Wavelength") there would be a similar [absorption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absorption_(electromagnetic_radiation) "Absorption (electromagnetic radiation)") by [oxygen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxygen "Oxygen") [molecules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecules "Molecules").
This was to have important consequences not just for military (and civil) [radar systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radar_system "Radar system") but later for the new science of [radioastronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radioastronomy "Radioastronomy").

Van Vleck (left) receives the [Lorentz Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentz_Medal "Lorentz Medal") from [Hendrik Brugt Gerhard Casimir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hendrik_Brugt_Gerhard_Casimir "Hendrik Brugt Gerhard Casimir") at the [Royal Netherlands Academy of Arts and Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Netherlands_Academy_of_Arts_and_Sciences "Royal Netherlands Academy of Arts and Sciences"), Amsterdam, 1974.

J. H. Van Vleck participated in the [Manhattan Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manhattan_Project "Manhattan Project"). In June [1942](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline_of_World_War_II_(1942) "Timeline of World War II (1942)"), [J. Robert Oppenheimer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._Robert_Oppenheimer "J. Robert Oppenheimer") held a summer study for confirming the concept and feasibility of a [nuclear weapon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_weapon "Nuclear weapon") at the [University of California, Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Berkeley "University of California, Berkeley"). Eight theoretical scientists, including J. H. Van Vleck, attended it. From July to September, the theoretical study group examined and developed the principles of [atomic bomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_bomb "Atomic bomb") design.

J. H. Van Vleck's theoretical work led to the establishment of the [Los Alamos Nuclear Weapons Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Los_Alamos_Nuclear_Weapons_Laboratory "Los Alamos Nuclear Weapons Laboratory"). He also served on the [Los Alamos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Los_Alamos_National_Laboratory "Los Alamos National Laboratory") Review committee in 1943. The committee, established by General [Leslie Groves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leslie_R._Groves "Leslie R. Groves"), also consisted of [W. K. Lewis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warren_K._Lewis "Warren K. Lewis") of [MIT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MIT "MIT"), Chairman; E. L. Rose, of Jones & Lamson; [E. B. Wilson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edgar_Bright_Wilson "Edgar Bright Wilson") of [Harvard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard "Harvard"); and [Richard C. Tolman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_C._Tolman "Richard C. Tolman"), Vice Chairman of [NDRC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Defense_Research_Committee "National Defense Research Committee"). The committee's important contribution (originating with Rose) was a reduction in the size of the firing gun for the [Little Boy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_Boy "Little Boy") atomic bomb, a concept that eliminated additional design weight and sped up production of the bomb for its eventual release over [Hiroshima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hiroshima "Hiroshima"). However, it was not employed for the [Fat Man](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fat_Man "Fat Man") bomb at [Nagasaki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nagasaki "Nagasaki"), which relied on implosion of a plutonium shell to reach critical mass.

The philosopher and historian of science [Thomas Kuhn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Kuhn "Thomas Kuhn") completed a Ph.D. in physics under Van Vleck's supervision at Harvard.

From 1951, Van Vleck was Hollis Professor of Mathematics and Natural Philosophy at Harvard. He concurrently held the first deanship of Harvard's Division of Engineering and Applied Physics until 1957.

Van Vleck's grave (back right) at Forest Hill Cemetery

In 1961/62 he was [George Eastman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Eastman "George Eastman") [Visiting Professor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visiting_Professor "Visiting Professor") at [University of Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Oxford "University of Oxford") and held a [professorship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Professorship "Professorship") at [Balliol College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balliol_College,_Oxford "Balliol College, Oxford").

In 1950 he became foreign member of the [Royal Netherlands Academy of Arts and Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Netherlands_Academy_of_Arts_and_Sciences "Royal Netherlands Academy of Arts and Sciences"). He was awarded the [National Medal of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Medal_of_Science "National Medal of Science") in 1966 and the [Lorentz Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentz_Medal "Lorentz Medal") in 1974.
For his contributions to the understanding of the behavior of [electrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron "Electron") in [magnetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic "Magnetic") [solids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solids "Solids"), Van Vleck was awarded the [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics") 1977, along with [Philip W. Anderson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_Warren_Anderson "Philip Warren Anderson") and Sir [Nevill Mott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nevill_Mott "Nevill Mott"). Van Vleck transformations, [Van Vleck paramagnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Van_Vleck_paramagnetism "Van Vleck paramagnetism") and [Van Vleck formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_chaos "Quantum chaos") are named after him.

Van Vleck died in [Cambridge, Massachusetts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge,_Massachusetts "Cambridge, Massachusetts"), aged 81. He was buried at [Forest Hill Cemetery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forest_Hill_Cemetery_(Madison,_Wisconsin) "Forest Hill Cemetery (Madison, Wisconsin)").

## Awards and honors

Van Vleck was elected to the [American Academy of Arts and Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Academy_of_Arts_and_Sciences "American Academy of Arts and Sciences") in 1934, the United States [National Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Academy_of_Sciences "National Academy of Sciences") in 1935, and the [American Philosophical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Philosophical_Society "American Philosophical Society") in 1939. He was awarded the [Irving Langmuir Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irving_Langmuir_Award "Irving Langmuir Award") in 1965, the [National Medal of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Medal_of_Science "National Medal of Science") in 1966 and elected a [Foreign Member of the Royal Society (ForMemRS) in 1967](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Fellows_of_the_Royal_Society_elected_in_1967 "List of Fellows of the Royal Society elected in 1967"). He was awarded the [Elliott Cresson Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliott_Cresson_Medal "Elliott Cresson Medal") in 1971, the [Lorentz Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentz_Medal "Lorentz Medal") in 1974 and the [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics") in 1977.

## Personal life

While a professor at the [University of Minnesota](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Minnesota "University of Minnesota"), John Hasbrouck Van Vleck met Abigail Pearson, a student at the university, and later married her on June 10, 1927. They inherited from Van Vleck's [father](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Burr_Van_Vleck "Edward Burr Van Vleck") a collection of Japanese woodblock prints, known as the *E. B. Van Vleck Collection*, which they donated to the [Chazen Museum of Art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chazen_Museum_of_Art "Chazen Museum of Art") in [Madison, Wisconsin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madison,_Wisconsin "Madison, Wisconsin") in 1980s. He was related to [Tom Van Vleck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tom_Van_Vleck "Tom Van Vleck"), a software engineer who co-authored an early email program.
