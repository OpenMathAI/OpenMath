**Leon N. Cooper** ([né](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Né "Né") **Kupchik**; February 28, 1930 – October 23, 2024) was an American theoretical [physicist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physicist "Physicist") and [neuroscientist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neuroscientist "Neuroscientist") who shared the 1972 [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics") for his work on [superconductivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superconductivity "Superconductivity"). Cooper developed the concept of [Cooper pairs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooper_pair "Cooper pair") and collaborated with [John Bardeen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Bardeen "John Bardeen") and [John Robert Schrieffer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Robert_Schrieffer "John Robert Schrieffer") to develop the [BCS theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BCS_theory "BCS theory") of [conventional superconductivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conventional_superconductivity "Conventional superconductivity"). In neuroscience, Cooper co-developed the [BCM theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BCM_theory "BCM theory") of [synaptic plasticity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synaptic_plasticity "Synaptic plasticity").

## Early life and education

Leon N. Kupchick was born on February 28, 1930 in [The Bronx](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Bronx "The Bronx"), [New York City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_City "New York City"). His father, Irving Kupchik, was from Belarus and moved to the United States after the [Russian Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Revolution "Russian Revolution") in 1917. His mother, Anna Zola, was from Poland; she died when Leon was seven. His father changed the family's surname from Kupchick to **Cooper** when he remarried.

Leon attended the [Bronx High School of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronx_High_School_of_Science "Bronx High School of Science"), graduating in 1947.
He then studied at [Columbia University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia_University "Columbia University"), receiving a [B.A.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B.A. "B.A.") in 1951. He remained at Columbia for graduate school, obtaining an [M.A.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M.A. "M.A.") in 1953, and a [Ph.D.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ph.D. "Ph.D.") in 1954. His doctoral thesis was on the subject of [muonic atoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muonic_atoms "Muonic atoms") and the [charge radius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charge_radius "Charge radius").

## Career

Cooper spent one year as a [postdoctoral researcher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postdoctoral_researcher "Postdoctoral researcher") at the [Institute for Advanced Study](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_for_Advanced_Study "Institute for Advanced Study") in Princeton. New Jersey. He then taught at the [University of Illinois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Illinois "University of Illinois") and [Ohio State University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ohio_State_University "Ohio State University") before joining [Brown University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brown_University "Brown University") in 1958. He would remain at Brown for the rest of his career.

Cooper founded Brown's Institute for Brain and Neural Systems in 1973, becoming its first director. In 1974 he was appointed Professor of Science at Brown, an [endowed chair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endowed_chair "Endowed chair") funded by [Thomas J. Watson Sr.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_J._Watson_Sr. "Thomas J. Watson Sr.") Cooper held visiting research positions at various institutions including the [Institute for Advanced Study](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_for_Advanced_Study "Institute for Advanced Study") in [Princeton, New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton,_New_Jersey "Princeton, New Jersey"), and at [CERN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CERN "CERN") (European Organization for Nuclear Research) in [Geneva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geneva "Geneva"), Switzerland.

Along with colleague [Charles Elbaum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Elbaum "Charles Elbaum"), he founded the tech company Nestor in 1975, which sought commercial applications for [artificial neural networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_neural_network "Artificial neural network"). Nestor partnered with [Intel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intel "Intel") to develop the [Ni1000](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ni1000 "Ni1000") neural network computer chip in 1994.

## Research

### Superconductivity

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d3/Bardeen_plaque_uiuc.jpg/250px-Bardeen_plaque_uiuc.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Bardeen_plaque_uiuc.jpg)

Plaque at the University of Illinois, commemorating the development of the [BCS theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BCS_theory "BCS theory") of [superconductivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superconductivity "Superconductivity")

While Cooper was a postdoc at Princeton, he was approached by [John Bardeen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Bardeen "John Bardeen"), a professor at the University of Illinois, and Bardeen's graduate student [John Robert Schrieffer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Robert_Schrieffer "John Robert Schrieffer"). Bardeen and Schrieffer were working on [superconductivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superconductivity "Superconductivity"), a topic which was new to Cooper, but he agreed to collaborate with them. Superconductivity had been [experimentally discovered in 1911](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_superconductivity "History of superconductivity"), but there was no theoretical explanation for the phenomenon. Cooper moved to Illinois as a postdoc to work with Bardeen.

After a year of theoretical investigation, Cooper developed the idea of a [quasiparticle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasiparticle "Quasiparticle") composed of two bound electrons, now known as a [Cooper pair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cooper_pair "Cooper pair"). Cooper published his concept of Cooper pairs in *[Physical Review](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_Review "Physical Review")* in September 1956. The movement of Cooper pairs through a low-temperature metal would be almost unimpeded, producing a very low [electrical resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_resistance "Electrical resistance"). After further development, Bardeen, Cooper and Schrieffer showed how this could produce superconductivity, publishing their theory in *Physical Reviews* in two papers during 1957. This theory became known as the [BCS theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BCS_theory "BCS theory"), after the authors' initials, and is widely accepted as the explanation for [conventional superconductivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conventional_superconductor "Conventional superconductor"). Bardeen, Schrieffer and Cooper were awarded the [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics") in 1972 for their theory.

### Neuroscience

After joining Brown University, Cooper became interested in [neuroscience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neuroscience "Neuroscience"), particularly the process of [learning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Learning "Learning"). In 1982, Cooper and two doctoral students, Elie Bienenstock and Paul Munro, published their theory of [synaptic plasticity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synaptic_plasticity "Synaptic plasticity") in *[The Journal of Neuroscience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Journal_of_Neuroscience "The Journal of Neuroscience")*. They estimated the weakening and strengthening of [synapses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synapse "Synapse") that could occur without saturation of the connections. As synapses saturate, electrical connections become less effective, thereby reducing the saturation. Connections therefore oscillate between saturation and unsaturation without reaching their limits. Their theory explained how the [visual cortex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visual_cortex "Visual cortex") works and how people learn to see. It became known as the [BCM theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BCM_theory "BCM theory"), after the authors' initials.

## Personal life

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b0/Leon_Cooper_with_wife_1972.jpg/250px-Leon_Cooper_with_wife_1972.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Leon_Cooper_with_wife_1972.jpg)

Cooper with his wife, Kay Allard, in 1972

Cooper first married Martha Kennedy, with whom he had two daughters. In 1969, he married for a second time, to Kay Allard.
He died at his home in [Providence, Rhode Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Providence,_Rhode_Island "Providence, Rhode Island"), on October 23, 2024, at the age of 94.

## Publications

Cooper was the author of [Science and Human Experience](http://www.cambridge.org/academic/subjects/physics/history-philosophy-and-foundations-physics/science-and-human-experience-values-culture-and-mind?format=HB) – a collection of essays, including previously unpublished material, on issues such as consciousness and the structure of space.
(Cambridge University Press, 2014).

Cooper also wrote an unconventional liberal-arts physics textbook, originally *An Introduction to the Meaning and Structure of Physics* (Harper and Row, 1968) and still in print in a somewhat condensed form as *Physics: Structure and Meaning* (Lebanon: New Hampshire, University Press of New England, 1992).
