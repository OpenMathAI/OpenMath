**Sir Nevill Francis Mott** (30 September 1905 – 8 August 1996) was a British [theoretical physicist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theoretical_physicist "Theoretical physicist") who won the [Nobel Prize for Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_for_Physics "Nobel Prize for Physics") in 1977 for his work on the electronic structure of magnetic and [disordered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amorphous_solid "Amorphous solid") systems, especially [amorphous semiconductors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amorphous_semiconductors "Amorphous semiconductors"). The Prize was shared with [Philip W. Anderson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_W._Anderson "Philip W. Anderson") and [John Van Vleck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Van_Vleck "John Van Vleck"). The three had conducted loosely related research. Mott and Anderson clarified the reasons why magnetic or amorphous materials can sometimes be metallic and sometimes insulating.

## Early life and education

Nevill Francis Mott was born on 30 September 1905 in [Leeds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leeds "Leeds"), England, the son of [Charles Francis Mott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Francis_Mott "Charles Francis Mott") and Lilian Mary Reynolds, a granddaughter of Sir [John Richardson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Richardson_(naturalist) "John Richardson (naturalist)"), and great granddaughter of [Sir John Henry Pelly, 1st Baronet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_John_Pelly,_1st_Baronet "Sir John Pelly, 1st Baronet"). Miss Reynolds was a Cambridge Mathematics Tripos graduate and at Cambridge was the best woman mathematician of her year. His parents met in the [Cavendish Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cavendish_Laboratory "Cavendish Laboratory"), when both were engaged in physics research under [J. J. Thomson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._J._Thomson "J. J. Thomson").

Nevill grew up first in the village of [Giggleswick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giggleswick "Giggleswick") in the [West Riding of Yorkshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Riding_of_Yorkshire "West Riding of Yorkshire"), where his father was Senior Science Master at [Giggleswick School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giggleswick_School "Giggleswick School"). His mother also taught Maths at the School. The family moved (due to his father's jobs) first to Staffordshire, then to Chester and finally Liverpool, where his father had been appointed Director of Education. Mott was at first educated at home by his mother. At age ten, he began formal education at [Clifton College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clifton_College "Clifton College") in Bristol, followed by study at [St John's College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_John's_College,_Cambridge "St John's College, Cambridge")—where he read the [Mathematics Tripos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_Mathematical_Tripos "Cambridge Mathematical Tripos"), supervised by [Ralph Fowler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ralph_Fowler "Ralph Fowler").

## Career and research

In 1929, Mott was appointed a lecturer in the [School of Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/School_of_Physics_and_Astronomy,_University_of_Manchester "School of Physics and Astronomy, University of Manchester") at the [University of Manchester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Manchester "University of Manchester"). He returned to Cambridge in 1930 as a Fellow of and lecturer at [Gonville and Caius College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gonville_and_Caius_College,_Cambridge "Gonville and Caius College, Cambridge"), and in 1933 moved to the [University of Bristol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Bristol "University of Bristol") as Melville Wills Professor of Theoretical Physics. In 1948, he became Henry Overton Wills Professor of Physics and Director of the Henry Herbert Wills Physical Laboratory at Bristol. In 1954, he was appointed [Cavendish Professor of Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cavendish_Professor_of_Physics "Cavendish Professor of Physics") at Cambridge, a position he held until 1971. He was instrumental in the painful cancellation of the planned [particle accelerator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_accelerator "Particle accelerator") because of its very high cost. He also served as Master of Gonville and Caius College, 1959–1966.

His early works were on the theoretical analysis of collisions in gases, notably the [collision with spin flip of an electron against a hydrogen atom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_line "Hydrogen line"), which would stimulate subsequent works by André Blandin and [Jun Kondo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jun_Kondo "Jun Kondo") about similar effects between [conduction electrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conduction_band "Conduction band"), as well as magnetic properties in metals. This sort of activity led Mott to writing two books. The first one, which was edited together with [Ian Sneddon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ian_Sneddon "Ian Sneddon"), gives a simple and clear description of quantum mechanics, with an emphasis on the [Schrödinger equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schrödinger_equation "Schrödinger equation") in [real space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Position_and_momentum_space "Position and momentum space"). The second describes atomic and electronic collisions in gases, using the rotational symmetry of [electronic states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_level "Energy level") in the [Hartree–Fock method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hartree–Fock_method "Hartree–Fock method").

But already in the middle of the 1930s, Mott's interests had broadened to include solid states, leading to two more books that would have a great impact on the development of the field in the years prior and after [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II"). In 1936, *Theory of the Properties of Metals and Alloys* (written together with H. Jones) describes a simplified framework which led to rapid progress.

The concept of [nearly free valence electrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nearly_free_electron_model "Nearly free electron model") in metallic alloys explained the special stability of the [Hume-Rothery phases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hume-Rothery_rules "Hume-Rothery rules") if the [Fermi sphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermi_surface "Fermi surface") of the *sp* [valence electron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valence_electron "Valence electron"), treated as free, would be scattered by the [Brillouin zone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brillouin_zone "Brillouin zone") boundaries of the atomic structure. The description of the impurities in metals by the [Thomas Fermi approximation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas–Fermi_model "Thomas–Fermi model") would explain why such impurities would not interact at long range. Finally the delocalisation of the [valence *d* electrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D_electron_count "D electron count") in [transitional metals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transition_metal "Transition metal") and alloys would explain the possibility for the [magnetic moments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_moment "Magnetic moment") of atoms to be expressed as fractions of [Bohr magnetons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bohr_magneton "Bohr magneton"), leading to [ferro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferromagnetism "Ferromagnetism") or [antiferromagnetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antiferromagnetism "Antiferromagnetism") coupling at short range. This last contribution, produced at the first international conference on magnetism, held in [Strasbourg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strasbourg "Strasbourg") in May 1939, reinforced similar points of view defended at the time in France by the future Nobel laureate [Louis Néel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Néel "Louis Néel"). In 1949, Mott suggested to [Jacques Friedel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques_Friedel "Jacques Friedel") to use the approach developed together with Marvey for a more accurate description of the [electric-field screening](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric-field_screening "Electric-field screening") of the impurity in a metal, leading to the characteristic long range charge oscillations. Friedel also used the concept developed in that book of virtual bound level to describe a situation when the atomic potential considered is not quite strong enough to create a (real) bound level of symmetry e ≠ o. The consequences of these remarks on the more exact approaches of cohesion in rp as well as d metals were mostly developed by his students in Orsay.

The second book, with [Ronald Wilfred Gurney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Wilfred_Gurney "Ronald Wilfred Gurney"), *On the Physical Chemistry of Solids* was more wide-ranging. It treated notably of the oxidation of metals at low temperatures, where it described the growth of the oxide layer as due to the electric field developed between the metal and absorbed oxygen ions, which could force the way of metallic or oxygen ions through a disordered oxide layer. The book also analysed the photographic reactions in ionic silver compound in terms of precipitation of silver ions into metallic clusters.

This second field had a direct and long lasting consequence on the research activity of John (Jack) Mitchell. Mott's accomplishments include explaining theoretically the effect of light on a [photographic emulsion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Film_stock "Film stock") (see [latent image](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latent_image "Latent image")). His work on oxidation, besides fostering new research in the field (notably by J. Bénard and [Nicolás Cabrera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolás_Cabrera_(physicist) "Nicolás Cabrera (physicist)")), was the root of the concept of the [band gap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Band_gap "Band gap") produced in semiconductors by gradients in the distribution of [donor and acceptor impurities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doping_(semiconductor) "Doping (semiconductor)").

During [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II"), Mott joined the "Army Cell" of [radar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radar "Radar") researchers. He was put in charge of getting the Army's [GL Mk. II radar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GL_Mk._II_radar "GL Mk. II radar") working in the presence of serious calibration problems that caused the measurements to change as the antenna tracked its targets. He solved this problem by designing a large metal wire mat that was built around the radars to provide a very flat reference plane.

During the war Mott worked on the role of plastic deformation in the progression of fracture cracks. When he returned to Bristol after the war, his having met and hired [Charles Frank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Frank_(physicist) "Charles Frank (physicist)") enabled the two of them to make considerable advances in the study of [dislocations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dislocation "Dislocation"), with the help of others such as [Frank Nabarro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Nabarro "Frank Nabarro") and [Alan Cottrell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_Cottrell "Alan Cottrell"). Bristol became an important centre of research in this topic, especially at the end of the 1940s. If Mott only produced early and somewhat minor contributions to that field, notably on alloy hardening with Nabarro and on the topology of a dislocation network lowering the apparent elastic constants of a crystal, there is no doubt that Mott's enthusiasm played its role in the three major steps forward in the field by Frank on crystal growth and plasticity and later, in Cambridge, by [Peter Hirsch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Hirsch_(metallurgist) "Peter Hirsch (metallurgist)") on thin film [electron microscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_microscopy "Electron microscopy").

At the same time, however, Mott gave a lot of thought to [electronic correlations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_correlation "Electronic correlation") and their possible role in [Verwey's compounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DLVO_theory "DLVO theory") such as nickel oxides which could switch from metals to nonmetallic insulators under various physical conditions - this is known as the [Mott transition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mott_transition "Mott transition"). The term [Mott insulator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mott_insulator "Mott insulator") is also named for him, as well as the [Mott polynomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mott_polynomials "Mott polynomials"), which he introduced.

## Personal life

Mott was married to Ruth Eleanor Horder, and had two daughters, Elizabeth and Alice. Alice was an educationist who worked with [Claus Moser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claus_Moser "Claus Moser") and married the mathematician Mike Crampin, who was a professor of mathematics at [The Open University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Open_University "The Open University"). Neville Mott retired to live near the Crampins in [Aspley Guise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aspley_Guise "Aspley Guise"), [Milton Keynes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milton_Keynes "Milton Keynes"), where he died on 8 August 1996 at the age of 90. His autobiography, *A Life in Science*, was published in 1986 by [Taylor & Francis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taylor_&_Francis "Taylor & Francis"). His great grandfather was Sir John Richardson, the arctic explorer.

## Publications

N. F. Mott revived the old *[Philosophical Magazine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophical_Magazine "Philosophical Magazine")* and transformed it into a lively publication essentially centred on the then-new field of solid state physics, attracting writers, readers and general interest on a wide scale. After receiving a paper on point defects in crystals by [Frederick Seitz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_Seitz "Frederick Seitz") that was obviously too long for the journal, Mott decided to create a new publication, [Advances in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advances_in_Physics "Advances in Physics"), for such review papers. Both publications are still active in 2017.
