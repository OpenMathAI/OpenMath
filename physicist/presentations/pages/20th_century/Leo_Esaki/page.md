**Leo Esaki** (born March 12, 1925) is a Japanese [physicist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physicist "Physicist") who shared the 1973 [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics") with [Ivar Giaever](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivar_Giaever "Ivar Giaever") and [Brian Josephson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brian_Josephson "Brian Josephson") for his work on [tunneling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_tunnelling "Quantum tunnelling") in [semiconductors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiconductor "Semiconductor"), which led to his invention of the [tunnel diode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tunnel_diode "Tunnel diode") that exploits this phenomenon. His research was done when he was with [Sony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sony "Sony"). He has also contributed in being a pioneer of semiconductor [superlattices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superlattice "Superlattice").

Since the death of [Yoichiro Nambu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoichiro_Nambu "Yoichiro Nambu") in 2015, Esaki is currently the oldest [Japanese Nobel laureate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_Nobel_laureate "Japanese Nobel laureate").

## Biography

Leo Esaki was born on March 12, 1925, in [Osaka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osaka "Osaka"), Japan. Esaki studied physics at Tokyo Imperial University (now the [University of Tokyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Tokyo "University of Tokyo")), where he received his [B.S.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B.S. "B.S.") in 1947, and his [Ph.D.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ph.D. "Ph.D.") in 1959. After graduating in 1947, he joined the Kobe Kogyo Corporation, before becoming chief physicist at Tokyo Tsushin Kogyo (now [Sony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sony "Sony")) in 1956.

In 1960, Esaki moved to the United States to join the [Thomas J. Watson Research Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_J._Watson_Research_Center "Thomas J. Watson Research Center") of [IBM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM "IBM"), where he was appointed an [IBM Fellow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM_Fellow "IBM Fellow") in 1967. In 1992, he returned to Japan, where he subsequently served as President of the [University of Tsukuba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Tsukuba "University of Tsukuba") until 1998.

## Research

### Tunneling

In 1957, Esaki recognized that when the [p–n junction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P–n_junction "P–n junction") width of [germanium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germanium "Germanium") is thinned, the [current–voltage characteristic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Current–voltage_characteristic "Current–voltage characteristic") is dominated by the influence of the [tunnel effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_tunnelling "Quantum tunnelling"). As a result, he discovered that as the voltage is increased, the current decreases inversely, indicating [negative resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negative_resistance "Negative resistance"). This discovery was the first demonstration of solid tunneling effects in physics, and it was the birth of the first [quantum electronic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_electronics "Quantum electronics") device, the [tunnel diode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tunnel_diode "Tunnel diode").

### Superlattices

In 1969, Esaki predicted that semiconductor [superlattices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superlattices "Superlattices") will be formed to induce a differential negative-resistance effect via an artificially one-dimensional periodic structural changes in semiconductor crystals. His unique "[molecular-beam epitaxy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular-beam_epitaxy "Molecular-beam epitaxy")" thin-film crystal growth method can be regulated quite precisely in ultrahigh vacuum. A 1987 comment by Esaki regarding the original paper notes:

> "The original version of the paper was rejected for publication by *[Physical Review](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_Review "Physical Review")* on the referee's unimaginative assertion that it was 'too speculative' and involved 'no new physics.' However, this proposal was quickly accepted by the Army Research Office..."

In 1972, Esaki realized his concept of superlattices in III-V group semiconductors. Later, this concept influenced many fields like metals and magnetic materials.

## Esaki's "five don'ts" rules

At the 1994 [Lindau Nobel Laureate Meetings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lindau_Nobel_Laureate_Meetings "Lindau Nobel Laureate Meetings"), Esaki suggested a list of "five don'ts" which anyone in realizing their creative potential should follow. Two months later, the chairman of the Nobel Committee for Physics [Carl Nordling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Nordling "Carl Nordling") incorporated the rules in his own speech.

1. Don't allow yourself to be trapped by your past experiences.
2. Don't allow yourself to become overly attached to any one authority in your field – the great professor, perhaps.
3. Don't hold on to what you don't need.
4. Don't avoid confrontation.
5. Don't forget your spirit of childhood curiosity.
