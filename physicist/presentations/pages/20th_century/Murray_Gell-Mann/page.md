**Murray Gell-Mann** ([/ˈmʌri ˈɡɛl ˈmæn/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English"); September 15, 1929 – May 24, 2019) was an American [theoretical physicist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theoretical_physicist "Theoretical physicist") who played a preeminent role in the development of the theory of [elementary particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_physics "Particle physics"). Gell-Mann introduced the concept of [quarks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quarks "Quarks") as the fundamental building blocks of the strongly interacting particles, and the [renormalization group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renormalization_group "Renormalization group") as a foundational element of [quantum field theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_field_theory "Quantum field theory") and [statistical mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_mechanics "Statistical mechanics"). Murray Gell-Mann received the 1969 [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics") for his contributions and discoveries concerning the classification of elementary particles and their interactions.

Gell-Mann played key roles in developing the concept of [chirality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chirality "Chirality") in the theory of the [weak interactions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weak_interaction "Weak interaction") and spontaneous [chiral symmetry breaking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chiral_symmetry_breaking "Chiral symmetry breaking") in the [strong interactions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strong_interaction "Strong interaction"), which controls the physics of the light [mesons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meson "Meson"). In the 1970s he was a co-inventor of [quantum chromodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_chromodynamics "Quantum chromodynamics") (QCD) which explains the confinement of quarks in mesons and [baryons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baryon "Baryon") and forms a large part of the Standard Model of elementary particles and forces.

## Life and education

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/97/Murray_Gell-Mann_1965.png/250px-Murray_Gell-Mann_1965.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Murray_Gell-Mann_1965.png)

Gell-Mann in 1965

Gell-Mann was born in Lower Manhattan to a family of [Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jews "Jews") immigrants from the [Austro-Hungarian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austria-Hungary "Austria-Hungary"), specifically from [Czernowitz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chernivtsi "Chernivtsi") in present-day [Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukraine "Ukraine"). His parents were Pauline (née Reichstein) and Arthur Isidore Gelman, who taught [English as a second language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_as_a_second_or_foreign_language "English as a second or foreign language"). Gell-Mann married J. Margaret Dow in 1955; they had a daughter and a son. Margaret died in 1981, and in 1992 he married [Marcia Southwick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcia_Southwick "Marcia Southwick"), whose son became his stepson.

Gell-Mann graduated [valedictorian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valedictorian "Valedictorian") from the [Columbia Grammar & Preparatory School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia_Grammar_&_Preparatory_School "Columbia Grammar & Preparatory School") aged 14 and subsequently entered [Yale College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yale_College "Yale College") as a member of [Jonathan Edwards College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonathan_Edwards_College "Jonathan Edwards College"). At Yale, he participated in the [William Lowell Putnam Mathematical Competition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Lowell_Putnam_Mathematical_Competition "William Lowell Putnam Mathematical Competition") and was on the team representing [Yale University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yale_University "Yale University") (along with [Murray Gerstenhaber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Murray_Gerstenhaber "Murray Gerstenhaber") and [Henry O. Pollak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_O._Pollak "Henry O. Pollak")) that won the second prize in 1947.

Gell-Mann graduated from Yale with a bachelor's degree in physics in 1948 and intended to pursue graduate studies in physics. He sought to remain in the [Ivy League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivy_League "Ivy League") for his graduate education and applied to [Princeton University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University "Princeton University") as well as [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University "Harvard University"). He was rejected by Princeton and accepted by Harvard, but the latter institution was unable to offer him needed financial assistance. He was then accepted by the [Massachusetts Institute of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massachusetts_Institute_of_Technology "Massachusetts Institute of Technology") (MIT) and received a letter from [Victor Weisskopf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victor_Weisskopf "Victor Weisskopf") urging him to attend MIT and become Weisskopf's research assistant. This would provide Gell-Mann with the financial assistance he required. Unaware of MIT's eminent status in physics research, Gell-Mann was "miserable" and said he first considered suicide.

Gell-Mann received his Ph.D. in physics from MIT in 1951 after completing a doctoral dissertation, titled "Coupling strength and nuclear reactions", under the supervision of Weisskopf. Subsequently, Gell-Mann was a postdoctoral fellow at the [Institute for Advanced Study](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_for_Advanced_Study "Institute for Advanced Study") at Princeton in 1951, and a visiting research professor at the [University of Illinois at Urbana–Champaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Illinois_at_Urbana–Champaign "University of Illinois at Urbana–Champaign") from 1952 to 1953. He was a visiting associate professor at [Columbia University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia_University "Columbia University") and an associate professor at the [University of Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Chicago "University of Chicago") in 1954–1955, before moving to the [California Institute of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California_Institute_of_Technology "California Institute of Technology"), where he taught from 1955 until he retired in 1993.

Gell-Mann died on May 24, 2019, at his home in Santa Fe, New Mexico.

## Professional life and controversies

Gell-Mann was the Robert Andrews Millikan Professor of Theoretical Physics Emeritus at [California Institute of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California_Institute_of_Technology "California Institute of Technology") as well as a university professor in the physics and astronomy department of the [University of New Mexico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_New_Mexico "University of New Mexico") in [Albuquerque, New Mexico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albuquerque,_New_Mexico "Albuquerque, New Mexico"), and the Presidential Professor of Physics and Medicine at the [University of Southern California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Southern_California "University of Southern California"). He was a member of the editorial board of the *[Encyclopædia Britannica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encyclopædia_Britannica "Encyclopædia Britannica")*.

Gell-Mann was on sabbatical at the *[Collège de France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collège_de_France "Collège de France")* for the academic year 1958–1959. Gell-Mann spent several periods at [CERN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CERN "CERN"), the laboratories of the European Organization for Nuclear Research in Geneva, Switzerland, including time as a fellow of the [John Simon Guggenheim Memorial Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Simon_Guggenheim_Memorial_Foundation "John Simon Guggenheim Memorial Foundation"). In 1972, he was on CERN's payroll as visiting professor. That same year, Gell-Mann was criticized for his involvement with [JASON](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JASON_(advisory_group) "JASON (advisory group)"), a scientific advisory group that supported the U.S. Department of Defense during the Vietnam War. His association with the group was the focus of protests in France and Italy.

In 1984 Gell-Mann was one of several co-founders of the [Santa Fe Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santa_Fe_Institute "Santa Fe Institute")—a non-profit theoretical research institute in [Santa Fe, New Mexico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santa_Fe,_New_Mexico "Santa Fe, New Mexico") intended to study various aspects of a [complex system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_system "Complex system") and disseminate the notion of a separate interdisciplinary study of [complexity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complexity "Complexity") theory.

In his 1994 popular book *The Quark and the Jaguar: Adventures in the Simple and the Complex*, Gell-Mann acknowledged financial support from [Jeffrey Epstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeffrey_Epstein "Jeffrey Epstein"), who contributed via the Santa Fe Institute.
In 2003, Gell-Mann also contributed a letter to *[The First Fifty Years](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeffrey_Epstein's_birthday_book "Jeffrey Epstein's birthday book")*, a collection of birthday greetings for Epstein. Years later, in 2011, Gell-Mann reportedly attended the "Mindshift Conference" on Epstein's private island, [Little Saint James](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_Saint_James "Little Saint James"). The gathering was organized by Epstein and science promoter [Al Seckel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al_Seckel "Al Seckel"). Gell-Mann's name also appeared in Epstein's so-called "black book," a personal address book listing Epstein's close contacts. In 2025, [Ghislaine Maxwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghislaine_Maxwell "Ghislaine Maxwell") testified that Gell-Mann had originally been introduced to Epstein through her.

[George Johnson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Johnson_(writer) "George Johnson (writer)") has written a [biography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biography "Biography") of Gell-Mann, *Strange Beauty: Murray Gell-Mann, and the Revolution in 20th-Century Physics* (1999), Although Gell-Mann himself criticized *Strange Beauty* for some inaccuracies, with one interviewer reporting him wincing at the mention of it, the book was acclaimed by a number of his colleagues. A revised second edition was published in 2023 by the Santa Fe Institute Press with a foreword by [Douglas Hofstadter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Douglas_Hofstadter "Douglas Hofstadter"). In 2012 Gell-Mann and his companion [Mary McFadden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_McFadden "Mary McFadden") published the book *Mary McFadden: A Lifetime of Design, Collecting, and Adventure*.

## Scientific contributions

In 1958, Gell-Mann, in collaboration with [Richard Feynman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Feynman "Richard Feynman"), in parallel with the independent team of [E. C. George Sudarshan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E._C._George_Sudarshan "E. C. George Sudarshan") and [Robert Marshak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Marshak "Robert Marshak"), discovered the [chiral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chirality_(physics) "Chirality (physics)") structures of the [weak interaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weak_interaction "Weak interaction") of physics and developed the V-A theory (vector minus axial vector theory). This work followed the experimental discovery of the [violation of parity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parity_(physics) "Parity (physics)") by [Chien-Shiung Wu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chien-Shiung_Wu "Chien-Shiung Wu"), as suggested theoretically by [Chen-Ning Yang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yang_Chen-Ning "Yang Chen-Ning") and [Tsung-Dao Lee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsung-Dao_Lee "Tsung-Dao Lee").

Gell-Mann's work in the 1950s involved recently discovered [cosmic ray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmic_ray "Cosmic ray") particles that came to be called [kaons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaon "Kaon") and [hyperons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperon "Hyperon"). Classifying these particles led him to propose that a [quantum number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_number "Quantum number"), called [strangeness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strangeness "Strangeness"), would be conserved by the [strong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strong_interaction "Strong interaction") and the [electromagnetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_interaction "Electromagnetic interaction") interactions, but not by the weak interaction. Another of Gell-Mann's ideas is the [Gell-Mann–Okubo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gell-Mann–Okubo_mass_formula "Gell-Mann–Okubo mass formula") formula, which was, initially, a formula based on empirical results, but was later explained by his [quark model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quark_model "Quark model"). Gell-Mann and [Abraham Pais](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraham_Pais "Abraham Pais") were involved in explaining this puzzling aspect of the [neutral kaon mixing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutral_kaon_mixing "Neutral kaon mixing").

Murray Gell-Mann's fortunate encounter with mathematician [Richard Earl Block](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Earl_Block "Richard Earl Block") at Caltech, in the fall of 1960, "enlightened" him to introduce a novel classification scheme, in 1961, for [hadrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadron "Hadron"). A similar scheme had been independently proposed by [Yuval Ne'eman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yuval_Ne'eman "Yuval Ne'eman"), and has come to be explained by the quark model. Gell-Mann referred to the scheme as the *[eightfold way](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eightfold_way_(physics) "Eightfold way (physics)")*, because of the *octets* of particles in the classification (the term is a reference to the [Eightfold Path](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_Eightfold_Path "Noble Eightfold Path") of [Buddhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhism "Buddhism")).

Gell-Mann, along with Maurice Lévy, developed the [sigma model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigma_model "Sigma model") of [pions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pion "Pion"), which describes low-energy pion interactions.

In 1964, Gell-Mann and, independently, [George Zweig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Zweig "George Zweig") went on to postulate the existence of [quarks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quark "Quark"), particles which make up the [hadrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadron "Hadron") of this scheme. The name "quark" was coined by Gell-Mann, and is a reference to the novel *[Finnegans Wake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finnegans_Wake "Finnegans Wake")*, by [James Joyce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Joyce "James Joyce") ("Three quarks for Muster Mark!" book 2, episode 4). Zweig had referred to the particles as "aces", but Gell-Mann's name caught on. Quarks, antiquarks, and [gluons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gluon "Gluon") were soon established as the underlying elementary objects in the study of the structure of hadrons. He was awarded a [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics") in 1969 for his contributions and discoveries concerning the classification of elementary particles and their interactions.

In the 1960s, he introduced [current algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Current_algebra "Current algebra") as a method of systematically exploiting symmetries to extract predictions from quark models, in the absence of reliable dynamical theory. This method led to model-independent [sum rules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sum_rule_in_quantum_mechanics "Sum rule in quantum mechanics") confirmed by experiment, and provided starting points underpinning the development of the [Standard Model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_Model "Standard Model") (SM), the widely accepted theory of elementary particles.

In 1972 Gell-Mann, together with [Harald Fritzsch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harald_Fritzsch "Harald Fritzsch"), [Heinrich Leutwyler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heinrich_Leutwyler "Heinrich Leutwyler") and [William A. Bardeen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_A._Bardeen "William A. Bardeen"), considered a Yang-Mills theory of "quark color," and coined the term [quantum chromodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_chromodynamics "Quantum chromodynamics") (QCD) as the [gauge theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauge_theory "Gauge theory") of the strong interaction. The [quark model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quark_model "Quark model") is a part of QCD, and it has been robust enough to accommodate in a natural fashion the discovery of new "[flavors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flavour_(particle_physics) "Flavour (particle physics)")" of quarks, which has superseded the eightfold way scheme.

Gell-Mann was responsible, with [Pierre Ramond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Ramond "Pierre Ramond") and [Richard Slansky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Slansky "Richard Slansky"), and independently of [Peter Minkowski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Minkowski "Peter Minkowski"), [Rabindra Mohapatra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rabindra_Mohapatra "Rabindra Mohapatra"), [Goran Senjanović](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goran_Senjanović "Goran Senjanović"), [Sheldon Glashow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheldon_Glashow "Sheldon Glashow"), and Tsutomu Yanagida, for proposing the [seesaw theory of neutrino masses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seesaw_mechanism "Seesaw mechanism"). This produces masses at the large scale in any theory with a right-handed neutrino. He is also known to have played a role in keeping [string theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/String_theory "String theory") alive through the 1970s and early 1980s, supporting that line of research at a time when it was a topic of niche interest.

Gell-Mann was a proponent of the [consistent histories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consistent_histories "Consistent histories") approach to understanding quantum mechanics, which he advocated in papers with [James Hartle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Hartle "James Hartle").

Gell-Mann's extensive interests outside of physics included [archaeology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaeology "Archaeology"), [numismatics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numismatics "Numismatics"), [birdwatching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birdwatching "Birdwatching") and [linguistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linguistics "Linguistics"). Along with [S. A. Starostin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sergei_Starostin "Sergei Starostin"), he established the [Evolution of Human Languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolution_of_Human_Languages "Evolution of Human Languages") project at the [Santa Fe Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santa_Fe_Institute "Santa Fe Institute"). As a [humanist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humanism "Humanism") and an [agnostic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agnosticism "Agnosticism"), Gell-Mann was a Humanist Laureate in the [International Academy of Humanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Academy_of_Humanism "International Academy of Humanism"). Novelist [Cormac McCarthy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cormac_McCarthy "Cormac McCarthy") saw Gell-Mann as a polymath who "knew more things about more things than anyone I've ever met...losing Murray is like losing the *[Encyclopædia Britannica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encyclopædia_Britannica "Encyclopædia Britannica")*."

- 1962 – [American Academy of Achievement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academy_of_Achievement "Academy of Achievement")'s Golden Plate Award
- 1964 – Elected member of the [American Academy of Arts and Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Academy_of_Arts_and_Sciences "American Academy of Arts and Sciences")
- 1966 – [Ernest Orlando Lawrence Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_Orlando_Lawrence_Award "Ernest Orlando Lawrence Award")
- 1967 – [Franklin Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franklin_Medal "Franklin Medal")
- 1968 – [National Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Academy_of_Sciences "National Academy of Sciences") – [John J. Carty Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_J._Carty_Award_for_the_Advancement_of_Science "John J. Carty Award for the Advancement of Science")
- 1969 – [Research Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Research_Corporation "Research Corporation") Award
- 1969 – [Nobel Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize "Nobel Prize") in Physics
- 1978 – Elected a [Foreign Member of the Royal Society (ForMemRS)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Fellows_of_the_Royal_Society_elected_in_1978 "List of Fellows of the Royal Society elected in 1978")
- 1988 – [United Nations Environment Programme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_Environment_Programme "United Nations Environment Programme") Roll of Honor for Environmental Achievement (The [Global 500](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_500_Roll_of_Honour "Global 500 Roll of Honour"))
- 1993 – Elected member of [The American Philosophical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_American_Philosophical_Society "The American Philosophical Society")
- 2005 – [Albert Einstein Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Einstein_Medal "Albert Einstein Medal")
- 2005 – [American Humanist Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Humanist_Association "American Humanist Association") – Humanist of the Year
- 2014 – Helmholtz-Medal of the Berlin-Brandenburg Academy of Sciences and Humanities

Universities that gave Gell-Mann [honorary doctorates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honorary_doctorate "Honorary doctorate") include [Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_University "Cambridge University"), [Columbia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia_University "Columbia University"), the [University of Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Chicago "University of Chicago"), [Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford_University "Oxford University") and [Yale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yale "Yale").
