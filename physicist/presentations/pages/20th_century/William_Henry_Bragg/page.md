**Sir William Henry Bragg** (2 July 1862 – 12 March 1942) was a British [X-ray crystallographer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray_crystallographer "X-ray crystallographer") who uniquely shared a [Nobel Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize#Specially_distinguished_laureates "Nobel Prize") with his son [Lawrence Bragg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawrence_Bragg "Lawrence Bragg") – the 1915 [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics") "for their services in the analysis of [crystal structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal_structure "Crystal structure") by means of [X-rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray "X-ray")," an important step in the development of [X-ray crystallography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray_crystallography "X-ray crystallography").

## Early life and education

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/16/Market_Harborough_Grammar_School.jpg/250px-Market_Harborough_Grammar_School.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Market_Harborough_Grammar_School.jpg)

The Old Grammar School, [Market Harborough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Market_Harborough "Market Harborough"), which has a plaque inside noting Bragg's attendance.

William Henry Bragg was born on 2 July 1862 in [Westward](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westward,_Cumbria "Westward, Cumbria"), [Cumberland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cumberland "Cumberland"), England, the son of Robert John Bragg, a merchant marine officer and farmer, and Mary Wood, a clergyman's daughter. His mother died when he was age 7, and was raised by his uncle (also named William Bragg) in [Market Harborough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Market_Harborough "Market Harborough"). He was educated at the [Grammar School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Smyth_Academy "Robert Smyth Academy") there, at [King William's College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_William's_College "King William's College") on the [Isle of Man](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isle_of_Man "Isle of Man"), and—having won an [exhibition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exhibition_(scholarship) "Exhibition (scholarship)")—at [Trinity College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College,_Cambridge "Trinity College, Cambridge"). He graduated in 1884 as third [Wrangler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wrangler_(University_of_Cambridge) "Wrangler (University of Cambridge)"), and in 1885 earned [First Class Honours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Class_Honours "First Class Honours") in the mathematical [Tripos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tripos "Tripos").

## Career and research

### University of Adelaide

In 1885, Bragg was appointed Elder Professor of Mathematics and Physics at the [University of Adelaide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Adelaide "University of Adelaide") in Australia, and started work there in early 1886. Being a skilled mathematician, at that time he had limited knowledge of physics, most of which was in the form of applied mathematics he had learnt at Trinity College. Also at that time, there were only about a hundred students doing full courses at Adelaide, of whom less than a handful belonged to the science school, whose deficient teaching facilities he improved by apprenticing himself to a firm of instrument makers. He was an able and popular lecturer; he encouraged the formation of the student union, and the attendance, free of charge, of science teachers at his lectures.

Bragg's interest in physics developed, particularly in the field of [electromagnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetism "Electromagnetism").  In 1895, he was visited by [Ernest Rutherford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_Rutherford "Ernest Rutherford"), en route from New Zealand to Cambridge; this was the commencement of a lifelong friendship. He had a keen interest in the new discovery of X-rays by [Wilhelm Röntgen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilhelm_Röntgen "Wilhelm Röntgen"). On 29 May 1896 at Adelaide, he demonstrated before a meeting of local doctors the application of "X-rays to reveal structures that were otherwise invisible". [Samuel Barbour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Barbour "Samuel Barbour"), senior chemist of [F. H. Faulding & Co.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F._H._Faulding_&_Co. "F. H. Faulding & Co."), an Adelaide pharmaceutical manufacturer, supplied the necessary apparatus in the form of a [Crookes tube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crookes_tube "Crookes tube"), a glass discharge tube. The tube had been obtained at Leeds, England, where Barbour visited the firm of [Reynolds and Branson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reynolds_and_Branson "Reynolds and Branson"), a manufacturer of photographic and laboratory equipment. Barbour returned to Adelaide in April 1896. Barbour had conducted his own experiments shortly after return to Australia, but results were limited due to limited battery power. At the University, the tube was attached to an induction coil and a battery borrowed from Sir [Charles Todd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Todd_(pioneer) "Charles Todd (pioneer)"), Bragg's father-in-law. The [induction coil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Induction_coil "Induction coil") was utilised to produce the [electric spark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_spark "Electric spark") necessary for Bragg and Barbour to "generate short bursts of X-rays". The audience was favorably impressed. He availed himself as a test subject, in the manner of Röntgen and allowed an X-ray photograph to be taken of his hand. The image of the fingers in his hand revealed "an old injury to one of his fingers sustained when using the turnip chopping machine on his father's farm in [Cumbria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cumbria "Cumbria")".

As early as 1895, Bragg was working on [wireless telegraphy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wireless_telegraphy "Wireless telegraphy"), though public lectures and demonstrations focussed on his X-ray research which would later lead to his Nobel Prize. In a hurried visit by Rutherford, he was reported as working on a Hertzian oscillator. There were many common practical threads to the two technologies and he was ably assisted in the laboratory by Arthur Lionel Rogers who manufactured much of the equipment. On 21 September 1897 Bragg gave the first recorded public demonstration of the working of wireless telegraphy in Australia during a lecture meeting at the University of Adelaide as part of the Public Teachers' Union conference. Bragg departed Adelaide in December 1897, and spent all of 1898 on a 12-month leave of absence, touring Great Britain and Europe and during this time visited Marconi and inspected his wireless facilities. He returned to Adelaide in early March 1899, and already on 13 May 1899, Bragg and his father-in-law, Sir Charles Todd, were conducting preliminary tests of wireless telegraphy with a transmitter at the Observatory and a receiver on the South Road (about 200 metres). Experiments continued throughout the southern winter of 1899 and the range was progressively extended to Henley Beach. In September the work was extended to two way transmissions with the addition of a second induction coil loaned by [Mr. James Oddie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Oddie "James Oddie") of Ballarat. It was desired to extend the experiments cross a sea path and Todd was interested in connecting Cape Spencer and Althorpe Island, but local costs were considered prohibitive while the charges for patented equipment from the [Marconi Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marconi_Company "Marconi Company") were exorbitant. At the same time Bragg's interests were leaning towards X-rays and practical work in wireless in South Australia was largely dormant for the next decade.

The turning-point in Bragg's career came in 1904 when he gave the presidential address to section A of the [Australasian Association for the Advancement of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australasian_Association_for_the_Advancement_of_Science "Australasian Association for the Advancement of Science") in [Dunedin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dunedin "Dunedin"), New Zealand, on "Some Recent Advances in the Theory of the Ionization of Gases". This idea was followed up "in a brilliant series of researches" which, within three years, earned him a fellowship of the [Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society "Royal Society") of London. This paper was also the origin of his first book *Studies in Radioactivity* (1912). Soon after the delivery of his 1904 address, some [radium bromide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radium_bromide "Radium bromide") was made available to Bragg for experimentation. In December 1904 his paper "On the Absorption of α Rays and on the Classification of the α Rays from Radium" appeared in the *[Philosophical Magazine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophical_Magazine "Philosophical Magazine")*, and in the same issue a paper "On the Ionization Curves of Radium", written in collaboration with his student [Richard Kleeman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Kleeman "Richard Kleeman"), also appeared.

At the end of 1908, Bragg returned to England. During his 23 years in Australia "he had seen the number of students at the University of Adelaide almost quadruple, and had a full share in the development of its excellent science school." He had returned to England on the maiden voyage of the [SS *Waratah*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SS_Waratah "SS Waratah"), a ship which vanished at sea on its second voyage the next year. He had been alarmed at the ship's tendency to list during his voyage, and had concluded that the ship's [metacentre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metacentric_height "Metacentric height") was just below her [centre of gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centre_of_gravity "Centre of gravity"). In 1911, he testified his belief that the *Waratah* was unstable at the Inquiry into the ship's disappearance.

There is a bust of Bragg in [North Terrace, Adelaide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Terrace,_Adelaide "North Terrace, Adelaide"), South Australia.

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/38/X-ray_spectrometer%2C_1912._%289660569929%29.jpg/250px-X-ray_spectrometer%2C_1912._%289660569929%29.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:X-ray_spectrometer,_1912._(9660569929).jpg)

X-ray spectrometer developed by Bragg.

From 1909 to 1915, Bragg was Cavendish Professor of Physics at the [University of Leeds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Leeds "University of Leeds"). He continued his work on X-rays with much success. He invented the [X-ray spectrometer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray_spectrometer "X-ray spectrometer") and with his son, [Lawrence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawrence_Bragg "Lawrence Bragg"), then a research student at Cambridge, founded the new science of [X-ray crystallography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray_crystallography "X-ray crystallography"), the analysis of crystal structure using [X-ray diffraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray_diffraction "X-ray diffraction").

### World War I

Both of his sons, Lawrence and Robert, were called into the army after the [First World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_World_War "First World War") broke out in 1914 . The following year, Bragg was appointed [Quain Professor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quain_Professor "Quain Professor") of Physics at [University College London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_College_London "University College London"). He had to wait for almost a year to contribute to the war effort; in July 1915, he was appointed to the [Board of Invention and Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Board_of_Invention_and_Research "Board of Invention and Research") set up by the Admiralty. In September, his younger son Robert died of wounds at [Gallipoli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gallipoli_campaign "Gallipoli campaign"). In November, he shared the [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics") with Lawrence. The Navy was struggling to prevent sinkings by unseen, submerged [U-boats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U-boat "U-boat"). The scientists recommended that the best tactic was to listen for the submarines. The Navy had a [hydrophone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrophone "Hydrophone") research establishment at [Aberdour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aberdour "Aberdour"), Scotland, staffed with navy men. In November 1915, two young physicists were added to its staff. Bowing to outside pressure to use science, in July 1916, the Admiralty appointed Bragg as scientific director at Aberdour, assisted by three additional young physicists. They developed an improved directional hydrophone, which finally convinced the Admiralty of their usefulness. Late in 1916, Bragg with his small group moved to [Harwich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harwich "Harwich"), where the staff was enlarged and they had access to a submarine for tests. In France, where scientists had been mobilized since the beginning of the war, the physicist [Paul Langevin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Langevin "Paul Langevin") made a major stride with echolocation, generating intense sound pulses with quartz sheets oscillated at high frequency, which were then used as microphones to listen for echoes. Quartz was usable when [vacuum tubes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacuum_tube "Vacuum tube") became available at the end of 1917 to amplify the faint signals. The British made [sonar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sonar "Sonar") practicable by using mosaics of small quartz bits rather than slices from a large crystal. In January 1918, Bragg moved into the Admiralty as head of scientific research in the anti-submarine division. By war's end, British vessels were being equipped with [sonar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sonar "Sonar") manned by trained listeners.

Inspired by Lawrence's methods for locating enemy guns by [the sound of their firing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sound_ranging "Sound ranging"), the output from six microphones miles apart along the coast were recorded on moving photographic film. [Sound ranging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artillery_sound_ranging "Artillery sound ranging") is much more accurate in the sea than in the turbulent atmosphere. They were able to localize the sites of distant explosions, which were used to obtain the precise positions of British warships and of minefields.

After the war, Bragg returned to University College London, where he continued to work on crystal analysis.

### Royal Institution

In 1923, Bragg was appointed [Fullerian Professor of Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fullerian_Professor_of_Chemistry "Fullerian Professor of Chemistry") at the [Royal Institution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Institution "Royal Institution") and Director of the Davy Faraday Research Laboratory. This institution was practically rebuilt in 1929–1930 and, under Bragg's directorship, many valuable papers were issued from the laboratory. In 1919, 1923, 1925, and 1931, he was invited to deliver the [Royal Institution Christmas Lecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Institution_Christmas_Lectures "Royal Institution Christmas Lectures") on *The World of Sound*; *Concerning the Nature of Things,* *Old Trades and New Knowledge* and *The Universe of Life* respectively.

### Royal Society and World War II

In 1935, Bragg was elected [President of the Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_of_the_Royal_Society "President of the Royal Society"). The physiologist [A. V. Hill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archibald_Hill "Archibald Hill") was biological secretary and soon A. C. G. Egerton became physical secretary. During World War I, all three had stood by for frustrating months before their skills were employed for the war effort. Now the cause of science was strengthened by the report of a high-level Army committee on lessons learned in the last war; their first recommendation was to "keep abreast of modern scientific developments". Anticipating another war, the Ministry of Labour was persuaded to accept Hill as a consultant on scientific manpower. The Royal Society compiled a register of qualified men. They proposed a small committee on science to advise the Committee on Imperial Defence, but this was rejected. Finally in 1940, as his term ended, a scientific advisory committee to the War Cabinet was appointed. He was among the 2,300 names of prominent persons listed on the Nazis' [Black Book](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Black_Book_(list) "The Black Book (list)"), of those who were to be arrested on the invasion of Great Britain and turned over to the [Gestapo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gestapo "Gestapo").

## Personal life and death

In 1889, in [Adelaide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adelaide "Adelaide"), Bragg married Gwendoline Todd, a skilled water-colour painter and the daughter of astronomer [Charles Todd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Todd_(astronomer) "Charles Todd (astronomer)"). They had three children; Gwendolen, [Lawrence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawrence_Bragg "Lawrence Bragg"), and Robert. Gwendolen married architect [Alban Caroe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alban_Caroe "Alban Caroe"). Bragg taught Lawrence at the [University of Adelaide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Adelaide "University of Adelaide"), and Robert was killed in the [Battle of Gallipoli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Gallipoli "Battle of Gallipoli").

Bragg played tennis and golf, and as a founding member of the [North Adelaide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Adelaide_Lacrosse_Club "North Adelaide Lacrosse Club") and [Adelaide University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adelaide_University_Lacrosse_Club "Adelaide University Lacrosse Club") lacrosse clubs, contributed to the introduction of [lacrosse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lacrosse "Lacrosse") to South Australia. He was also the Secretary of the Adelaide University Chess Association.

Bragg died of heart failure on 12 March 1942 in [London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London "London") at the age of 79.

## Commemoration

The [Electoral district of Bragg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electoral_district_of_Bragg "Electoral district of Bragg") in the [South Australian House of Assembly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Australian_House_of_Assembly "South Australian House of Assembly") was created in 1970, and was named after both William and Lawrence Bragg. [Bragg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg_(crater) "Bragg (crater)") crater on the Moon was named in his honour in 1970.

The lecture theatre of [King William's College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_William's_College "King William's College") (KWC) is named in memory of Bragg; the Sixth-Form invitational literary debating society at KWC, the Bragg Society, is also named in his memory. One of the school "Houses" at Robert Smyth School, [Market Harborough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Market_Harborough "Market Harborough"), Leicester, is named "Bragg" in memory of him being a student there. Since 1992, the [Australian Institute of Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_Institute_of_Physics "Australian Institute of Physics") has awarded the [Bragg Gold Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bragg_Gold_Medal "Bragg Gold Medal") for excellence in physics for the best Ph.D. thesis by a student at an Australian university. The two sides of the medal contain the images of Sir William Henry and his son Sir Lawrence Bragg.

The Experimental Technique Centre at [Brunel University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brunel_University "Brunel University") is named the Bragg Building. The Sir William Henry Bragg Building at the [University of Leeds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Leeds "University of Leeds") opened in 2021.

In 1962, the Bragg Laboratories were constructed at the [University of Adelaide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Adelaide "University of Adelaide") to commemorate 100 years since the birth of Sir William H. Bragg.

The Australian Bragg Centre for Proton Therapy and Research also in Adelaide, Australia was completed in late 2023. It is named for both father and son and offers radiation therapy for cancer patients.

In August 2013, Bragg's relative, the broadcaster [Melvyn Bragg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melvyn_Bragg "Melvyn Bragg"), presented a BBC Radio 4 programme "Bragg on the Braggs" on the 1915 [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics") winners.
