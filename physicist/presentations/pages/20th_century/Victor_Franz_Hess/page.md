**Victor Franz Hess** (German: [[ˈvɪktɔʁ ˈfʁants ˈhɛs]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Standard_German "Help:IPA/Standard German"); 24 June 1883 – 17 December 1964) was an Austrian–American experimental [physicist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physicist "Physicist") who shared the 1936 [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics") with [Carl David Anderson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_David_Anderson "Carl David Anderson") for his discovery of [cosmic rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmic_rays "Cosmic rays").

## Education

Victor Franz Hess was born on 24 June 1883 at Waldstein Castle in [Deutschfeistritz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deutschfeistritz "Deutschfeistritz"), [Styria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duchy_of_Styria "Duchy of Styria"), the son of Vinzens Hess and Serafine Edle von Grossbauer-Waldstätt. His father was a royal forester in Prince [Louis of Oettingen-Wallerstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_of_Oettingen-Wallerstein "Louis of Oettingen-Wallerstein")'s service.

Hess attended Graz-Gymnasium from 1893 to 1901. He then studied at the [University of Graz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Graz "University of Graz") from 1901 to 1905, and received his [Ph.D.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ph.D. "Ph.D.") in 1906 from the [University of Vienna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Vienna "University of Vienna"), where he stayed to do postdoctoral research until 1910.

## Career

In 1910, Hess became an assistant to [Stefan Meyer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stefan_Meyer_(physicist) "Stefan Meyer (physicist)") at the [Institute for Radium Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_for_Radium_Research,_Vienna "Institute for Radium Research, Vienna") in Vienna. In 1920, he was appointed Extraordinary Professor of Experimental Physics at the [University of Graz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Graz "University of Graz"). In 1921, he took a leave of absence to become Director of the Research Laboratory at the [United States Radium Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Radium_Corporation "United States Radium Corporation") in New Jersey, as well as Consulting Physicist for the [United States Bureau of Mines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Bureau_of_Mines "United States Bureau of Mines") in Washington, D.C. In 1923, he returned to Graz, where he was appointed Ordinary Professor of Experimental Physics in 1925. In 1931, he was appointed Director of the Institute of Radiology at the [University of Innsbruck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Innsbruck "University of Innsbruck").

In 1937, Hess returned to the University of Graz to become Director of the Institute of Physics. The following year, [Austria was annexed by Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anschluss "Anschluss"), and Hess was dismissed from his post. He then moved to the United States, and was appointed Professor of Physics at [Fordham University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fordham_University "Fordham University") in New York City, where he remained until his retirement in 1958. He became a [naturalized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naturalized "Naturalized") U.S. citizen in 1944.

## Discovery of cosmic rays

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/5d/Hessballon.jpg/250px-Hessballon.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Hessballon.jpg)

Hess (center) on the return from his balloon flight, 1912

For many years, scientists had been puzzled by the levels of [ionizing radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionizing_radiation "Ionizing radiation") measured in the atmosphere. The assumption at the time was that the radiation would decrease as the distance from the earth, the then assumed source of the radiation, increased. The [electroscopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electroscope "Electroscope") previously used gave an approximate measurement of the radiation but indicated that at greater altitude in the atmosphere the level of radiation might actually be higher than that on the ground. He approached this mystery first by greatly increasing the precision of the measuring equipment, and then by personally taking the equipment aloft in a balloon. He systematically measured the radiation at altitudes up to 5.3 kilometres (3.3 mi) during 1911–1912. The daring flights were made both by day and during the night, at significant risk to himself.

The result of Hess' meticulous work was published in the *Proceedings of the Austrian Academy of Sciences*, and showed the level of radiation decreased up to an altitude of about 1 kilometre (0.6 mi), but above that the level increased considerably, with the radiation detected at 5 kilometres (3.1 mi), being about twice that at sea level. His conclusion was that there was radiation penetrating the atmosphere from outer space, and his discovery was confirmed by [Robert Millikan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Millikan "Robert Millikan"), who gave the radiation the name *[cosmic rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmic_ray "Cosmic ray")*. His discovery opened the door to many new discoveries in [particle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_physics "Particle physics") and [nuclear physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_physics "Nuclear physics"). In particular, both the [positron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positron "Positron") and the [muon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muon "Muon") were first discovered in cosmic rays by [Carl David Anderson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_David_Anderson "Carl David Anderson"). In 1936, Hess and Anderson shared the [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics") for their discoveries of cosmic rays and the positron, respectively.

## Personal life and death

In 1920, Hess married Marie Bertha Warner Breisky, who died of cancer in 1955. The same year, he married Elizabeth M. Hoenke, who nursed Marie at the end of her life.

Hess was a practicing [Roman Catholic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Catholic "Roman Catholic"). In 1946, he wrote on the topic of the [relationship between science and religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relationship_between_science_and_religion "Relationship between science and religion") in his article "My Faith", in which he explained why he believed in God.

Hess died of [Parkinson's disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parkinson's_disease "Parkinson's disease") on 17 December 1964 in [Mount Vernon, New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Vernon,_New_York "Mount Vernon, New York"), at the age of 81.
