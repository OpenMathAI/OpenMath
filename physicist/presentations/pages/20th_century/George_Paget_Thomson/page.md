**Sir George Paget Thomson** (3 May 1892 – 10 September 1975) was a British experimental [physicist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physicist "Physicist") who shared the 1937 [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics") with [Clinton Davisson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clinton_Davisson "Clinton Davisson") "for their experimental discovery of the [diffraction of electrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_diffraction "Electron diffraction") by crystals." His father, [J. J. Thomson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._J._Thomson "J. J. Thomson"), won the Nobel Prize in 1906 "for his theoretical and experimental investigations on the [conduction of electricity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conduction_of_electricity "Conduction of electricity") by gases". It has been said that the elder Thomson won the Nobel for showing the electron is a particle, the younger for showing it is a wave.

## Education and military service

George Paget Thomson was born on 3 May 1892 in [Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge "Cambridge"), England, the son of physicist and Nobel laureate [J. J. Thomson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._J._Thomson "J. J. Thomson") and Rose Elisabeth Paget, the daughter of [George Edward Paget](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Edward_Paget "George Edward Paget").

Thomson attended [The Perse School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Perse_School "The Perse School"), before going on to read mathematics and physics at [Trinity College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College,_Cambridge "Trinity College, Cambridge"). After graduating in 1913, he went to the [Cavendish Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cavendish_Laboratory "Cavendish Laboratory") to do research under his father, until the outbreak of [World War I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I "World War I") the following year, when he was commissioned into the [Queen's Royal West Surrey Regiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen's_Royal_West_Surrey_Regiment "Queen's Royal West Surrey Regiment"). After brief service in France, he transferred to the [Royal Flying Corps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Flying_Corps "Royal Flying Corps") the following year to undertake research on [aerodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerodynamics "Aerodynamics") at the Royal Aircraft Establishment at [Farnborough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farnborough_Airfield "Farnborough Airfield") and elsewhere. He resigned from his commission as a [captain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Captain_(land) "Captain (land)") in 1920.

## Career and research

In 1919, Thomson became a Fellow of and a lecturer at [Corpus Christi College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corpus_Christi_College,_Cambridge "Corpus Christi College, Cambridge"). In 1922, he was appointed Professor of Natural Philosophy at the [University of Aberdeen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Aberdeen "University of Aberdeen") in Scotland.

Whereas Thomson's father, [J. J. Thomson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._J._Thomson "J. J. Thomson") (winner of the 1906 Nobel Prize in Physics), had seen the [electron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron "Electron") as a [particle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elementary_particle "Elementary particle"), the son demonstrated that the electron could be [diffracted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffraction "Diffraction") like a [wave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave "Wave"). By scattering electrons through thin metallic films (3 × 10−8 m thick) with known crystal structures, such as aluminium, gold and platinum, he found the dimensions of the observed diffraction patterns. In each case, his observed diffractions were within 5 percent of the predicted values given by [Louis de Broglie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_de_Broglie "Louis de Broglie")'s wave theory. This discovery provided further evidence for the principle of [wave–particle duality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave–particle_duality "Wave–particle duality"), which had first been posited by de Broglie in the 1920s as what is often called the [de Broglie hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Broglie_hypothesis "De Broglie hypothesis").

In 1937, Thomson was awarded the [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics") for his work in discovering the [wave-like properties of matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matter_wave "Matter wave"). He shared the prize with the American physicist [Clinton Davisson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clinton_Davisson "Clinton Davisson"), who had made the same discovery independently.

In 1929, Thomson became a non-resident lecturer at [Cornell University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornell_University "Cornell University") in [Ithaca, New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ithaca,_New_York "Ithaca, New York"). In 1930, he was appointed Professor of Physics at [Imperial College London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_College_London "Imperial College London"). In the late 1930s and during [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II"), he specialised in [nuclear physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_physics "Nuclear physics"), concentrating on practical military applications. In particular, he was the chairman of the crucial [MAUD Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MAUD_Committee "MAUD Committee") in 1940–1941 that concluded that an [atomic bomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_bomb "Atomic bomb") was feasible. In later life, he continued this work on nuclear energy, but also wrote works on [aerodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerodynamics "Aerodynamics") and the value of science in society.

## Later life and death

From 1952 to 1962, Thomson served as [Master of Corpus Christi College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master_of_Corpus_Christi_College,_Cambridge "Master of Corpus Christi College, Cambridge"). In 1964, the college honoured his tenure with the George Thomson Building, a work of modernist architecture on the college's [Leckhampton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leckhampton,_Corpus_Christi_College,_Cambridge "Leckhampton, Corpus Christi College, Cambridge") campus.

Thomson gave the address "Two aspects of science" as President of the [British Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Science_Association "British Science Association") for 1959–1960.

Thomson died on 10 September 1975 in [Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge "Cambridge") at the age of 83. He is buried in [Grantchester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grantchester "Grantchester") parish churchyard to the south of Cambridge.

## Family

In 1924, Thomson married Kathleen Buchanan Smith, the daughter of [George Adam Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Adam_Smith "George Adam Smith"), who served as Principal of the [University of Aberdeen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Aberdeen "University of Aberdeen") (1909–1935). They had two sons and two daughters. Kathleen died in 1941.

One of their sons, [John Thomson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Thomson_(diplomat) "John Thomson (diplomat)") (1927–2018), became a senior diplomat who served as High Commissioner to India (1977–1982) and as Permanent Representative to the United Nations (1982–1987). Their grandson, [Adam Thomson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adam_Thomson_(diplomat) "Adam Thomson (diplomat)") (born 1955), also became a senior diplomat, serving as High Commissioner to Pakistan (2010–2013) and as Permanent Representative to NATO (2014–2016). Another son, David Paget Thomson (1931–2022), was a merchant banker. One daughter, Lillian Clare Thomson (born 1929), married the South African economist and mountaineer [Johannes de Villiers Graaff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johannes_de_Villiers_Graaff "Johannes de Villiers Graaff").
