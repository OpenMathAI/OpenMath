**Nicolaas Bloembergen** (March 11, 1920 – September 5, 2017) was a Dutch–American [physicist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physicist "Physicist") recognized for his work in developing driving principles behind [nonlinear optics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonlinear_optics "Nonlinear optics") for [laser spectroscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laser_spectroscopy "Laser spectroscopy"). During his career, he was a professor at [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University "Harvard University") and later at the [University of Arizona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Arizona "University of Arizona") and at Leiden University in 1973 (as Lorentz Professor).

Bloembergen shared the 1981 [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics") along with [Arthur Schawlow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Schawlow "Arthur Schawlow") and [Kai Siegbahn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kai_Siegbahn "Kai Siegbahn") because their work "has had a profound effect on our present knowledge of the constitution of matter" through the use of [laser spectroscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laser_spectroscopy "Laser spectroscopy"). In particular, Bloembergen was singled out because he "founded a new field of science we now call [non-linear optics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-linear_optics "Non-linear optics")" by mixing "two or more beams of laser light... in order to produce laser light of a different wave length" and thus significantly broaden the laser spectroscopy frequency band.

## Education

Nicolaas Bloembergen was born on March 11, 1920, in [Dordrecht](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dordrecht "Dordrecht"), Netherlands, where his father was a chemical engineer and executive. He had five siblings, with his brother [Auke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auke_Bloembergen "Auke Bloembergen") later becoming a legal scholar. Bloembergen entered [Utrecht University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utrecht_University "Utrecht University") in 1938 to study physics, receiving his [C.Phil.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Candidate_of_Philosophy "Candidate of Philosophy") in 1941 and his *[doctorandus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctorandus "Doctorandus")* in 1943. In 1943, during the German occupation of the Netherlands, the German authorities closed the University and Bloembergen spent two years in hiding.

Bloembergen left the war-ravaged Netherlands in 1945 to pursue graduate studies at [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University "Harvard University") under Professor [Edward Mills Purcell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Mills_Purcell "Edward Mills Purcell"). Through Purcell, Bloembergen was part of the prolific academic lineage tree of [J. J. Thomson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._J._Thomson "J. J. Thomson"), which includes many other Nobel Laureates, beginning with Thomson himself (Physics Nobel, 1906) and [Lord Rayleigh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_William_Strutt,_3rd_Baron_Rayleigh "John William Strutt, 3rd Baron Rayleigh") (Physics Nobel, 1904), [Ernest Rutherford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_Rutherford "Ernest Rutherford") (Chemistry Nobel 1908), [Owen Richardson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Owen_Richardson "Owen Richardson") (Physics Nobel, 1928), and finally Purcell (Physics, Nobel 1952). Bloembergen's other influences include [John Van Vleck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Van_Vleck "John Van Vleck") (Physics Nobel, 1977) and [Percy Bridgman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Percy_Bridgman "Percy Bridgman") (Physics Nobel, 1946).

Six weeks before his arrival, Purcell and his graduate students Torrey and Pound discovered [nuclear magnetic resonance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_magnetic_resonance "Nuclear magnetic resonance") (NMR). Bloembergen was hired to develop the first NMR machine. At Harvard he attended lectures by [Schwinger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julian_Schwinger "Julian Schwinger"), [Van Vleck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Hasbrouck_Van_Vleck "John Hasbrouck Van Vleck"), and [Kemble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edwin_C._Kemble "Edwin C. Kemble"). Bloembergen's NMR systems are the predecessors of modern-day [MRI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_resonance_imaging "Magnetic resonance imaging") machines, which are used to examine internal organs and tissues. Bloembergen's research on NMR led to an interest in [masers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maser "Maser"), which were introduced in 1953 and are the predecessors of [lasers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laser "Laser").

Bloembergen returned to the Netherlands in 1947, and submitted his doctoral thesis, *Nuclear Magnetic Relaxation*, at [Leiden University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leiden_University "Leiden University"). This was because he had completed all the preliminary examinations in the Netherlands, and [Cornelis Jacobus Gorter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornelis_Jacobus_Gorter "Cornelis Jacobus Gorter") of Leiden offered him a [postdoctoral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postdoctoral_researcher "Postdoctoral researcher") appointment there. He received his [Ph.D.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Philosophy "Doctor of Philosophy") from Leiden in 1948, and then was a postdoc at Leiden for about a year.

## Career

In 1949, Bloembergen returned to Harvard as a junior fellow of the [Society of Fellows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_Society_of_Fellows "Harvard Society of Fellows"). In 1951, he became an associate professor; he then became Gordon McKay Professor of Applied Physics in 1957; [Rumford Professor of Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rumford_Chair_of_Physics "Rumford Chair of Physics") in 1974; and Gerhard Gade University Professor in 1980. He retired from Harvard in 1990.

In addition, Bloembergen served as a visiting professor. From 1964 to 1965, Bloembergen was a visiting professor at the [University of California, Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Berkeley "University of California, Berkeley"). In 1996–1997, he was a visiting scientist in the Optical Sciences Center at the [University of Arizona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Arizona "University of Arizona"); he became a professor at Arizona in 2001. Bloembergen was a member of the board of sponsors of the *[Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists](http://www.thebulletin.org)* and honorary editor of the *[Journal of Nonlinear Optical Physics & Materials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Journal_of_Nonlinear_Optical_Physics_&_Materials "Journal of Nonlinear Optical Physics & Materials")*.

## Research

By 1960 while at Harvard, Bloembergen experimented with [microwave spectroscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microwave_spectroscope "Microwave spectroscope"). He had modified the [maser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maser "Maser") of [Charles Townes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Townes "Charles Townes"), and in 1956, he developed a crystal maser, which was more powerful than the standard gaseous version.

With the advent of the laser, Bloembergen participated in the development of the field of [laser spectroscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laser_spectroscopy "Laser spectroscopy"), which allows precise observations of atomic structure using lasers. Following the development of [second-harmonic generation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second-harmonic_generation "Second-harmonic generation") by [Peter Franken](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Franken "Peter Franken") and others in 1961, Bloembergen studied how a new structure of matter is revealed, when one bombards [matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matter "Matter") with a focused and high-intensity beam of [photons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photons "Photons"). This he termed the study of [nonlinear optics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonlinear_optics "Nonlinear optics"). In reflection to his work in a Dutch newspaper in 1990, Bloembergen said: "We took a standard textbook on optics and for each section we asked ourselves what would happen if the intensity was to become very high. We were almost certain that we were bound to encounter an entirely new type of physics within that domain".

From this theoretical work, Bloembergen found ways to combine two or more laser sources consisting of photons in the [visible light frequency range](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light "Light") to generate a single laser source with photons of different frequencies in the [infrared](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infrared "Infrared") and [ultraviolet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultraviolet "Ultraviolet") ranges, which extends the amount of atomic detail that can be gathered from laser spectroscopy.

## Personal life and death

Bloembergen met Huberta Deliana "Deli" Brink in 1948 while on vacation with his university's Physics Club. She was able to travel with him to the United States in 1949 on a student hospitality exchange program; he proposed to her when they arrived in the States, and were married by 1950 on return to Amsterdam. They both became [naturalized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naturalization "Naturalization") U.S. citizens in 1958. They had three children.

Bloembergen died on September 5, 2017, at an assisted living facility in his hometown [Tucson, Arizona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tucson,_Arizona "Tucson, Arizona"), of cardiorespiratory failure at the age of 97. His wife died on June 19, 2019.

## Legacy

On March 11, 2020, the day of Bloembergen's 100th birthday, a team of researchers at the [University of New South Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_New_South_Wales "University of New South Wales") published an article in *[Nature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nature_(journal) "Nature (journal)")*, demonstrating for the first time the successful coherent control of the nucleus of a single atom using only electric fields, an idea first proposed by Bloembergen back in 1961.
