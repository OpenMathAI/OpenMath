**Brian David Josephson** (born 4 January 1940) is a British theoretical [physicist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physicist "Physicist") and emeritus professor at the [University of Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Cambridge "University of Cambridge"). He shared the 1973 [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics") with [Leo Esaki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leo_Esaki "Leo Esaki") and [Ivar Giaever](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivar_Giaever "Ivar Giaever") for his discovery of the [Josephson effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josephson_effect "Josephson effect"), made in 1962 when he was a Ph.D. student at Cambridge.

Josephson has spent his academic career as a member of the Theory of Condensed Matter Group in Cambridge's [Cavendish Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cavendish_Laboratory "Cavendish Laboratory"). He has been a Fellow of [Trinity College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College,_Cambridge "Trinity College, Cambridge"), since 1962, and served as Professor of Physics from 1974 until 2007.

In the early 1970s, Josephson took up [Transcendental Meditation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transcendental_Meditation "Transcendental Meditation") and turned his attention to issues outside the boundaries of mainstream science. He set up the Mind–Matter Unification Project at Cavendish to explore the idea of intelligence in nature, the relationship between [quantum mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_mechanics "Quantum mechanics") and [consciousness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consciousness "Consciousness"), and the synthesis of science and Eastern mysticism, broadly known as [quantum mysticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_mysticism "Quantum mysticism"). He has expressed support for topics such as [parapsychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parapsychology "Parapsychology"), [water memory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_memory "Water memory") and [cold fusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cold_fusion "Cold fusion"), which has made him a focus of criticism from fellow scientists.

## Education

Brian David Josephson was born on 4 January 1940 in [Cardiff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff "Cardiff"), Wales, to [Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish "Jewish") parents, Abraham Josephson and Mimi Weisbard. He attended [Cardiff High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiff_High_School "Cardiff High School"), where he credits some of the school masters for having helped him, particularly the physics master, Emrys Jones, who introduced him to theoretical physics. In 1957, he went up to Cambridge, where he initially read mathematics at [Trinity College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College,_Cambridge "Trinity College, Cambridge"). After completing [Maths Part II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_Mathematical_Tripos "Cambridge Mathematical Tripos") in two years, and finding it somewhat sterile, he decided to switch to physics.

Josephson was known at Cambridge as a brilliant but shy student. Physicist John Waldram recalled overhearing [Nicholas Kurti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicholas_Kurti "Nicholas Kurti"), an examiner from Oxford, discuss Josephson's exam results with [David Shoenberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Shoenberg "David Shoenberg"), Reader in Physics at Cambridge, and asking: "Who is this chap Josephson? He seems to be going through the theory like a knife through butter." While still an undergraduate, he published a paper on the [Mössbauer effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mössbauer_effect "Mössbauer effect"), pointing out a crucial issue other researchers had overlooked. According to one eminent physicist speaking to *[Physics World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics_World "Physics World")*, he wrote several papers important enough to assure him a place in the history of physics even without his discovery of the [Josephson effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josephson_effect "Josephson effect").

Josephson graduated in 1960, and became a research student in Cambridge's [Mond Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Museums_Site "New Museums Site") on the old Cavendish site, where he was supervised by [Brian Pippard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brian_Pippard "Brian Pippard"). American physicist [Philip Anderson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_Warren_Anderson "Philip Warren Anderson")—also a future Nobel Prize laureate—spent a year in Cambridge in 1961–1962, and recalled that having Josephson in a class was "a disconcerting experience for a lecturer, I can assure you, because everything had to be right or he would come up and explain it to me after class." It was during this period, as a [Ph.D.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ph.D. "Ph.D.") student in 1962, that he carried out the research that led to his discovery of the Josephson effect; the [Cavendish Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cavendish_Laboratory "Cavendish Laboratory") unveiled a plaque on the Mond Building dedicated to the discovery in November 2012. He was elected a Fellow of Trinity College in 1962, and received his Ph.D. in 1964 with a thesis titled *Non-linear conduction in superconductors*.

## Career

Josephson spent a postdoctoral year in the United States (1965–1966) as Research Assistant Professor at the [University of Illinois at Urbana–Champaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Illinois_at_Urbana–Champaign "University of Illinois at Urbana–Champaign"). After returning to Cambridge, he was made Assistant Director of Research in the [Cavendish Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cavendish_Laboratory "Cavendish Laboratory") in 1967, where he remained a member of the Theory of Condensed Matter Group for the rest of his career. He was [elected a Fellow of the Royal Society (FRS) in 1970](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Fellows_of_the_Royal_Society_elected_in_1970 "List of Fellows of the Royal Society elected in 1970"), and was awarded a [National Science Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Science_Foundation "National Science Foundation") Fellowship by [Cornell University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornell_University "Cornell University") the same year. In 1972, he became Reader in Physics, and in 1974 was appointed Professor of Physics, a position he held until his retirement in 2007.

A practitioner of [Transcendental Meditation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transcendental_Meditation "Transcendental Meditation") (TM) since the early 1970s, Josephson became a visiting faculty member in 1975 of the [Maharishi European Research University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transcendental_Meditation_in_education "Transcendental Meditation in education") in the Netherlands, part of the [TM movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transcendental_Meditation_movement "Transcendental Meditation movement"). He also held visiting professorships at [Wayne State University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wayne_State_University "Wayne State University") in 1983, the [Indian Institute of Science, Bangalore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Institute_of_Science,_Bangalore "Indian Institute of Science, Bangalore") in 1984, and the [University of Missouri-Rolla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Missouri-Rolla "University of Missouri-Rolla") in 1987.

## Josephson effect

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/16/NISTvoltChip.jpg/250px-NISTvoltChip.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:NISTvoltChip.jpg)

One-volt [NIST](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Institute_of_Standards_and_Technology "National Institute of Standards and Technology") Josephson junction array standard with 3020 superconducting junctions.

[![photograph](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/44/Plaque_on_the_Mond_Building%2C_Cambridge%2C_for_Brian_Josephson.jpg/250px-Plaque_on_the_Mond_Building%2C_Cambridge%2C_for_Brian_Josephson.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Plaque_on_the_Mond_Building,_Cambridge,_for_Brian_Josephson.jpg)

The Cavendish Laboratory unveiled a plaque on the Mond Building in November 2012 for the 50th anniversary of the discovery of the [Josephson effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josephson_effect "Josephson effect").

Josephson was 22-years-old when he did the work on [quantum tunnelling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_tunnelling "Quantum tunnelling") that earned him the [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics") in 1973. He discovered that a [supercurrent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supercurrent "Supercurrent") could tunnel through a thin barrier, predicting, according to physicist Andrew Whitaker, that "at a [junction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_junction "Electrical junction") of two superconductors, a current will flow even if there is no drop in voltage; that when there is a [voltage drop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voltage_drop "Voltage drop"), the current should oscillate at a frequency related to the drop in voltage; and that there is a dependence on any [magnetic field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_field "Magnetic field")." This became known as the [Josephson effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josephson_effect "Josephson effect") and the junction as a Josephson junction.

Josephson's calculations were published in *[Physics Letters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics_Letters "Physics Letters")* (chosen by Pippard because it was a new journal) in a paper titled "Possible new effects in superconductive tunnelling," received on 8 June 1962 and published on 1 July. They were confirmed experimentally by Philip Anderson and John Rowell of [Bell Labs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell_Labs "Bell Labs") in Princeton; this appeared in their paper, "Probable Observation of the Josephson Superconducting Tunneling Effect," submitted to *[Physical Review Letters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_Review_Letters "Physical Review Letters")* in January 1963.

Before Anderson and Rowell confirmed the calculations, the American physicist [John Bardeen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Bardeen "John Bardeen"), who had shared the 1956 Nobel Prize in Physics (and who shared it again in 1972), objected to Josephson's work. He submitted an article to *Physical Review Letters* on 25 July 1962, arguing that "there can be no such superfluid flow." The disagreement led to a confrontation in September that year at [Queen Mary College, London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen_Mary,_University_of_London "Queen Mary, University of London"), at the Eighth [International Conference on Low Temperature Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_International_Conference_on_Low_Temperature_Physics "The International Conference on Low Temperature Physics"). When Bardeen (then one of the most eminent physicists in the world) began speaking, Josephson (still a student) stood up and interrupted him. The men exchanged views, reportedly in a civil and soft-spoken manner. See also: [John Bardeen § Josephson effect controversy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Bardeen#Josephson_effect_controversy "John Bardeen").

Whitaker writes that the discovery of the Josephson effect led to "much important physics," including the invention of [SQUIDs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SQUID "SQUID") (superconducting quantum interference devices), which are used in geology to make highly sensitive measurements, as well as in medicine and computing. [IBM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM "IBM") used Josephson's work in 1980 to build a prototype of a computer that would be up to 100 times faster than the [IBM 3033 mainframe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM_System/370 "IBM System/370").

Josephson was awarded several important prizes for his discovery, including the 1969 [Research Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Research_Corporation "Research Corporation") Award for outstanding contributions to science, and the [Hughes Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hughes_Medal "Hughes Medal") and [Holweck Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holweck_Prize "Holweck Prize") in 1972. In 1973, he won the Nobel Prize in Physics, sharing the $122,000 award with two other scientists who had also worked on quantum tunnelling. Josephson was awarded half the prize "for his theoretical predictions of the properties of a supercurrent through a tunnel barrier, in particular those phenomena which are generally known as the Josephson effects". The other half of the award was shared equally by Japanese physicist [Leo Esaki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leo_Esaki "Leo Esaki") of the Thomas Watson Research Center in Yorktown, New York, and Norwegian–American physicist [Ivar Giaever](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivar_Giaever "Ivar Giaever") of General Electric in Schenectady, New York.

## Parapsychology

### Early interest and Transcendental Meditation

Josephson became interested in [philosophy of mind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_mind "Philosophy of mind") in the late 1960s and, in particular, in the [mind–body problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mind–body_problem "Mind–body problem"), and is one of the few scientists to argue that parapsychological phenomena ([telepathy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telepathy "Telepathy"), [psychokinesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychokinesis "Psychokinesis") and other paranormal themes) may be real. In 1971, he began practising Transcendental Meditation (TM).

Winning the Nobel Prize in 1973 gave Josephson the freedom to work in less orthodox areas, and he became increasingly involved—including during science conferences, to the irritation of fellow scientists—in talking about meditation, telepathy, and higher states of consciousness. In 1974, he angered scientists during a colloquium of molecular and cellular biologists in Versailles by inviting them to read the *[Bhagavad Gita](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhagavad_Gita "Bhagavad Gita")* (5th – 2nd century BCE) and the work of [Maharishi Mahesh Yogi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maharishi_Mahesh_Yogi "Maharishi Mahesh Yogi"), the founder of the TM movement, and by arguing about special states of consciousness achieved through meditation. "Nothing forces us," one scientist shouted at him, "to listen to your wild speculations." Biophysicist [Henri Atlan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_Atlan "Henri Atlan") wrote that the session ended in uproar.

In May that year, Josephson addressed a symposium held to welcome the Maharishi to Cambridge. The following month, at the first Canadian conference on psychokinesis, he was one of 21 scientists who tested claims by [Matthew Manning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matthew_Manning "Matthew Manning"), a Cambridgeshire teenager who said he had psychokinetic abilities; Josephson apparently told a reporter that he believed Manning's powers were a new kind of energy. He later withdrew or corrected the statement.

Josephson said that Trinity College's tradition of interest in the paranormal meant that he did not dismiss these ideas out of hand. Several presidents of the [Society for Psychical Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Society_for_Psychical_Research "Society for Psychical Research") had been fellows of Trinity, and the [Perrott-Warrick Fund](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perrott-Warrick_Fund "Perrott-Warrick Fund"), set up in Trinity in 1937 to fund parapsychology research, is still administered by the college. He continued to explore the idea that there is intelligence in nature, particularly after reading [Fritjof Capra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fritjof_Capra "Fritjof Capra")'s *[The Tao of Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Tao_of_Physics "The Tao of Physics")* (1975), and in 1979 took up a more advanced form of TM, known as the [TM-Sidhi program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transcendental_Meditation_technique#TM-Sidhi_program "Transcendental Meditation technique"). According to [Anderson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_W._Anderson "Philip W. Anderson"), the TM movement produced a poster showing Josephson levitating several inches above the floor. Josephson argued that meditation could lead to mystical and scientific insights, and that, as a result of it, he had come to believe in a creator.

Josephson became involved in the mid-1970s with a group of physicists associated with the [Lawrence Berkeley Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawrence_Berkeley_Laboratory "Lawrence Berkeley Laboratory") at the [University of California, Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Berkeley "University of California, Berkeley"), who were investigating paranormal claims. They had organized themselves loosely into the [Fundamental Fysiks Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_Fysiks_Group "Fundamental Fysiks Group"), and had effectively become the [Stanford Research Institute's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SRI_International "SRI International") (SRI) "house theorists," according to historian of science [David Kaiser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Kaiser_(physicist) "David Kaiser (physicist)"). Core members in the group were Elizabeth Rauscher, George Weissmann, John Clauser, Jack Sarfatti, Saul-Paul Sirag, Nick Herbert, Fred Alan Wolf, Fritjof Capra, Henry Stapp, Philippe Eberhard and Gary Zukav.

There was significant government interest at the time in [quantum mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_mechanics "Quantum mechanics") – the American government [was financing research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stargate_Project_(U.S._Army_unit) "Stargate Project (U.S. Army unit)") at SRI into telepathy – and physicists able to understand it found themselves in demand. The Fundamental Fysiks Group used ideas from quantum physics, particularly [Bell's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell's_theorem "Bell's theorem") and [quantum entanglement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_entanglement "Quantum entanglement"), to explore issues such as [action at a distance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_at_a_distance "Action at a distance"), [clairvoyance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clairvoyance "Clairvoyance"), [precognition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precognition "Precognition"), [remote viewing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Remote_viewing "Remote viewing") and [psychokinesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychokinesis "Psychokinesis").

In 1976, Josephson travelled to California at the invitation of one of the Fundamental Fysiks Group members, [Jack Sarfatti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jack_Sarfatti "Jack Sarfatti"), who introduced him to others including laser physicists [Russell Targ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russell_Targ "Russell Targ") and [Harold Puthoff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_E._Puthoff "Harold E. Puthoff"), and quantum physicist [Henry Stapp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Stapp "Henry Stapp"). The *[San Francisco Chronicle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Francisco_Chronicle "San Francisco Chronicle")* covered Josephson's visit.

Josephson co-organised a symposium on consciousness at Cambridge in 1978, publishing the proceedings as *Consciousness and the Physical World* (1980), with neuroscientist [V. S. Ramachandran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V._S._Ramachandran "V. S. Ramachandran"). A conference on "Science and Consciousness" followed a year later in Cordoba, Spain, attended by physicists and [Jungian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Jung "Carl Jung") psychoanalysts, and addressed by Josephson, [Fritjof Capra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fritjof_Capra "Fritjof Capra") and [David Bohm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Bohm "David Bohm") (1917–1992).

By 1996, Josephson had set up the Mind–Matter Unification Project at the Cavendish Laboratory to explore intelligent processes in nature. In 2002, he told *Physics World*: "Future science will consider quantum mechanics as the phenomenology of particular kinds of organised complex system. Quantum entanglement would be one manifestation of such organisation, paranormal phenomena another."

### Reception and views on the scientific community

Josephson delivered the Pollock Memorial Lecture in 2006, the [Hermann Staudinger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann_Staudinger "Hermann Staudinger") Lecture in 2009 and the Sir [Nevill Mott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nevill_Francis_Mott "Nevill Francis Mott") Lecture in 2010.

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/71/Cmglee_Cambridge_Wikimedia_Meetup_23_tour_Brian_Josephson.jpg/250px-Cmglee_Cambridge_Wikimedia_Meetup_23_tour_Brian_Josephson.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Cmglee_Cambridge_Wikimedia_Meetup_23_tour_Brian_Josephson.jpg)

Josephson on a Cambridge Wikimedia walk, 2014.

Matthew Reisz wrote in *[Times Higher Education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Times_Higher_Education "Times Higher Education")* in 2010 that Josephson has long been one of physics' "more colourful figures." His support for unorthodox causes has attracted criticism from fellow scientists since the 1970s, including from [Philip Anderson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_W._Anderson "Philip W. Anderson"). Josephson regards the criticism as prejudice, and believes that it has served to deprive him of an academic support network.

Josephson has repeatedly criticised "science by consensus," arguing that the scientific community is too quick to reject certain kinds of ideas. "Anything goes among the physics community – cosmic wormholes, time travel," he argues, "just so long as it keeps its distance from anything mystical or New Age-ish." Referring to this position as "pathological disbelief," he holds it responsible for the rejection by academic journals of papers on the paranormal. He has compared parapsychology to the theory of [continental drift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continental_drift "Continental drift"), proposed in 1912 by [Alfred Wegener](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Wegener "Alfred Wegener") (1880–1930) to explain observations that were otherwise inexplicable, which was resisted and ridiculed until evidence led to its acceptance after Wegener's death.

Science writer [Martin Gardner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Gardner "Martin Gardner") criticised Josephson in 1980 for complaining to *[The New York Review of Books](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_York_Review_of_Books "The New York Review of Books")*, along with three other physicists, about an article by [J. A. Wheeler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Archibald_Wheeler "John Archibald Wheeler") that ridiculed parapsychology. Several physicists complained in 2001 when, in a [Royal Mail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Mail "Royal Mail") booklet celebrating the Nobel Prize's centenary, Josephson wrote that Britain was at the forefront of research into telepathy. Physicist [David Deutsch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Deutsch "David Deutsch") said the Royal Mail had "let itself be hoodwinked" into supporting nonsense, although another physicist, [Robert Matthews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Matthews_(scientist) "Robert Matthews (scientist)"), suggested that Deutsch was skating on thin ice given the latter's own work on [parallel universes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Many-worlds_interpretation "Many-worlds interpretation") and [time travel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_mechanics_of_time_travel "Quantum mechanics of time travel").

In 2004, Josephson criticised an experiment by the [Committee for Skeptical Inquiry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Committee_for_Skeptical_Inquiry "Committee for Skeptical Inquiry") to test claims by Russian schoolgirl [Natasha Demkina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natasha_Demkina "Natasha Demkina") that she could see inside people's bodies using a special kind of vision. The experiment involved her being asked to match six people to their confirmed medical conditions (plus one with none); to pass the test she had to make five correct matches, but made only four. Josephson argued that this was statistically significant, and that the experiment had set her up to fail. One of the researchers, [Richard Wiseman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Wiseman "Richard Wiseman"), professor of psychology at the [University of Hertfordshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Hertfordshire "University of Hertfordshire"), responded by highlighting that the conditions of the experiment had been agreed to before it started, and the potential significance of her claims warranted a higher than normal bar. Keith Rennolis, professor of applied statistics at the [University of Greenwich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Greenwich "University of Greenwich"), supported Josephson's position, asserting that the experiment was "woefully inadequate" to determine any effect.

Josephson's reputation for promoting unorthodox causes was cemented by his support for the ideas of [water memory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_memory "Water memory") and [cold fusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cold_fusion "Cold fusion"), both of which are rejected by mainstream scientists. Water memory is purported to provide a possible explanation for [homeopathy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeopathy "Homeopathy"); it is dismissed by a majority of scientists as [pseudoscience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoscience "Pseudoscience"), although he has expressed support for it since attending a conference at which French immunologist [Jacques Benveniste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques_Benveniste "Jacques Benveniste") first proposed it. Cold fusion is the hypothesis that nuclear reactions can occur at room temperature. When [Martin Fleischmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Fleischmann "Martin Fleischmann"), the British chemist who pioneered research into it, died in 2012, Josephson wrote a supportive obituary in the *Guardian*, and had published in *Nature* a letter complaining that its obituary had failed to give Fleischmann due credit. [Antony Valentini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antony_Valentini "Antony Valentini") of Imperial College London withdrew Josephson's invitation to a 2010 conference on the [de Broglie–Bohm theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Broglie–Bohm_theory "De Broglie–Bohm theory") because of his work on the paranormal, although it was reinstated after complaints.

Josephson's defense of paranormal claims and of cold fusion have led him to being described as an exemplar of a sufferer of the hypothetical [Nobel disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_disease "Nobel disease").
