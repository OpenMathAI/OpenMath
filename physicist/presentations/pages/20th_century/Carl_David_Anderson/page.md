**Carl David Anderson** (September 3, 1905 – January 11, 1991) was an American experimental [physicist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physicist "Physicist") who shared the 1936 [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics") with [Victor Hess](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victor_Hess "Victor Hess") for his discovery of the [positron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positron "Positron"), which confirmed the existence of [antimatter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antimatter "Antimatter").

## Biography

Carl David Anderson was born on September 3, 1905, in [New York City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_City "New York City"), to Swedish immigrants, Carl David Anderson Sr. and Emma Adolfina Ajaxson. Anderson received his [B.S.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B.S. "B.S.") in Physics and Engineering in 1927, and his [Ph.D.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ph.D. "Ph.D.") in Physics in 1930, both from the [California Institute of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California_Institute_of_Technology "California Institute of Technology") (Caltech).

Anderson spent the entirety of his career at Caltech; he was Research Fellow (1930–1933) and Assistant Professor to Associate Professor of Physics (1933–1939), before becoming Professor of Physics in 1939, a position he held until his retirement in 1976.

In 1946, Anderson married Lorraine Bergman, with whom he had two sons.

Anderson died on January 11, 1991, in [San Marino, California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Marino,_California "San Marino, California"), at the age of 85. His remains were interred in the [Forest Lawn Memorial Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forest_Lawn_Memorial_Park_(Hollywood_Hills) "Forest Lawn Memorial Park (Hollywood Hills)") in Los Angeles. He was a Christian.

## Research

### Discovery of the positron

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/69/PositronDiscovery.jpg/250px-PositronDiscovery.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:PositronDiscovery.jpg)

Photo by Anderson of the first positron ever observed, March 15, 1933

Under the supervision of [Robert Millikan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Millikan "Robert Millikan"), Anderson began investigations into [cosmic rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmic_ray "Cosmic ray") during the course of which he encountered unexpected particle tracks in his (modern versions now commonly referred to as an Anderson) [cloud chamber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cloud_chamber "Cloud chamber") photographs that he correctly interpreted as having been created by a particle with the same mass as the [electron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron "Electron"), but with opposite [electric charge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_charge "Electric charge").

This discovery, announced in 1932 and later confirmed by others, validated [Paul Dirac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Dirac "Paul Dirac")'s theoretical prediction of the existence of the [positron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positron "Positron"). Anderson first detected the particles in cosmic rays. He then produced more conclusive proof by shooting [gamma rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamma_ray "Gamma ray") produced by the natural radioactive nuclide ThC'' ([208Tl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thallium-208 "Thallium-208")) into other materials, resulting in the creation of positron-electron pairs.

For this work, Anderson shared the 1936 [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics") with [Victor Hess](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victor_Hess "Victor Hess"). Fifty years later, Anderson acknowledged that his discovery was inspired by the work of his Caltech classmate, [Chung-Yao Chao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chung-Yao_Chao "Chung-Yao Chao"), whose research formed the foundation from which much of Anderson's work developed but was not credited at the time.

### Discovery of the muon

In 1936, Anderson and his first graduate student, [Seth Neddermeyer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seth_Neddermeyer "Seth Neddermeyer"), discovered the [muon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muon "Muon") (or 'mu-meson', as it was known for many years), a [subatomic particle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subatomic_particle "Subatomic particle") 207 times more massive than the electron, but with the same negative electric charge and spin 1/2 as the electron, again in cosmic rays.

Anderson and Neddermeyer at first believed that they had seen a [pion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pion "Pion"), a particle which [Hideki Yukawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hideki_Yukawa "Hideki Yukawa") had postulated in his theory of the [strong interaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strong_interaction "Strong interaction"). When it became clear that what Anderson had seen was *not* the pion, the physicist [I. I. Rabi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I._I._Rabi "I. I. Rabi"), puzzled as to how the unexpected discovery could fit into any logical scheme of [particle physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_physics "Particle physics"), quizzically asked "Who ordered *that*?" (sometimes the story goes that he was dining with colleagues at a Chinese restaurant at the time).

The muon was the first of a long list of [subatomic particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subatomic_particles "Subatomic particles") whose discovery initially baffled theoreticians who could not make the confusing "zoo" fit into some tidy conceptual scheme. [Willis Lamb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Willis_Lamb "Willis Lamb"), in his 1955 Nobel Prize Lecture, joked that he had heard it said that "the finder of a new elementary particle used to be rewarded by a Nobel Prize, but such a discovery now ought to be punished by a 10,000 dollar fine."
