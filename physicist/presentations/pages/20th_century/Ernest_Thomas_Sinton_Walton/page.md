**Ernest Thomas Sinton Walton** (6 October 1903 – 25 June 1995) was an Irish experimental [physicist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physicist "Physicist"). He shared the 1951 [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics") with [John Cockcroft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Cockcroft "John Cockcroft") "for their pioneer work on the transmutation of [atomic nuclei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_nuclei "Atomic nuclei") by artificially accelerated [atomic particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_particle "Atomic particle")." According to their Nobel Prize speech: "Thus, for the first time, a [nuclear transmutation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_transmutation "Nuclear transmutation") was produced by means entirely under human control."

Walton was a key member of the nuclear physics faculty at the [University of Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Cambridge "University of Cambridge"), where he worked with Cockcroft and [Ernest Rutherford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_Rutherford "Ernest Rutherford"). He then spent the majority of his career in Ireland, after returning from England in 1934. He remained active as a member of the teaching faculty at [Trinity College Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College_Dublin "Trinity College Dublin"), where he served as [Erasmus Smith's Professor of Natural and Experimental Philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erasmus_Smith's_Professor_of_Natural_and_Experimental_Philosophy "Erasmus Smith's Professor of Natural and Experimental Philosophy") from 1946 until his retirement in 1974, after which he continued to be associated with the physics department at the college. Along with [William Rowan Hamilton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Rowan_Hamilton "William Rowan Hamilton"), Walton is regarded as one of the most influential Irish physicists.

## Early life and education

Ernest Thomas Sinton Walton was born on 6 October 1903 in [Dungarvan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dungarvan "Dungarvan"), Ireland, the son of John Walton (1874–1936), a [Methodist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methodist "Methodist") minister from [Cloughjordan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cloughjordan "Cloughjordan"), and Anna Sinton (1874–1906) from [Richhill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richhill,_County_Armagh "Richhill, County Armagh"). In those days, a general clergyman's family moved once every three years, and this practice carried Ernest and his family, while he was a small child, to [Rathkeale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rathkeale "Rathkeale"), [County Limerick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Limerick "County Limerick") (where his mother died), and to [County Monaghan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Monaghan "County Monaghan").

Walton attended day schools in counties [Down](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Down "County Down") and [Tyrone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Tyrone "County Tyrone"), and at [Wesley College Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wesley_College_Dublin "Wesley College Dublin"), before becoming a boarder at [Methodist College Belfast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methodist_College_Belfast "Methodist College Belfast") in 1915, where he excelled in science and mathematics.

In 1922, Walton won scholarships to attend [Trinity College Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College_Dublin "Trinity College Dublin") for the study of mathematics and science, and would go on to be elected a [Foundation Scholar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Scholars_of_Trinity_College_Dublin "List of Scholars of Trinity College Dublin") in 1924. He was awarded his [B.Sc.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_Science "Bachelor of Science") in 1926, and his [M.Sc.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master_of_Science "Master of Science") the following year. During these years at college, he received numerous prizes for excellence in physics and mathematics (seven prizes in all), including the Foundation Scholarship in 1924. After graduating in 1927, he was awarded an [1851 Research Fellowship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1851_Research_Fellowship "1851 Research Fellowship") from the [Royal Commission for the Exhibition of 1851](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Commission_for_the_Exhibition_of_1851 "Royal Commission for the Exhibition of 1851") and was accepted as a research student at [Trinity College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College,_Cambridge "Trinity College, Cambridge"), under the supervision of [Ernest Rutherford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_Rutherford "Ernest Rutherford"), Director of the [Cavendish Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cavendish_Laboratory "Cavendish Laboratory"). At the time there were four Nobel Prize laureates on the staff in the Cavendish Laboratory and a further five were to emerge, including Walton and [John Cockcroft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Cockcroft "John Cockcroft"). Walton received his [Ph.D.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ph.D. "Ph.D.") in 1931, and remained at Cambridge as a researcher until 1934.

## Research

During the early 1930s, Walton and Cockcroft collaborated to build an apparatus that split the [nuclei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_nucleus "Atomic nucleus") of [lithium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithium "Lithium") [atoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atoms "Atoms") by bombarding them with a stream of [protons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protons "Protons") accelerated inside a high-voltage tube (700 kilovolts). The splitting of the lithium nuclei produced [helium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helium "Helium") nuclei. They went on to use [boron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boron "Boron") and [carbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon "Carbon") as targets for their 'disintegration' experiments, and to report [induced radioactivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Induced_radioactivity "Induced radioactivity"). These experiments provided verification of theories about [atomic structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_structure "Atomic structure") that had been proposed earlier by Rutherford, [George Gamow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Gamow "George Gamow"), and others. The successful apparatus, a type of [particle accelerator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_accelerator "Particle accelerator") (now called the [Cockcroft–Walton generator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cockcroft–Walton_generator "Cockcroft–Walton generator")), helped to usher in an era of particle accelerator-based experimental [nuclear physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_physics "Nuclear physics"). It was this research at Cambridge in the early 1930s that won Walton and Cockcroft the [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics") in 1951.

## Career in Dublin

Walton returned to Ireland in 1934 to become a Fellow of [Trinity College Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College_Dublin "Trinity College Dublin") in the Physics Department. He was appointed [Erasmus Smith's Professor of Natural and Experimental Philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erasmus_Smith's_Professor_of_Natural_and_Experimental_Philosophy "Erasmus Smith's Professor of Natural and Experimental Philosophy") in 1946, and was promoted to Senior Fellow in 1960. His lecturing was considered outstanding as he had the ability to present complicated matters in simple and easy-to-understand terms. His research interests were pursued with very limited resources, yet he was able to study, in the late 1950s, the [phosphorescent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phosphorescent "Phosphorescent") effect in glasses, secondary-electron emissions from surfaces under positive-ion bombardment, radiocarbon dating and low-level counting, and the deposition of thin films on glass.

Walton was associated with the [Dublin Institute for Advanced Studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_Institute_for_Advanced_Studies "Dublin Institute for Advanced Studies") for over 40 years, where he served long periods on the board of the School of Cosmic Physics and on the council of the Institute. Following the 1952 death of [John J. Nolan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_J._Nolan "John J. Nolan"), the inaugural chairman of the School of Cosmic Physics, Walton assumed the role and served in that position until 1960, when he was succeeded by John H. Poole.

Along with [Lochlainn O'Raifeartaigh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lochlainn_O'Raifeartaigh "Lochlainn O'Raifeartaigh") and Michael Fry, Walton helped found the Irish [Pugwash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pugwash_Conferences_on_Science_and_World_Affairs "Pugwash Conferences on Science and World Affairs") group, opposing the [nuclear arms race](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_arms_race "Nuclear arms race").

Walton served on a committee of [Wesley College, Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wesley_College,_Dublin "Wesley College, Dublin").

## Later life and death

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/30/Ernest_Walton_Grave_2016.jpg/250px-Ernest_Walton_Grave_2016.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Ernest_Walton_Grave_2016.jpg)

Walton's grave in [Dean's Grange Cemetery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dean's_Grange_Cemetery "Dean's Grange Cemetery"), south County Dublin.

Although Walton retired from [Trinity College Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College_Dublin "Trinity College Dublin") in 1974, he retained his association with the Physics Department at Trinity up to his final illness. Shortly before his death, he marked his lifelong devotion to Trinity by presenting his Nobel medal and citation to the College.

Walton died on 25 June 1995 in [Belfast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belfast "Belfast") at the age of 91. He is buried in [Deansgrange Cemetery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deansgrange_Cemetery "Deansgrange Cemetery"), near [Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin "Dublin").

## Family

In 1934, Walton married Winifred Wilson, the daughter of a Methodist minister. They had four children: Alan Walton (a physicist at the [University of Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Cambridge "University of Cambridge")), Marian Woods, Philip Walton (Professor of Applied Physics, [NUI Galway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NUI_Galway "NUI Galway")), and Jean Clarke.

## Religious views

Raised as a [Methodist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methodist "Methodist"), Walton has been described as someone who was strongly committed to the Christian faith. He gave lectures about the relationship of science and religion in several countries after he won the Nobel Prize, and he encouraged the progress of science as a way to know more about God.

Walton is quoted as saying:

> "One way to learn the mind of the Creator is to study His creation. We must pay God the compliment of studying His work of art and this should apply to all realms of human thought. A refusal to use our intelligence honestly is an act of contempt for Him who gave us that intelligence."

— V. J. McBrierty (2003): *Ernest Thomas Sinton Walton, The Irish Scientist, 1903-1995*, Trinity College Dublin Press.)

Walton held an interest in topics about the government and the Church, and after his death, the organisation *[Christians in Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christians_in_Science "Christians in Science") Ireland* established the *Walton Lectures on Science and Religion* (an initiative similar to the [Boyle Lectures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boyle_Lectures "Boyle Lectures")). [David Wilkinson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Wilkinson_(theologian) "David Wilkinson (theologian)"), [Denis Alexander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denis_Alexander "Denis Alexander"), and others have given Walton Lectures in universities across Ireland.

## Commemoration

The Walton Causeway Park in Walton's native [Dungarvan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dungarvan "Dungarvan") was dedicated in his honour with Walton himself attending the ceremony in 1989. After his death the [Waterford Institute of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waterford_Institute_of_Technology "Waterford Institute of Technology") named a building the ETS Walton Building and a plaque was placed on the site of his birthplace.

Other honours for Walton include the Walton Building at [Methodist College Belfast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methodist_College_Belfast "Methodist College Belfast"), the school where he had been a boarder for five years, and a memorial plaque outside the main entrance to Methodist College. [Wesley College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wesley_College,_Dublin "Wesley College, Dublin") in Dublin, where he attended and for many years served as chairman of the board of Governors, established the Walton Prize for Physics, and a prize with the same name at Methodist College is awarded to the pupil who obtains the highest marks in A Level Physics. There is also a scholarship in [Waterford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waterford "Waterford") named after Walton. In 2014, [Trinity College Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College_Dublin "Trinity College Dublin") set up the Trinity Walton Club, an extracurricular STEM Education centre for teenagers.
