**Karl Ferdinand Braun** (German: [[ˈfɛʁdinant ˈbʁaʊ̯n]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Standard_German "Help:IPA/Standard German") [ⓘ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:De-Ferdinand_Braun.ogg "File:De-Ferdinand Braun.ogg"); 6 June 1850 – 20 April 1918) was a German applied [physicist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physicist "Physicist") who shared the 1909 [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics") with [Guglielmo Marconi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guglielmo_Marconi "Guglielmo Marconi") for their contributions to the development of [radio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio "Radio"). With his [two circuit system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K._Ferdinand_Braun#Radio_transmitter), long range radio transmissions and modern [telecommunications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telecommunications "Telecommunications") were made possible. His invention of the [phased array](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phased_array "Phased array") antenna in 1905 led to the development of [radar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radar "Radar"), [smart antennas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smart_antenna "Smart antenna"), and [MIMO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MIMO "MIMO"). Braun built the first [cathode-ray tube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathode-ray_tube "Cathode-ray tube") in 1897, which led to the development of [television](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Television "Television"), and the first [semiconductor diode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diode#Semiconductor "Diode") in 1874, which co-started the development of [electronics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronics "Electronics") and [electronic engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_engineering "Electronic engineering").

Braun was a co-founder of [Telefunken](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telefunken "Telefunken"), one of the pioneering communications and television companies. He has been called the "father of television" (shared with inventors like [Paul Nipkow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Nipkow "Paul Nipkow")), the "great-grandfather of every semiconductor ever manufactured," and a co-father of [radiotelegraphy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiotelegraphy "Radiotelegraphy"), together with Marconi, laying the foundation for all modern [wireless](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wireless "Wireless") systems.

## Biography

### Education and career

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/62/Fulda%2C_Ferdinand-Braun-Geburtshaus%2C_2019-10_CN-01.jpg/250px-Fulda%2C_Ferdinand-Braun-Geburtshaus%2C_2019-10_CN-01.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Fulda,_Ferdinand-Braun-Geburtshaus,_2019-10_CN-01.jpg)

The house in [Fulda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fulda "Fulda"), Germany, where Braun was born

Karl Ferdinand Braun was born on 6 June 1850 in [Fulda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fulda "Fulda"). In 1868, Braun started studying physics, chemistry, and mathematics at the [University of Marburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Marburg "University of Marburg"). The following year, he transferred to the [University of Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Berlin "University of Berlin") and became an assistant to [Heinrich Gustav Magnus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heinrich_Gustav_Magnus "Heinrich Gustav Magnus"). After Magnus' death in 1870, Braun continued his training with [Georg Hermann Quincke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georg_Hermann_Quincke "Georg Hermann Quincke"). In 1872, he received his [Ph.D.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ph.D. "Ph.D.") with a thesis on [vibrating strings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vibrating_string "Vibrating string"), and subsequently followed Quincke to the [University of Würzburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Würzburg "University of Würzburg") as an assistant.

In 1874, Braun accepted a teaching appointment at the [Thomasschule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomasschule "Thomasschule") in [Leipzig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leipzig "Leipzig"). In 1876, he returned to the University of Marburg as Extraordinary Professor of Theoretical Physics, and in 1880 was invited to fill a similar post at the [University of Strassburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Strassburg "University of Strassburg"). He was made Professor of Physics at the [Karlsruhe Institute of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karlsruhe_Institute_of_Technology "Karlsruhe Institute of Technology") in 1883, and was invited by the [University of Tübingen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Tübingen "University of Tübingen") in 1885. In 1895, he returned to Strassburg as Principal of the Physics Institute.

### Radio work

In 1897, Braun joined the line of [wireless](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wireless "Wireless") pioneers. His major contributions to the [development of radio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_radio "History of radio") were the introduction of a closed tuned circuit in the generating part of the [transmitter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transmitter "Transmitter"), its separation from the radiating part (the [antenna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antenna_(radio) "Antenna (radio)")) by means of [inductive coupling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inductive_coupling "Inductive coupling"), and later on the usage of crystals for receiving purposes. Around 1898, he invented a [crystal detector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal_detector "Crystal detector") . [Wireless telegraphy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wireless_telegraphy "Wireless telegraphy") claimed his full attention in 1898, and for many years after that he applied himself almost exclusively to the task of solving its problems. He had written extensively on wireless subjects and was well known through his many contributions to *[The Electrician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Electrician "The Electrician")* and other scientific journals. In 1899, he applied for the patent *Wireless electro transmission of signals over surfaces*. Also in 1899, he is said to have applied for a patent on *Electro telegraphy by means of condensers and induction coils*.

Pioneers working on wireless devices eventually came to a limit of distance they could cover; connecting the antenna directly to the [spark gap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spark_gap "Spark gap") produced only a heavily damped pulse train. There were only a few cycles before oscillations ceased. Braun's circuit afforded a much longer sustained oscillation because the energy encountered less losses swinging between coil and [Leyden jars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leyden_jar "Leyden jar"). And by means of inductive antenna coupling the radiator was better matched to the [generator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_generator "Electric generator"). The resultant stronger and less bandwidth consuming signals bridged a much longer distance.

In 1905, Braun invented the [phased array](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phased_array "Phased array") antenna; he described in his Nobel Prize lecture how he carefully arranged three antennas to transmit a [directional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Directional_antenna "Directional antenna") signal. This invention led to the development of [radar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radar "Radar"), [smart antennas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smart_antennas "Smart antennas"), and [MIMO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MIMO "MIMO").

Braun's British patent on tuning was used by [Guglielmo Marconi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guglielmo_Marconi "Guglielmo Marconi") in many of his tuning patents. Marconi used Braun's patents (among others). Marconi would later admit to Braun himself that he had "borrowed" portions of Braun's work. In 1909, Braun and Marconi were jointly awarded the [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics") "in recognition of their contributions to the development of wireless telegraphy"; the prize awarded to Braun depicts this design. He experimented initially at the University of Strassburg, not long before he bridged a distance of 42 km to [Mutzig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutzig "Mutzig"). In spring 1899, Braun, accompanied by his colleagues Cantor and Zenneck, went to [Cuxhaven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuxhaven "Cuxhaven") to continue their experiments at the North Sea. On 24 September 1900, radio telegraphy signals were exchanged regularly with the island of [Heligoland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heligoland "Heligoland") over a distance of 62 km. Light vessels in the river [Elbe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elbe "Elbe") and a coast station at Cuxhaven commenced a regular radio telegraph service.

### Later life and death

In 1914, Braun went to [New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_(state) "New York (state)") in the United States to be a witness for the defense in a lawsuit regarding a
patent claim by the [Marconi Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marconi_Company "Marconi Company") against Telefunken's wireless station in [Sayville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sayville,_New_York "Sayville, New York"). He was a Lutheran.

After the U.S. declared war on Germany in 1917, Braun was detained as an [enemy alien](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enemy_alien "Enemy alien"). He was allowed to move freely within [Brooklyn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brooklyn "Brooklyn"), where he died the following year on 20 April at the age of 67.

## Inventions and discoveries

### Semiconductor

In 1874, Braun discovered the asymmetric conduction properties of certain materials, which became the foundation for the point-contact [rectifier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rectifier "Rectifier"). This discovery showed that certain [metal-semiconductor junctions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metal-semiconductor_junction "Metal-semiconductor junction") could conduct electricity more easily in one direction than the other, a crucial property for [diodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diode "Diode").

Braun's work with semiconductors led to the development of the first [point-contact diode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point-contact_diode "Point-contact diode"), often credited as a basic semiconductor device that allowed the rectification of [alternating current](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alternating_current "Alternating current") into [direct current](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct_current "Direct current"). This is important because it was one of the first real-world applications of semiconducting materials, paving the way for future [semiconductor devices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiconductor_device "Semiconductor device") that would later evolve into modern diodes, [transistors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transistor "Transistor"), and other semiconductor technology.

Braun's discoveries were instrumental in the early development of [electronics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronics "Electronics") and helped lay the groundwork for the [semiconductor industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiconductor_industry "Semiconductor industry") we know today.

### Cathode-ray tube

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/69/Braun_cathode_ray_tube.jpg/250px-Braun_cathode_ray_tube.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Braun_cathode_ray_tube.jpg)

Braun's original cathode-ray tube, 1897

Braun's enduring fame is largely due to his invention of the [cathode-ray tube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathode-ray_tube "Cathode-ray tube") (CRT), which is still commonly referred to as the "Braun tube"; today, the term typically refers to a high-vacuum tube, in which an electron beam can be deflected in both horizontal and vertical directions. The first version, developed in Strassburg in 1897, was far from perfect; it featured a [cold cathode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cold_cathode "Cold cathode") and a moderate vacuum, which required a 100,000 V [acceleration voltage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acceleration_voltage "Acceleration voltage") to produce a visible trace of the magnetically deflected beam. Furthermore, magnetic deflection affected only one direction, while the other was controlled by a rotating mirror placed in front of the [phosphorescent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phosphorescent "Phosphorescent") screen.

However, industry immediately recognized the potential of the invention, leading to its further development. By 1899, his assistant, [Jonathan Zenneck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonathan_Zenneck "Jonathan Zenneck"), introduced oscillations to magnetically control the Y deflection, and later improvements included the addition of a heated cathode, a [Wehnelt cylinder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wehnelt_cylinder "Wehnelt cylinder"), and high-vacuum technology. This tube was not only used for [oscilloscopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oscilloscope "Oscilloscope"), but also for fully [electronic television](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_television "Electronic television") transmission as a picture tube for [television sets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Television_set "Television set"), although Braun had considered it unsuitable for television.

The CRT became the cornerstone in developing fully electronic television, being a part of every TV, computer, and any other screen setup till the introduction of the [LCD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LCD "LCD") screen at the end of the 20th century. It is still occasionally called the "Braun tube" in German-speaking countries (*[Braunsche Röhre](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Braunsche%20Röhre#German "wikt:Braunsche Röhre")*) and other countries such as Korea (브라운관: *Buraun-kwan*) and Japan ([ブラウン管](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/ブラウン管#Japanese "wikt:ブラウン管"): *Buraun-kan*).

### Radio receiver

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/92/Braun_wireless_receiving_transformer_1905.jpg/250px-Braun_wireless_receiving_transformer_1905.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Braun_wireless_receiving_transformer_1905.jpg)

An early [resonant transformer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resonant_transformer "Resonant transformer") invented by Braun used in the coherer radio receivers in wireless telegraphy radio systems made by the [Telefunken](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telefunken "Telefunken") company in 1903

Following the invention of his tube, Braun began researching in the field of [wireless telegraphy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wireless_telegraphy "Wireless telegraphy"). A key issue in early radio technology was the development of a reliable receiver. Braun, as a physicist, was accustomed to working under reproducible experimental conditions, which the commonly used [coherer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coherer "Coherer") receivers at the time failed to meet. He replaced the coherer with a [crystal detector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal_detector "Crystal detector"), which greatly improved the sensitivity of the receiver, although the crystal detector required frequent re-adjustment. It was only later that the [electron tube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacuum_tube "Vacuum tube") replaced the crystal detector, although devices like germanium diodes continued to be used in simpler receivers for some time. The first FM [radar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radar "Radar") systems still employed a crystal detector.

In late 1898, the technology was commercialized when the chocolate manufacturer from Cologne, Ludwig Stollwerck, founded a consortium to exploit Braun's patents, contributing 560,000 marks in capital. After the successful transmission of signals over longer distances, the consortium was transformed into the "Professor Braun’s Telegraphy Company," which eventually became [Telefunken AG](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telefunken "Telefunken"). They set up the first world-wide network of communications, and was the first in the world to sell electronic televisions with [cathode-ray tubes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathode-ray_tube "Cathode-ray tube") in Germany in 1934. In 1900, Stollwerck facilitated contact with Professor August Raps, head of the [Siemens & Halske](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siemens_&_Halske "Siemens & Halske") Telegraph Construction Company, which later took over the development of the apparatus.

See more: [Crystal detector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal_detector "Crystal detector")

### Radio transmitter

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/6f/Braunsche_Telegraphiesystem_1898.jpg/250px-Braunsche_Telegraphiesystem_1898.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Braunsche_Telegraphiesystem_1898.jpg)

Braun's two circuits to send and receive

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b4/KF_Braun.png/250px-KF_Braun.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:KF_Braun.png)

Braun in his laboratory, 1904

Braun also made significant contributions to radio transmission technology. While [Guglielmo Marconi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guglielmo_Marconi "Guglielmo Marconi") had developed his transmitter primarily through empirical methods, Braun was able to improve it by focusing on the underlying physics. Originally, the resonant and antenna circuits were combined, but he separated them into two parts: a primary circuit consisting of a [capacitor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capacitor "Capacitor") and spark gap, and an antenna circuit inductively coupled to it. This innovation allowed for greater energy transmission in the system.

By 1898, the resulting powerful systems made the term "long-distance telegraphy" more appropriate, as the maximum range, previously limited to 20 km, steadily increased. On 24 September 1900, a radio link was successfully established between [Cuxhaven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuxhaven "Cuxhaven") and [Helgoland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helgoland "Helgoland") over a distance of 62 km. On 12 December 1901, Marconi received radio signals at his station in [Poldhu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poldhu "Poldhu"), Cornwall, at Signal Hill in [St. Johns, Newfoundland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Johns,_Newfoundland "St. Johns, Newfoundland"), using a transmitter designed in Braun's circuit. Whether this reception actually occurred remains debated in the literature.

Meanwhile, Braun attempted to replace the spark-gap transmitter, which produced damped oscillations, with AC generators that generated undamped oscillations, though he was unable to implement a feedback loop using electron tubes at the time.

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d4/FerdinandBraun_drahtlose_Station_Telegraphie_crop.jpg/250px-FerdinandBraun_drahtlose_Station_Telegraphie_crop.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:FerdinandBraun_drahtlose_Station_Telegraphie_crop.jpg)

A Braun mobile station, 1903

Together with [Georg Graf von Arco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georg_Graf_von_Arco "Georg Graf von Arco") and [Adolf Slaby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adolf_Slaby "Adolf Slaby"), Braun was part of the team that developed the concept for "mobile stations for wireless telegraphy for military purposes," which in 1903 led to a practical implementation by [AEG](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AEG_(German_company) "AEG (German company)") and Siemens & Halske. The system consisted of two horse-drawn wagons: one with all the transmitting and receiving equipment, including a battery, and the other with auxiliary and reserve supplies. This allowed the wagons to be separated in difficult terrain, as the station could still operate with just the front wagon.

See more: [Wireless telegraphy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wireless_telegraphy "Wireless telegraphy")

### Antennas

Braun also focused on early problems in directional radio—the alignment of transmitting and receiving antennas. He was among the first to achieve directed radiation and optimized antenna performance through calculations.

### Electroscope

Braun is also credited with the invention of the pointer [electroscope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electroscope "Electroscope"), which was named after him.
