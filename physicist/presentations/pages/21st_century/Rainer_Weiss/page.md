**Rainer Weiss** ([/waɪs/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English") [*WYSSE*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:Pronunciation_respelling_key "Help:Pronunciation respelling key"), German: [[vaɪs]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Standard_German "Help:IPA/Standard German"); September 29, 1932 – August 25, 2025) was a German-American physicist, known for his contributions in [gravitational physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitation "Gravitation") and [astrophysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrophysics "Astrophysics"). He was a professor of physics at the [Massachusetts Institute of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massachusetts_Institute_of_Technology "Massachusetts Institute of Technology") and an adjunct professor at [Louisiana State University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louisiana_State_University "Louisiana State University"). He is best known for inventing the laser interferometric technique which is the basic operation of [LIGO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LIGO "LIGO"). He was Chair of the [COBE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmic_Background_Explorer "Cosmic Background Explorer") Science Working Group.

In 2017, Weiss was awarded the [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics"), along with [Kip Thorne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kip_Thorne "Kip Thorne") and [Barry Barish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barry_Barish "Barry Barish"), "for decisive contributions to the LIGO detector and the observation of gravitational waves".

Weiss helped realize a number of challenging experimental tests of fundamental physics. He was a member of the [Fermilab Holometer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermilab_Holometer "Fermilab Holometer") experiment, which uses a 40m [laser interferometer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michelson_Interferometer "Michelson Interferometer") to measure properties of space and time at quantum scale and provide Planck-precision tests of quantum [holographic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holographic_principle "Holographic principle") fluctuation.

## Early life and education

Rainer Weiss was born in Berlin, [Brandenburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Province_of_Brandenburg "Province of Brandenburg"), [Prussia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_State_of_Prussia "Free State of Prussia"), [Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weimar_Republic "Weimar Republic"), on September 29, 1932, the son of Gertrude Loesner and Frederick A. Weiss. His father, a physician, neurologist, and psychoanalyst, was forced out of Germany by [Nazis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazis "Nazis") because he was [Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashkenazi_Jews "Ashkenazi Jews") and an active member of the [Communist Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communist_Party_of_Germany "Communist Party of Germany"). His mother, an actress, was Christian. His aunt was the sociologist [Hilda Weiss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilda_Weiss "Hilda Weiss"). His younger sister is playwright [Sybille Pearson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sybille_Pearson "Sybille Pearson").

The family fled first to [Prague](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prague "Prague"), but [Germany's occupation of Czechoslovakia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_occupation_of_Czechoslovakia "German occupation of Czechoslovakia") after the 1938 [Munich Agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Munich_Agreement "Munich Agreement") caused them to flee again; the philanthropic Stix family of [St. Louis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Louis,_Missouri "St. Louis, Missouri") helped them obtain visas to enter the United States. Weiss spent his youth in New York City, where he attended [Columbia Grammar School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia_Grammar_&_Preparatory_School "Columbia Grammar & Preparatory School").

He studied at [MIT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massachusetts_Institute_of_Technology "Massachusetts Institute of Technology"), dropping out at the beginning of his junior year with the excuse that he had abandoned his coursework to pursue a romantic relationship with a music student from Chicago. While this affair was a contributing factor, Weiss's concurrent vacillation between MIT's engineering and physics tracks may also have played a significant role. [Jerrold Zacharias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerrold_R._Zacharias "Jerrold R. Zacharias"), then an influential physicist and MIT professor, intervened, and Weiss, after working as a technician in Zacharias's lab, eventually returned to receive his [S.B.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_Science "Bachelor of Science") degree in 1955. He would complete his PhD in 1962, still with Zacharias as advisor/mentor.

## Career

Weiss taught at [Tufts University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tufts_University "Tufts University") from 1960 to 1962, was a postdoctoral scholar at [Princeton University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University "Princeton University") from 1962 to 1964, and then joined the faculty at MIT in 1964 starting a new research group in the [Research Laboratory of Electronics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Research_Laboratory_of_Electronics "Research Laboratory of Electronics") dedicated to research in cosmology and gravitation.

For Weiss's initial work at MIT, he started a group studying [cosmology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmology "Cosmology") and [gravitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitation "Gravitation"). Needing to develop new technology, particularly in regards to the stabilization of equipment set to measure minute fluctuations, his lab included machine and electronics shop, with a hands-on expectation of his students for fabrication and design.

By 1966, Weiss's tenure at MIT was at risk because of the failure of his group to produce publications. On advice from [Bernard Burke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernard_F._Burke "Bernard F. Burke"), then head of the division on astrophysics in the Physics Department, Weiss recalibrated his standards for submitting articles for publication, eventually finding grounds for publication that he believed met his personal standards as scientifically worthy and publishable. He was then able to qualify for tenure and remain at MIT.

That same year [Joseph Weber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Weber "Joseph Weber") claimed to have invented a way to detect gravitational waves. When Weiss's students asked him about Weber's work, he was unable to explain it to them, as it seemed to contradict his understanding of general relativity. In 1967, to illustrate the principle of gravitational wave detection in a simpler way, Weiss devised a thought experiment involving [time of flight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_of_flight "Time of flight") measurements of light between free masses in space, which in principle required "impossibly precise clocks". About a year later, as Weber's claims remained unconfirmed, Weiss started to realize that maybe Weber was wrong. He eventually revisited his idea and replaced the clocks with laser [interferometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interferometry "Interferometry") and concluded that such an approach could realistically detect gravitational waves, at sensitivities beyond what [Weber's resonant bars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weber_bar "Weber bar") could achieve.

### Vietnam Era cuts to science grants

In 1973, Weiss was forced to pivot with his work as the US military cut funding for any science that was not determined to be "directly relevant to its core mission." Weiss wrote a proposal to the NSF that described "a new way to measure gravitational waves." This was the work that would eventually lead to his 2017 Nobel Prize, though it was many years before the interferometers Weiss and his students built were sensitive enough to actually detect gravitational waves, making for numerous unpleasant doctoral thesis defenses where Weiss's graduate students were unable to present positive (in layman's terms: any) results.

### MIT/Caltech collaboration

Weiss proposed the concept of LIGO to [Kip Thorne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kip_Thorne "Kip Thorne") in 1972, but it took three years before Thorne was convinced it could work. After the study of prototypes at MIT, Caltech, Garching, and Glasgow, and Weiss's estimates what it would take to build a full scale interferometer, Caltech and MIT signed an agreement about the design and construction of LIGO in 1984, with joint leadership by [Ronald Drever](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Drever "Ronald Drever"), Weiss, and Thorne.

In a 2022 interview given to [Federal University of Pará](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_University_of_Pará "Federal University of Pará") in Brazil, Weiss talks about his life and career, the memories of his childhood and youth, his undergraduate and graduate studies at [MIT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MIT "MIT"), and the future of [gravitational waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_waves "Gravitational waves") astronomy.

## Achievements

Weiss brought two fields of fundamental physics research from birth to maturity: characterization of the cosmic background radiation, and interferometric gravitational wave observation.

In 1973 he made pioneering measurements of the spectrum of the [cosmic microwave background](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmic_microwave_background "Cosmic microwave background") radiation, taken from a [weather balloon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weather_balloon "Weather balloon"), showing that the microwave background exhibited the thermal spectrum characteristic of the remnant radiation from the [Big Bang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Bang "Big Bang"). He later became co-founder and science advisor of the [NASA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NASA "NASA") [Cosmic Background Explorer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmic_Background_Explorer "Cosmic Background Explorer") (COBE) satellite, which made detailed mapping of the radiation.

Weiss also pioneered the concept of using lasers for an interferometric [gravitational wave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_wave "Gravitational wave") detector, suggesting that the path length required for such a detector would necessitate kilometer-scale arms. He built a prototype in the 1970s, following earlier work by [Robert L. Forward](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_L._Forward "Robert L. Forward"). He co-founded the [NSF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Science_Foundation "National Science Foundation") LIGO (gravitational-wave detection) project, which was based on his report "A study of a long Baseline Gravitational Wave Antenna System".

Both of these efforts couple challenges in instrument science with physics important to the understanding of the Universe.

In February 2016, he was one of the four scientists of the [LIGO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LIGO_Scientific_Collaboration "LIGO Scientific Collaboration")/[Virgo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virgo_interferometer "Virgo interferometer") collaboration presenting at the press conference for the announcement that the [first direct gravitational wave observation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_observation_of_gravitational_waves "First observation of gravitational waves") had been made in September 2015.

Kip Thorne described Weiss as "by a large margin, the most influential person this field [the study of gravitational waves] has seen."

According the Nobel Prize website, Weiss received one half of the [2017 Nobel Prize for Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2017_Nobel_Prizes "2017 Nobel Prizes") prize money share, while his LIGO colleagues and co-winners [Barry Barish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barry_Barish "Barry Barish") and [Kip Thorne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kip_Thorne "Kip Thorne") only received one quarter of it.

## Personal life and death

Classical music was reportedly an influence and shaping force in Weiss's life, from his early youth in an immigrant family, through his shared love of Beethoven's Spring Sonata, which cemented his deep personal relationship with mentor Jerrold Zacharias.

He married and had his first child while still in graduate school, "the best time of my life." He was married to Rebecca Young from 1959 until his death, and they had two children.

Weiss died at a hospital in [Cambridge, Massachusetts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge,_Massachusetts "Cambridge, Massachusetts"), on August 25, 2025, at the age of 92.

## Honors and awards

Weiss has been recognized by numerous awards including:

- In 2006, with [John C. Mather](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_C._Mather "John C. Mather"), he and the COBE team received the [Gruber Prize in Cosmology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gruber_Prize_in_Cosmology "Gruber Prize in Cosmology").
- In 2007, with [Ronald Drever](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Drever "Ronald Drever"), he was awarded the APS [Einstein Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Einstein_Prize_(APS) "Einstein Prize (APS)") for his work.
- In 2016 and 2017, for the achievement of gravitational waves detection, he received:

        - The *[Smithsonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smithsonian_(magazine) "Smithsonian (magazine)")* magazine's American Ingenuity Award in the Physical Science category, with Kip Thorne and [Barry Barish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barry_Barish "Barry Barish").
        - The Willis E. Lamb Award for Laser Science and Quantum Optics, 2017.
        - The [Cocconi Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cocconi_Prize "Cocconi Prize") (2017) of the [European Physical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Physical_Society "European Physical Society") (jointly with [Kip Thorne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kip_Thorne "Kip Thorne") and [Barry Barish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barry_Barish "Barry Barish")).

- In 2020 he was elected a Legacy Fellow of the [American Astronomical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Astronomical_Society "American Astronomical Society").
