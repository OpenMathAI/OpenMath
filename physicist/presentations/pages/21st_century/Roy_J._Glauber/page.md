**Roy Jay Glauber** (September 1, 1925 – December 26, 2018) was an American [theoretical physicist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theoretical_physicist "Theoretical physicist"). He was the [Mallinckrodt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mallinckrodt "Mallinckrodt") [Professor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Professor "Professor") of [Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics "Physics") at [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University "Harvard University") and Adjunct Professor of Optical Sciences at the [University of Arizona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Arizona "University of Arizona"). Born in New York City, he was awarded one half of the 2005 [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics") "for his contribution to the quantum theory of [optical coherence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coherence_(physics) "Coherence (physics)")", with the other half shared by [John L. Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_L._Hall "John L. Hall") and [Theodor W. Hänsch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodor_W._Hänsch "Theodor W. Hänsch"). In this work, published in 1963, he created a model for [photodetection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photodetection "Photodetection") and explained the fundamental characteristics of different types of light, such as [laser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laser "Laser") light (see [coherent state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coherent_state "Coherent state")) and light from light bulbs (see [blackbody](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blackbody "Blackbody")). His theories are widely used in the field of [quantum optics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_optics "Quantum optics"). In [statistical physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_physics "Statistical physics") he pioneered the study of the dynamics of first-order [phase transitions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_transitions "Phase transitions"), since he first defined and investigated the [stochastic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stochastic "Stochastic") dynamics of an [Ising model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ising_model "Ising model") in a paper published in 1963.

He served on the National Advisory Board of the Center for Arms Control and Non-Proliferation, the research arm of [Council for a Livable World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_for_a_Livable_World "Council for a Livable World").

## Education

Glauber was born in 1925 in New York City the son of Felicia (Fox) and Emanuel B. Glauber. He was a member of the 1941 graduating class of the [Bronx High School of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronx_High_School_of_Science "Bronx High School of Science"), the first graduating class from that school. He then did his undergraduate work at [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University "Harvard University").

After his sophomore year, he was recruited to work on the [Manhattan Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manhattan_Project "Manhattan Project"), where (at the age of 18) he was one of the youngest scientists at [Los Alamos National Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Los_Alamos_National_Laboratory "Los Alamos National Laboratory"). His work involved calculating the [critical mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critical_mass "Critical mass") for the [atom bomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atom_bomb "Atom bomb"). After two years at Los Alamos, he returned to Harvard, receiving his bachelor's degree in 1946 and his PhD in 1949.

## Research

Glauber's research dealt with problems in a number of areas of quantum optics, a field which, broadly speaking, studies the [quantum electrodynamical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_electrodynamic "Quantum electrodynamic") interactions of light and matter. He also continued work on several topics in high-energy collision theory, including the analysis of [hadron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadron "Hadron") collisions, and the [statistical correlation of particles produced in high-energy reactions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glauber_multiple_scattering_theory "Glauber multiple scattering theory").

Roy Glauber’s early work on multiple scattering theory started in the 1950s and was continued with his students, such as Victor Franco.

Specific topics of his research included: the quantum mechanical behavior of trapped [wave packets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_packet "Wave packet"); interactions of light with trapped ions; atom counting-the statistical properties of free atom beams and their measurement; algebraic methods for dealing with [fermion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermion "Fermion") statistics; coherence and correlations of [bosonic atoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bosonic_atom "Bosonic atom") near the [Bose–Einstein condensation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bose–Einstein_condensation "Bose–Einstein condensation"); the theory of continuously monitored photon counting-and its reaction on quantum sources; the fundamental nature of "[quantum jumps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_electron_transition "Atomic electron transition")"; resonant transport of particles produced multiply in high-energy collisions; the multiple diffraction model of proton-proton and proton-antiproton scattering.

## Awards and honors

Glauber received the [Albert A. Michelson Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_A._Michelson_Medal "Albert A. Michelson Medal") from the [Franklin Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franklin_Institute "Franklin Institute") in [Philadelphia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philadelphia "Philadelphia") (1985), the Max Born Award from the [Optical Society of America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_Society_of_America "Optical Society of America") (1985), the [Dannie Heineman Prize for Mathematical Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dannie_Heineman_Prize_for_Mathematical_Physics "Dannie Heineman Prize for Mathematical Physics") from the [American Physical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Physical_Society "American Physical Society") (1996), and shared the 2005 [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics"). Glauber was awarded the 'Medalla de Oro del [CSIC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_National_Research_Council "Spanish National Research Council")' ('CSIC's Gold Medal') in a ceremony held in [Madrid, Spain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madrid,_Spain "Madrid, Spain"). He was elected a [Foreign Member of the Royal Society (ForMemRS) in 1997](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Fellows_of_the_Royal_Society_elected_in_1997 "List of Fellows of the Royal Society elected in 1997").

Glauber was awarded half the 2005 Nobel prize, along with experimentalists [John Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_L._Hall "John L. Hall") and [Theodor Hänsch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodor_W._Hänsch "Theodor W. Hänsch"), recognized for their work on precision spectroscopy.

### Ig Nobel

For many years before winning his Nobel Prize, Glauber took part in the [Ig Nobel Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ig_Nobel_Prize "Ig Nobel Prize") ceremonies, where he appeared each year as "Keeper of the Broom," sweeping the stage clean of the [paper airplanes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paper_airplane "Paper airplane") that have traditionally been thrown during the event. He missed the 2005 event as he was being awarded the Nobel Prize for Physics.

## Personal life

Glauber's father was a traveling salesman. When Glauber was six years of age, his mother gave birth to his sister, and the family settled in the New York City area. Glauber was very interested in astronomy as a child. In December 1937, along with several other children, he gave a presentation at the [American Museum of Natural History](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Museum_of_Natural_History "American Museum of Natural History") in New York City, about a reflecting telescope he had built on his own. The assistant to the director of the [Hayden Planetarium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hayden_Planetarium "Hayden Planetarium"), Dorothy Bennett, was present; she was a lecturer at the planetarium. Bennett was impressed with Glauber's work and she encouraged his membership and subsequent activity in an extra-curricular group for astronomy for the next few years. Glauber said she was "an influence in my life" and "a truly extraordinary spirit". After his work at Los Alamos, he visited her at her home, as she had moved to Taos, New Mexico.

Glauber lived in [Arlington, Massachusetts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arlington,_Massachusetts "Arlington, Massachusetts"). He was a guest scientist at the [European Organization for Nuclear Research (CERN)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CERN "CERN") in 1967, during a sabbatical. In 1951, he became a temporary lecturer at the [California Institute of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California_Institute_of_Technology "California Institute of Technology"), where he replaced [Richard Feynman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Feynman "Richard Feynman").

Glauber had a son and a daughter, and five grandchildren. He died on December 26, 2018, in [Newton, Massachusetts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton,_Massachusetts "Newton, Massachusetts"). He was one of the last surviving witnesses to the [Trinity nuclear test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_(nuclear_test) "Trinity (nuclear test)"). He was buried in [Kensico Cemetery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kensico_Cemetery "Kensico Cemetery"), [Valhalla, New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valhalla,_New_York "Valhalla, New York").

## Memoirs

A book by quantum physicist [José Ignacio Latorre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/José_Ignacio_Latorre?action=edit&redlink=1 "José Ignacio Latorre") and M.T. Soto-Sanfiel contains Roy J. Glauber's memoirs of the Manhattan Project and aspects of his scientific and personal life, based on a series of interviews conducted in Singapore, Spain, and the U.S. It has been published in English and Spanish. The same authors produced a documentary of the same name, "That's the Story: Roy J. Glauber Remembers the Making of the Atomic Bomb" featuring Glauber recounting some of his experiences within the Manhattan Project
