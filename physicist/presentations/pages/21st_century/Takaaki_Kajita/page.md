**Takaaki Kajita** (梶田 隆章, *Kajita Takaaki*; Japanese pronunciation: [[kadʑita takaːki]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Japanese "Help:IPA/Japanese"); born 9 March 1959) is a Japanese [physicist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physicist "Physicist"), known for [neutrino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutrino "Neutrino") experiments at the [Kamioka Observatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kamioka_Observatory "Kamioka Observatory") – [Kamiokande](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kamiokande "Kamiokande") and its successor, [Super-Kamiokande](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Super-Kamiokande "Super-Kamiokande"). In 2015, he was awarded the [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics") jointly with Canadian physicist [Arthur B. McDonald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_B._McDonald "Arthur B. McDonald") for the discovery of [neutrino oscillations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutrino_oscillations "Neutrino oscillations"). On 1 October 2020, he became the president of the [Science Council of Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_Council_of_Japan "Science Council of Japan").

## Early life and education

Kajita was born in 1959 in [Higashimatsuyama, Saitama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higashimatsuyama,_Saitama "Higashimatsuyama, Saitama"), Japan. He liked studying thought rather than memorizing, especially with interest in [physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics "Physics"), [biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biology "Biology"), [world history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_history_(field) "World history (field)"), [Japanese history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Japan "History of Japan"), and [earth science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth_science "Earth science") in high school. He studied physics at [Saitama University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saitama_University "Saitama University") and graduated in 1981. He received his [doctorate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctorate "Doctorate") in 1986 at the [University of Tokyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Tokyo "University of Tokyo"). At the University of Tokyo, he joined [Masatoshi Koshiba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masatoshi_Koshiba "Masatoshi Koshiba")'s research group because neutrinos "seemed like they might be interesting."

## Career and research

Since 1988, Kajita has been at the Institute for Cosmic Radiation Research, University of Tokyo, where he became an assistant professor in 1992 and professor in 1999.

He became director of the Center for Cosmic Neutrinos at the [Institute for Cosmic Ray Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_for_Cosmic_Ray_Research "Institute for Cosmic Ray Research") (ICRR) in 1999. As of 2017, he is a Principal Investigator at the [Institute for the Physics and Mathematics of the Universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_for_the_Physics_and_Mathematics_of_the_Universe "Institute for the Physics and Mathematics of the Universe") in Tokyo, and Director of ICRR.

In 1998, Kajita's team at the [Super-Kamiokande](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Super-Kamiokande "Super-Kamiokande") found that when [cosmic rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmic_ray "Cosmic ray") hit the Earth's atmosphere, the resulting neutrinos switched between two flavours before they reached the detector under Mt. Kamioka. This discovery helped prove the existence of [neutrino oscillation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutrino_oscillation "Neutrino oscillation") and that neutrinos have mass. In 2015, Kajita shared the [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics") with Canadian physicist [Arthur McDonald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_B._McDonald "Arthur B. McDonald"), whose [Sudbury Neutrino Observatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sudbury_Neutrino_Observatory "Sudbury Neutrino Observatory") discovered similar results. Kajita's and McDonald's work solved the longstanding [Solar neutrino problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_neutrino_problem "Solar neutrino problem"), which was a major discrepancy between the predicted and measured Solar neutrino fluxes, and indicated that the [Standard Model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_Model "Standard Model"), which required neutrinos to be massless, had weaknesses. In a news conference at the University of Tokyo, shortly after the Nobel announcement, Kajita said, "I want to thank the neutrinos, of course. And since neutrinos are created by cosmic rays, I want to thank them, too."

One of the first people Kajita called after receiving the Nobel Prize was 2002 Nobel physics laureate [Masatoshi Koshiba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masatoshi_Koshiba "Masatoshi Koshiba"), his former mentor and a fellow neutrino researcher.

Kajita served as chair of the commission on astroparticle physics (C4) of the [International Union of Pure and Applied Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IUPAP "IUPAP") in the period 2021–2024.

Kajita is currently the [principal investigator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principal_investigator "Principal investigator") of another ICRR project located at the [Kamioka Observatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kamioka_Observatory "Kamioka Observatory"), the [KAGRA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/KAGRA "KAGRA") [gravitational wave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_wave "Gravitational wave") detector.

### Awards

- 1987 – [Asahi Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asahi_Prize "Asahi Prize") as part of Kamiokande (Representative – Masatoshi Koshiba)
- 1989 – [Bruno Rossi Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bruno_Rossi_Prize "Bruno Rossi Prize") along with the other members of the Kamiokande collaboration
- 1998 – Asahi Prize as part of [Super-Kamiokande](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Super-Kamiokande "Super-Kamiokande") (Representative – Yoji Totsuka)
- 1999 – [Nishina Memorial Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nishina_Memorial_Prize "Nishina Memorial Prize")
- 2002 – [Panofsky Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panofsky_Prize "Panofsky Prize") for compelling experimental evidence for neutrino oscillations using atmospheric neutrinos
- 2010 – Yoji Totsuka Award
- 2012 – [Japan Academy Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan_Academy_Prize_(academics) "Japan Academy Prize (academics)") for "Discovery of Atmospheric Neutrino Oscillations"
- 2013 – Julius Wess Award for his "significant role in the Discovery of Atmospheric Neutrino Oscillations with the Super-KAMIOKANDE Experiment."
- 2015 – [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics") jointly with [Arthur B. McDonald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_B._McDonald "Arthur B. McDonald") for the discovery of [neutrino oscillations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutrino_oscillation "Neutrino oscillation"), which shows that neutrinos have mass.
- 2016 – [Breakthrough Prize in Fundamental Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breakthrough_Prize_in_Fundamental_Physics "Breakthrough Prize in Fundamental Physics")
- 2016 – [Asian Scientist 100](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asian_Scientist_100 "Asian Scientist 100"), *[Asian Scientist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asian_Scientist "Asian Scientist")*
- 2019 – [Homi Bhabha Medal and Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homi_Bhabha_Medal_and_Prize "Homi Bhabha Medal and Prize")
