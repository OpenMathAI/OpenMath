**Syukuro "Suki" Manabe**  (真鍋 淑郎, *Manabe Shukurō*; born 21 September 1931) is a Japanese–[American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Americans "Americans") [physicist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physicist "Physicist"), [meteorologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meteorologist "Meteorologist"), and [climatologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climatologist "Climatologist"), who pioneered the use of computers to [simulate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_simulation "Computer simulation") global [climate change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change "Climate change") and [natural climate variations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_variability_and_change "Climate variability and change"). He was awarded the 2021 [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics") jointly with [Klaus Hasselmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klaus_Hasselmann "Klaus Hasselmann") and [Giorgio Parisi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giorgio_Parisi "Giorgio Parisi"), for his contributions to the [physical modeling of Earth's climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_model "Climate model"), quantifying its variability, and predictions of climate change.

## Early life and education

Born in 1931 in [Shinritsu Village](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/新立村 "ja:新立村"), [Uma District](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uma_District,_Ehime "Uma District, Ehime"), [Ehime Prefecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ehime_Prefecture "Ehime Prefecture"), [Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empire_of_Japan "Empire of Japan"). Both his grandfather and his father were physicians, who operated the only clinic in the village. A classmate recalled that, even in elementary school, he was already "interested in the weather, making comments such as 'If Japan didn't have [typhoons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Typhoon "Typhoon"), we wouldn't have so much rain.'" Manabe attended [Ehime Prefectural Mishima High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ehime_Prefectural_Mishima_High_School "Ehime Prefectural Mishima High School"). When he was accepted into the [University of Tokyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Tokyo "University of Tokyo"), his family expected him to study medicine, but "whenever there's an emergency, the blood rushes to my head, so I would not have made a good doctor." Furthermore, "I had a horrible memory and I was clumsy with my hands. I thought that my only good trait was to gaze at the sky and get lost in my thoughts." He joined the research team of Shigekata Shono (1911–1969), and majored in [meteorology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meteorology "Meteorology"). Manabe received a [BA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_Arts "Bachelor of Arts") degree in 1953, an [MA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master_of_Arts "Master of Arts") degree in 1955, and a [DSc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Science "Doctor of Science") degree in 1958, all from the University of Tokyo.

## Career

After finishing his doctorate, Manabe went to the United States to work at the General Circulation Research Section of the U.S. Weather Bureau, now the [Geophysical Fluid Dynamics Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geophysical_Fluid_Dynamics_Laboratory "Geophysical Fluid Dynamics Laboratory") of [NOAA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NOAA "NOAA"), continuing until 1997. From 1997 to 2001, he worked at the Frontier Research System for Global Change in Japan serving as Director of the Global Warming Research Division. In 2002 he returned to the United States as a visiting research collaborator at the Program in Atmospheric and Oceanic Science, [Princeton University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University "Princeton University"). He currently serves as senior meteorologist at the university. He also engaged as a specially invited professor at [Nagoya University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nagoya_University "Nagoya University") from December 2007 to March 2014.

## Scientific accomplishments

Working at [NOAA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NOAA "NOAA")'s [Geophysical Fluid Dynamics Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geophysical_Fluid_Dynamics_Laboratory "Geophysical Fluid Dynamics Laboratory"), first in Washington, DC and later in [Princeton, New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton,_New_Jersey "Princeton, New Jersey"), Manabe worked with director [Joseph Smagorinsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Smagorinsky "Joseph Smagorinsky") to develop [three-dimensional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3D_computer_graphics "3D computer graphics") [models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Model_(abstract) "Model (abstract)") of the [atmosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmosphere "Atmosphere"). As the first step, [Manabe and Wetherald (1967)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_Equilibrium_of_the_Atmosphere_with_a_Given_Distribution_of_Relative_Humidity "Thermal Equilibrium of the Atmosphere with a Given Distribution of Relative Humidity") developed a one-dimensional, single-column model of the atmosphere in radiative-convective equilibrium with positive feedback effect of water vapor. Using the model, they found that, in response to the change in atmospheric concentration of carbon dioxide, the temperature increases at the Earth's surface and in the troposphere, whereas it decreases in the stratosphere.

The development of the radiative-convective model was a critically important step towards the development of comprehensive general circulation model of the atmosphere (Manabe et al. 1965). They used the model to simulate for the first time the three-dimensional response of temperature and the hydrologic cycle to increased carbon dioxide (Manabe and Wetherald, 1975). In 1969, Manabe and Bryan published the first simulations of the climate by a coupled ocean-atmosphere models, in which the general circulation model of the atmosphere is combined with that of ocean.

Throughout the 1990s and early 2000s, Manabe's research group published seminal papers using the coupled atmosphere ocean models to investigate the time-dependent response of [climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate "Climate") to changing [greenhouse gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenhouse_gas "Greenhouse gas") concentrations of the atmosphere (Stouffer et al., 1989; Manabe et al., 1991 & 1992). They also applied the model to the study of past climate change, including the role of freshwater input to the North Atlantic Ocean as a potential cause of the so-called, [abrupt climate change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abrupt_climate_change "Abrupt climate change") evident in the paleoclimatic record (Manabe and Stouffer, 1995 & 2000).

## Awards and honors

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/af/Syukuro_Manabe_cropped_1_Syukuro_Manabe_20211103.jpg/250px-Syukuro_Manabe_cropped_1_Syukuro_Manabe_20211103.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Syukuro_Manabe_cropped_1_Syukuro_Manabe_20211103.jpg)

Portrait of Manabe (released by the [Ministry of Education, Culture, Sports, Science and Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Education,_Culture,_Sports,_Science_and_Technology "Ministry of Education, Culture, Sports, Science and Technology") when Manabe received the [Order of Culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_Culture "Order of Culture"))

Manabe is a member of the [United States National Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_National_Academy_of_Sciences "United States National Academy of Sciences"), and a foreign member of Japan Academy, [Academia Europaea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academia_Europaea "Academia Europaea") and the [Royal Society of Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society_of_Canada "Royal Society of Canada").

In 1992, Manabe was the first recipient of the Blue Planet Prize of the Asahi Glass Foundation. In 1995, he received the Asahi Prize from Asahi News-Cultural Foundation. In 1997 Manabe was awarded the Volvo Environmental Prize from the Volvo Foundation. In 2015 he was awarded the Benjamin Franklin Medal of Franklin Institute.

Manabe has also been honored with the [American Meteorological Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Meteorological_Society "American Meteorological Society")'s [Carl-Gustaf Rossby Research Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl-Gustaf_Rossby_Research_Medal "Carl-Gustaf Rossby Research Medal"), the Second Half Century Award, and the Clarence Leroy Meisinger Award. In addition, he is honored with the [American Geophysical Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Geophysical_Union "American Geophysical Union")'s William Bowie Medal and Revelle Medal, and in 1998 received the [Milutin Milankovic Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milutin_Milankovic_Medal "Milutin Milankovic Medal") from the [European Geophysical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Geophysical_Society "European Geophysical Society").

Manabe and Bryan's work in the development of the first [global climate models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_climate_model "Global climate model") has been selected as one of the Top Ten Breakthroughs to have occurred in [NOAA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NOAA "NOAA")'s first 200 years. In honor of his retirement from [NOAA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NOAA "NOAA") / [GFDL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geophysical_Fluid_Dynamics_Laboratory "Geophysical Fluid Dynamics Laboratory"), a three-day scientific meeting was held in [Princeton, New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton,_New_Jersey "Princeton, New Jersey") in March 1998. "Understanding Climate Change: A Symposium in honor of Syukuro Manabe". The 2005 annual meeting of [American Meteorological Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Meteorological_Society "American Meteorological Society") included a special Suki Manabe Symposium.

Jointly with climatologist [James Hansen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Hansen "James Hansen"), Manabe received the [BBVA Foundation Frontiers of Knowledge Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBVA_Foundation_Frontiers_of_Knowledge_Award "BBVA Foundation Frontiers of Knowledge Award") in the Climate Change category in the ninth edition (2016) of the awards. The two laureates were separately responsible for constructing the first computational models with the power to simulate climate behavior. Decades ago, they correctly predicted how much Earth's temperature would rise due to increasing atmospheric CO2. The scores of models currently in use to chart climate evolution are heirs to those developed by Manabe and Hansen.

In 2018, Manabe received the [Crafoord Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crafoord_Prize "Crafoord Prize") in Geosciences jointly with [Susan Solomon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susan_Solomon "Susan Solomon") "for fundamental contributions to understanding the role of atmospheric trace gases in Earth's climate system".

In 2021, he received the [Order of Culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_Culture "Order of Culture").

In 2022, Manabe was named by [Carnegie Corporation of New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carnegie_Corporation_of_New_York "Carnegie Corporation of New York") as an honoree of the [Great Immigrants Awards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Immigrants_Award "Great Immigrants Award").

### The Nobel Prize

In 2021, one half of the [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics") was shared between Manabe and [Klaus Hasselmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klaus_Hasselmann "Klaus Hasselmann") "for the physical modeling of Earth's climate, quantifying variability and reliably predicting global warming". [Shuji Nakamura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shuji_Nakamura "Shuji Nakamura"), the 2014 Nobel Prize in Physics awardee who also came from [Ehime Prefecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ehime_Prefecture "Ehime Prefecture") and immigrated to the United States, congratulated Manabe on 6 October.
