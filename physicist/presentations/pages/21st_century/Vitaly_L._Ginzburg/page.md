**Vitaly Lazarevich Ginzburg** [ForMemRS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow_of_the_Royal_Society "Fellow of the Royal Society") (Russian: Вита́лий Ла́заревич Ги́нзбург; 4 October [[O.S.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Style_and_New_Style_dates "Old Style and New Style dates") 21 September] 1916 – 8 November 2009) was a [Russian physicist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Russian_physicists "List of Russian physicists") who was honored with the [Nobel Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics") in [Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics "Physics") in 2003, together with [Alexei Abrikosov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexei_Alexeyevich_Abrikosov "Alexei Alexeyevich Abrikosov") and [Anthony Leggett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthony_James_Leggett "Anthony James Leggett") for their "pioneering contributions to the theory of superconductors and superfluids."

He spent his career in the former [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union "Soviet Union") and was one of the leading figure in former [Soviet program of nuclear weapons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_atomic_bomb "Russian atomic bomb"), working towards designs of the [thermonuclear devices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RDS-37 "RDS-37"). He became a member of the [Russian Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Academy_of_Sciences "Russian Academy of Sciences") and succeeded [Igor Tamm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Igor_Tamm "Igor Tamm") as head of the Department of Theoretical Physics of the [Lebedev Physical Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebedev_Physical_Institute "Lebedev Physical Institute") of the Russian Academy of Sciences ([FIAN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebedev_Physical_Institute "Lebedev Physical Institute")). In his later life, Ginzburg become an outspoken [atheist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atheism "Atheism") and was critical of [clergy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Orthodox_Church "Russian Orthodox Church")'s influence in Russian society.

## Biography

Vitaly Ginzburg was born to a [Jewish family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Jews "Russian Jews") in Moscow on 4 October 1916— the son of an engineer, Lazar Yefimovich Ginzburg, and a doctor, Augusta Wildauer who was a graduate from the Physics Faculty of [Moscow State University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moscow_State_University "Moscow State University") in 1938. After attending his mother's alma mater, he defended his qualifications of the [candidate's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Candidate_of_Sciences "Candidate of Sciences") (*Kandidat Nauk*) dissertation in 1940, and his comprehensive thesis for the [doctor's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Sciences "Doctor of Sciences") (*Doktor Nauk*) qualification in 1942. In 1944, he became a member of the [Communist Party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communist_Party_of_the_Soviet_Union "Communist Party of the Soviet Union") of the Soviet Union. Among his achievements are a partially [phenomenological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phenomenological_model "Phenomenological model") theory of [superconductivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superconductivity "Superconductivity"), the [Ginzburg–Landau theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ginzburg–Landau_theory "Ginzburg–Landau theory"), developed with [Lev Landau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lev_Landau "Lev Landau") in 1950; the theory of [electromagnetic wave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_wave "Electromagnetic wave") propagation in [plasmas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasma_(physics) "Plasma (physics)") (for example, in the [ionosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionosphere "Ionosphere")); and a theory of the origin of [cosmic radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmic_radiation "Cosmic radiation"). He is also known to biologists as being part of the group of scientists that helped bring down the reign of the politically connected anti-[Mendelian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregor_Mendel "Gregor Mendel") [agronomist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agronomy "Agronomy") [Trofim Lysenko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trofim_Lysenko "Trofim Lysenko"), thus allowing modern [genetic science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetics "Genetics") to return to the [USSR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union "Soviet Union").

In 1937, Ginzburg married Olga Zamsha. In 1946, he married his second wife, Nina Ginzburg (*nee* Yermakova), who had spent more than a year in custody on fabricated charges of plotting to assassinate the Soviet leader [Joseph Stalin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Stalin "Joseph Stalin").

As a renowned professor and researcher, Ginzburg was an obvious candidate for the [Soviet bomb project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_atomic_bomb_project "Soviet atomic bomb project"). From 1948 through 1952 Ginzburg worked under [Igor Kurchatov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Igor_Kurchatov "Igor Kurchatov") to help with the [hydrogen bomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_bomb "Hydrogen bomb"). Ginzburg and [Igor Tamm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Igor_Tamm "Igor Tamm") both proposed ideas that would make it possible to build a hydrogen bomb. When the bomb project moved to [Arzamas-16](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarov "Sarov") to continue in even more secrecy, Ginzburg was not allowed to follow. Instead he stayed in Moscow and supported from afar, staying under watch due to his background and past. As the work got continuously more classified, Ginzburg was phased out of the project and allowed to pursue his true passion, superconductors. During the [Cold War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cold_War "Cold War"), the thirst for knowledge and technological advancement was never-ending. This was no different with the research done on superconductors. The Soviet Union believed that the research done on superconductors would place them ahead of their American counterparts. Both sides sought to leverage the [potential military applications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Railgun "Railgun") of superconductors.

Ginzburg was the [editor-in-chief](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Editor-in-chief "Editor-in-chief") of the [scientific journal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_journal "Scientific journal") *[Uspekhi Fizicheskikh Nauk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uspekhi_Fizicheskikh_Nauk "Uspekhi Fizicheskikh Nauk")*. He also headed the Academic Department of Physics and Astrophysics Problems, which Ginzburg founded at the [Moscow Institute of Physics and Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moscow_Institute_of_Physics_and_Technology "Moscow Institute of Physics and Technology") in 1968.

Ginzburg identified as a secular Jew, and following the [collapse of communism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collapse_of_communism "Collapse of communism") in the former Soviet Union, he was very active in Jewish life, especially in Russia, where he served on the board of directors of the [Russian Jewish Congress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Jewish_Congress "Russian Jewish Congress"). He is also well known for fighting anti-Semitism and supporting the state of [Israel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Israel "Israel").

In the 2000s (decade), Ginzburg was politically active, supporting the Russian liberal opposition and [human rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_rights_in_Russia "Human rights in Russia") movement. He defended [Igor Sutyagin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Igor_Sutyagin "Igor Sutyagin") and [Valentin Danilov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valentin_Danilov "Valentin Danilov") against charges of espionage put forth by the authorities. On 2 April 2009, in an interview to the [Radio Liberty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_Liberty "Radio Liberty") Ginzburg denounced the [FSB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Security_Service "Federal Security Service") as an institution harmful to Russia and the ongoing expansion of its authority as a return to [Stalinism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stalinism "Stalinism").

Ginzburg worked at the [P. N. Lebedev Physical Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebedev_Physical_Institute "Lebedev Physical Institute") of [Soviet and Russian Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Academy_of_Sciences "Russian Academy of Sciences") in Moscow since 1940. [Russian Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Academy_of_Sciences "Russian Academy of Sciences") is a major institution where mostly all Nobel Prize laureates of physics from Russia have done their studies and/or research works.

## Stance on religion

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/1e/Vitaly_Ginzburg.jpg/250px-Vitaly_Ginzburg.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Vitaly_Ginzburg.jpg)

Ginzburg, Moscow, December 1980.

Ginzburg was an avowed atheist, both under the primarily atheist Soviet government and in post-Communist Russia when religion made a strong revival. He criticized [clericalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clericalism "Clericalism") in the [press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Journalism "Journalism") and wrote several books devoted to the questions of [religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion "Religion") and atheism. Because of this, some Orthodox Christian groups denounced him and said no science [award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Award "Award") could excuse his verbal attacks on the [Russian Orthodox Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Orthodox_Church "Russian Orthodox Church"). He was one of the signers of the [Open letter to the President Vladimir V. Putin from the Members of the Russian Academy of Sciences against clericalisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_letter_to_the_President_Vladimir_V._Putin_from_the_Members_of_the_Russian_Academy_of_Sciences_against_clericalisation "Open letter to the President Vladimir V. Putin from the Members of the Russian Academy of Sciences against clericalisation") of Russia.

## Nobel Prize

Vitaly Ginzburg, along with [Anthony Leggett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthony_James_Leggett "Anthony James Leggett") and [Alexei Abrikosov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexei_Abrikosov_(physicist) "Alexei Abrikosov (physicist)") were awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics in 2003 for their groundbreaking work on the theory of [superconductors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superconductivity "Superconductivity"). The Nobel Prize recognized Ginzburg's work in [theoretical physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theoretical_physics "Theoretical physics"), specifically his contributions to understanding the behavior of matter at extremely low temperatures.

His collaboration with [Lev Landau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lev_Landau "Lev Landau") in 1950 led to the development of the [Ginzburg-Landau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ginzburg–Landau_theory "Ginzburg–Landau theory") theory, which became paramount to later work on superconductors. Landau had been working on superconductors for years before their partnership, with Landau publishing many papers between 1941 and 1947 on the properties of [quantum fluids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_fluid "Quantum fluid") at extremely low temperatures. Lev Landau would later receive a Nobel Prize in 1962 for this research on the properties of the [superfluid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superfluidity "Superfluidity") liquid helium in 1941. Before their collaboration, Landau had just done research on liquid helium and other quantum fluids, but Ginzburg allowed them to go a step further.

Ginzburg introduced the concept of an order parameter, which would allow them to characterize the state of the superconductor. To do this, they derived a [complex set of equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ginzburg–Landau_equation "Ginzburg–Landau equation") that would allow them to describe the behavior of the superconductor. These equations provided a model from which researchers can understand the transition between a normal and superconducting state, as well as be able to predict various properties of other superconductors. Using these equations, they were also able to introduce the Ginzburg–Landau parameter. This parameter used a separate set of equations in order to classify if they were looking at a [type-I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type-I_superconductor "Type-I superconductor") or [type-II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type-II_superconductor "Type-II superconductor") superconductor. This advancement allowed Anthony Leggett to build upon it and complete his own research on superconductors.

Applications of his research findings include [MRI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_resonance_imaging "Magnetic resonance imaging") machines, [engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superconducting_electric_machine "Superconducting electric machine"), and new [Maglev trains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maglev "Maglev").

## Death

A spokeswoman for the Russian Academy of Sciences announced that Ginzburg died in Moscow on 8 November 2009 from [cardiac arrest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiac_arrest "Cardiac arrest"). He had been suffering from ill health for several years, and three years before his death said "In general, I envy believers. I am 90, and [am] being overcome by illnesses. For believers, it is easier to deal with them and with life's other hardships. But what can be done? I cannot believe in resurrection after death."

Prime Minister of Russia [Vladimir Putin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladimir_Putin "Vladimir Putin") sent his condolences to Ginzburg's family, saying "We bid farewell to an extraordinary personality whose outstanding talent, exceptional strength of character and firmness of convictions evoked true respect from his colleagues". [President of Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_of_Russia "President of Russia") [Dmitry Medvedev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dmitry_Medvedev "Dmitry Medvedev"), in his letter of condolences, described Ginzburg as a "top physicist of our time whose discoveries had a huge impact on the development of national and world science."

Ginzburg was buried on 11 November in the [Novodevichy Cemetery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Novodevichy_Cemetery "Novodevichy Cemetery") in Moscow, the resting place of many famous politicians, writers and scientists of Russia.

## Ginzburg's list

Over the years, Ginzburg compiled a [list of unsolved problems in physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_unsolved_problems_in_physics "List of unsolved problems in physics"). In 1971 the list included 17 problems, and 20 problems in 1985. In 1999, he published his list of 30 problems for the 21st century. During his 2003 Nobel lecture he presented the list, and was finally published in his *About Science, Myself and Others* in 2005. Physicist Ivan Bozovic who knew the Ginzburg family referred to it as "The Ginzburg's list". The list includes:

1. Controlled [nuclear fusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_fusion "Nuclear fusion").
2. [High-temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-temperature_superconductivity "High-temperature superconductivity") and [room-temperature superconductivity.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Room-temperature_superconductor "Room-temperature superconductor")
3. [Metallic hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metallic_hydrogen "Metallic hydrogen"). Other exotic substances.
4. [Two-dimensional electron liquid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-dimensional_electron_gas "Two-dimensional electron gas") (the [anomalous Hall effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anomalous_Hall_effect "Anomalous Hall effect") and other effects).
5. Some questions of solid-state physics ([heterostructures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heterojunction "Heterojunction") in semiconductors, [quantum wells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_well "Quantum well") and [dots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_dot "Quantum dot"), metal-dielectric transitions, charge- and spin-density waves, [mesoscopics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesoscopic_physics "Mesoscopic physics")).
6. Second-order and related phase transitions. Some examples of such transitions. Cooling (in particular, [laser cooling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laser_cooling "Laser cooling")) to superlow temperatures. [Bose-Einstein condensation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bose–Einstein_condensate "Bose–Einstein condensate") in gases.
7. [Surface physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_science "Surface science"). [Clusters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cluster_(physics) "Cluster (physics)").
8. [Liquid crystals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid_crystal "Liquid crystal"). [Ferroelectrics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferroelectricity "Ferroelectricity"). Ferrotoroics.
9. [Fullerenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fullerene "Fullerene"). [Nanotubes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_nanotube "Carbon nanotube").
10. The behavior of matter in superstrong magnetic fields.
11. Nonlinear physics. [Turbulence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turbulence "Turbulence"). [Solitons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soliton "Soliton"). [Chaos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chaos_theory "Chaos theory"). Strange [attractors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attractor "Attractor").
12. [X-ray lasers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray_laser "X-ray laser"), [gamma-ray lasers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamma-ray_laser "Gamma-ray laser"), superhigh-power lasers.
13. [Superheavy elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superheavy_element "Superheavy element"). [Exotic nuclei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypernucleus "Hypernucleus").
14. [Mass spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_gap "Mass gap"). [Quarks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quark "Quark") and [gluons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gluon "Gluon"). [Quantum chromodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_chromodynamics "Quantum chromodynamics"). [Quark-gluon plasma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quark–gluon_plasma "Quark–gluon plasma").
15. [Unified theory of weak and electromagnetic interactions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electroweak_interaction "Electroweak interaction"). [W and Z bosons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W_and_Z_bosons "W and Z bosons"). [Leptons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lepton "Lepton").
16. [Standard Model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_Model "Standard Model"). [Grand unification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Unified_Theory "Grand Unified Theory"). [Superunification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theory_of_everything "Theory of everything"). [Proton decay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proton_decay "Proton decay"). [Neutrino mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutrino_mass_hierarchy "Neutrino mass hierarchy"). [Magnetic monopoles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_monopole "Magnetic monopole").
17. Fundamental length. Particle interaction at high and superhigh energies. [Colliders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collider "Collider").
18. [Nonconservation of CP invariance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CP_violation "CP violation").
19. Nonlinear phenomena in vacuum and in superstrong magnetic fields. Phase transitions in a vacuum.
20. [Strings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/String_theory "String theory"). [M-theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M-theory "M-theory").
21. [Experimental verification of the general theory of relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tests_of_general_relativity "Tests of general relativity").
22. [Gravitational waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_wave "Gravitational wave") and their detection.
23. The [cosmological problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmological_constant_problem "Cosmological constant problem"). [Inflation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmic_inflation "Cosmic inflation"). The [Λ term](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmological_constant "Cosmological constant") and “[quintessence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quintessence_(physics) "Quintessence (physics)").” Relationship between cosmology and high-energy physics.
24. [Neutron stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron_star "Neutron star") and [pulsars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulsar "Pulsar"). Supernova stars.
25. [Black holes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_hole "Black hole"). [Cosmic strings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmic_string "Cosmic string").
26. [Quasars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasar "Quasar") and [galactic nuclei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_galactic_nucleus "Active galactic nucleus"). Formation of galaxies.
27. The problem of [dark matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dark_matter "Dark matter") (hidden mass) and its detection.
28. The origin of [superhigh-energy cosmic rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultra-high-energy_cosmic_ray "Ultra-high-energy cosmic ray").
29. [Gamma-ray bursts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamma-ray_burst "Gamma-ray burst"). [Hypernovae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypernova "Hypernova").
30. Neutrino physics and astronomy. [Neutrino oscillations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutrino_oscillation "Neutrino oscillation").

Alongside the list he also discussed three "great problems":

1. The [increase in entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics "Second law of thermodynamics"), [time irreversibility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_reversibility "Time reversibility"), and the “[time arrow.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arrow_of_time "Arrow of time")”
2. The problem of [interpretation of nonrelativistic quantum mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interpretations_of_quantum_mechanics "Interpretations of quantum mechanics") and the possibility of learning something new even in the field of its applicability.
3. The question of the [emergence of life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abiogenesis "Abiogenesis"), i.e., the feasibility of explaining the origin of life and thought on the basis of physics alone.

## Family

The first wife (in 1937–1946) is a graduate of the Faculty of Physics of [Moscow State University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moscow_State_University "Moscow State University") (1938) Olga Ivanovna Zamsha (born 1915, [Yeysk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yeysk "Yeysk")), candidate of physical and mathematical sciences (1945), associate professor at [MEPhI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MEPhI "MEPhI") (1949–1985), author of the "Collection of problems on general physics" (with co-authors, 1968, 1972, 1975).
The second wife (since 1946) is a graduate of the Faculty of Mechanics and Mathematics of Moscow State University, experimental physicist Nina Ivanovna Ginzburg (née Ermakova) (October 2, 1922 — May 19, 2019).
