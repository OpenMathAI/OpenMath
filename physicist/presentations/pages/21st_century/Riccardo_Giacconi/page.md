**Riccardo Giacconi** ([/dʒəˈkoʊni/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English") [*jə-KOH-nee*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:Pronunciation_respelling_key "Help:Pronunciation respelling key"), Italian: [[rikˈkardo dʒakˈkoːni]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Italian "Help:IPA/Italian"); October 6, 1931 – December 9, 2018) was an Italian-American [Nobel Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize "Nobel Prize")-winning [astrophysicist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrophysicist "Astrophysicist") who laid down the foundations of [X-ray astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray_astronomy "X-ray astronomy"). He was a professor at the [Johns Hopkins University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johns_Hopkins_University "Johns Hopkins University").

## Biography

Born in [Genoa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genoa "Genoa"), Italy, Giacconi received his [Laurea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laurea#Former_status_of_the_Laurea_degree "Laurea") from the [Physics Department](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milan_school_of_physics "Milan school of physics") of [University of Milan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Milan "University of Milan") before moving to the US to pursue a career in [astrophysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrophysics "Astrophysics") research. In 1956, his [Fulbright Fellowship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S.-Italy_Fulbright_Commission "U.S.-Italy Fulbright Commission") led him to go to the United States to collaborate with physics professor R. W. Thompson at [Indiana University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indiana_University "Indiana University").

Since cosmic X-ray radiation is absorbed by the Earth's atmosphere, space-based telescopes are needed for X-ray astronomy. Applying himself to this problem, Giacconi worked on the instrumentation for X-ray astronomy; from rocket-borne detectors in the late 1950s and early 1960s, to [Uhuru](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uhuru_(satellite) "Uhuru (satellite)"), the first orbiting X-ray astronomy satellite, in the 1970s. Giacconi's pioneering research continued in 1978 with the [Einstein Observatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Einstein_Observatory "Einstein Observatory"), the first fully imaging X-ray telescope put into space, and later with the [Chandra X-ray Observatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chandra_X-ray_Observatory "Chandra X-ray Observatory"), which was launched in 1999 and is still in operation.

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b7/Giacconi_lg2.jpg/250px-Giacconi_lg2.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Giacconi_lg2.jpg)

Harvey Tananbaum, Riccardo Giacconi, [Martin C. Weisskopf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_C._Weisskopf "Martin C. Weisskopf") (Chandra Project Scientist) and [Claude R. Canizares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claude_R._Canizares "Claude R. Canizares") (PI for one of Chandra's instruments)

Giacconi applied his expertise to other fields of astronomy, becoming the first permanent director (1981-1993) of the [Space Telescope Science Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Telescope_Science_Institute "Space Telescope Science Institute") (the science operations center for the [Hubble Space Telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hubble_Space_Telescope "Hubble Space Telescope")). This was followed by Director General of the [European Southern Observatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Southern_Observatory "European Southern Observatory") (ESO) from 1993 to 1999, overseeing the construction of the [Very Large Telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Very_Large_Telescope "Very Large Telescope"), then President of [Associated Universities, Inc.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associated_Universities,_Inc. "Associated Universities, Inc.") (1999-2004) managing the early years of the [ALMA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atacama_Large_Millimeter_Array "Atacama Large Millimeter Array") array.

Giacconi was awarded a share of the [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics") in 2002 "for pioneering contributions to astrophysics, which have led to the discovery of [cosmic X-ray sources](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrophysical_X-ray_source "Astrophysical X-ray source")". The other shares of the Prize in that year were awarded to [Masatoshi Koshiba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masatoshi_Koshiba "Masatoshi Koshiba") and [Raymond Davis, Jr.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raymond_Davis,_Jr. "Raymond Davis, Jr.") for neutrino astronomy.

Giacconi held the positions of professor of physics and astronomy (1982–1997) and research professor (from 1998 to his death in 2018) at [Johns Hopkins University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johns_Hopkins_University "Johns Hopkins University"), and was a university professor. During the 2000s he was [principal investigator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principal_investigator "Principal investigator") for the major Chandra Deep Field-South project with [NASA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NASA "NASA")'s [Chandra X-ray Observatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chandra_X-ray_Observatory "Chandra X-ray Observatory"). Giacconi died on December 9, 2018.
