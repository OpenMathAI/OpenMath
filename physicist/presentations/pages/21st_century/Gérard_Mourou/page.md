**Gérard Albert Mourou** (French: [[ʒeʁaʁ muʁu]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/French "Help:IPA/French"); born 22 June 1944) is a French scientist and pioneer in the field of [electrical engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_engineering "Electrical engineering") and [lasers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lasers "Lasers"). He was awarded a [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics") in 2018, along with [Donna Strickland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donna_Strickland "Donna Strickland"), for the invention of [chirped pulse amplification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chirped_pulse_amplification "Chirped pulse amplification"), a technique later used to create ultrashort-pulse, very high-intensity ([petawatt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petawatt "Petawatt")) laser pulses.

In 1994, Mourou and his team at the [University of Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Michigan "University of Michigan") discovered that the balance between the self-focusing refraction (see [Kerr effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerr_effect "Kerr effect")) and self-attenuating [diffraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffraction "Diffraction") by [ionization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionization "Ionization") and rarefaction of a laser beam of terawatt intensities in the atmosphere creates "filaments" that act as [waveguides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waveguide "Waveguide") for the beam, thus preventing divergence.

## Career

Mourou was born on 22 June 1944 in [Albertville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albertville "Albertville"), France. Mourou obtained a [PhD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Philosophy "Doctor of Philosophy") degree from [Pierre and Marie Curie University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_and_Marie_Curie_University "Pierre and Marie Curie University") in 1973. He then moved to the United States, spending a year as a postdoctoral student at the [University of California San Diego](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_San_Diego "University of California, San Diego"). He later became a professor at the [University of Rochester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Rochester "University of Rochester") in 1977, where he and his then student [Donna Strickland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donna_Strickland "Donna Strickland") produced their Nobel prize-winning work in the [Laboratory for Laser Energetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laboratory_for_Laser_Energetics "Laboratory for Laser Energetics") at the university in the 1980s. The pair co-invented [chirped pulse amplification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chirped_pulse_amplification "Chirped pulse amplification"), a "method of generating high-intensity, ultra-short optical pulses". Strickland's doctoral thesis was on "development of an ultra-bright laser and an application to multi-photon ionization".

Mourou joined the [University of Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Michigan "University of Michigan") in 1988, where he became the founding director of the Center for Ultrafast Optical Science in 1990 or 1991. He subsequently returned to France as a professor at the [École polytechnique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/École_Polytechnique "École Polytechnique") to direct the Laboratoire d'optique appliquée at [ENSTA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ENSTA "ENSTA") from 2005 to 2009 and develop the [Extreme Light Infrastructure project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extreme_Light_Infrastructure "Extreme Light Infrastructure") from 2007 on.

In October 2024, he was appointed as a Chair Professor at Peking University. He is also the A. D. Moore Distinguished University Professor Emeritus at the University of Michigan.

### Nobel Prize

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/ec/Ecole_polytechnique_%2831183345568%29.jpg/330px-Ecole_polytechnique_%2831183345568%29.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Ecole_polytechnique_(31183345568).jpg)

Mourou, speaking in 2018 after being awarded the [Nobel Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics")

On 2 October 2018, Mourou and [Strickland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donna_Strickland "Donna Strickland") were awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics, for their joint work on [chirped pulse amplification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chirped_pulse_amplification "Chirped pulse amplification"). They shared half of the Prize, while the other half was awarded to [Arthur Ashkin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Ashkin "Arthur Ashkin") for his invention of "optical tweezers that grab particles, atoms, viruses and other living cells with their laser beam fingers".

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/1a/G%C3%A9rard_Mourou_EM1B5793_%2846234161551%29.jpg/330px-G%C3%A9rard_Mourou_EM1B5793_%2846234161551%29.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Gérard_Mourou_EM1B5793_(46234161551).jpg)

Gérard Mourou during Nobel press conference in [Stockholm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stockholm "Stockholm"), December 2018

Mourou and Strickland found that stretching a laser out reduced its peak power, which could then be greatly amplified using normal instruments.
It could then be compressed to create the short-lived, highly powerful lasers they were after. The technique, which was described in Strickland's first scientific publication, came to be known as chirped pulse amplification (CPA). They were probably unaware at the time that their tools would make it possible to study natural phenomena in unprecedented ways. CPA could also per definition be used to create a laser pulse that only lasts one attosecond, one-billionth of a billionth of a second. At those timescales, it became possible not only to study chemical reactions, but what happens inside individual atoms.

*[The Guardian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Guardian "The Guardian")* and [Scientific American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_American "Scientific American") provided simplified summaries of the work of Strickland and Mourou: it "paved the way for the shortest, most intense laser beams ever created". "The ultrabrief, ultrasharp beams can be used to make extremely precise cuts so their technique is now used in laser machining and enables doctors to perform millions of corrective" [laser eye surgeries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laser_eye_surgery "Laser eye surgery"). Canadian Prime Minister [Justin Trudeau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justin_Trudeau "Justin Trudeau") acknowledged the achievements of Mourou and Strickland: "Their innovative work can be found in applications including corrective eye surgery, and is expected to have a significant impact on cancer therapy and other physics research in the future".

## Controversy

At the time he received the Nobel prize, a publicity video filmed in 2010 to represent the [Extreme Light Infrastructure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extreme_Light_Infrastructure "Extreme Light Infrastructure") (ELI), went viral. It featured female students swaying around Mourou and losing their lab coats. It was widely criticized, included by the Nobel committee for its representation of women in sciences. Mourou commented it was intended as humoristic, and added: *"I am not very proud of this video".*
