**Albert Abraham Michelson** (December 19, 1852 – May 9, 1931) was an American experimental [physicist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physicist "Physicist") known for his work on measuring the [speed of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light "Speed of light") and especially for the [Michelson–Morley experiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michelson–Morley_experiment "Michelson–Morley experiment"). In 1907, he received the [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics"), becoming the first American to win the Nobel Prize in a science. He was the founder and the first head of the physics departments of the [Case School of Applied Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Case_School_of_Applied_Science "Case School of Applied Science") and the [University of Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Chicago "University of Chicago").

## Biography

### Early life

Albert Abraham Michelson was born on December 19, 1852, in Strelno (now [Strzelno](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strzelno "Strzelno"), Poland), [Prussia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prussia "Prussia"), into a [Polish-Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish-Jewish "Polish-Jewish") family. He moved to the United States with his parents in 1855, at the age of 2. He grew up in the mining towns of [Murphy's Camp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Murphys,_California "Murphys, California"), California, and [Virginia City, Nevada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virginia_City,_Nevada "Virginia City, Nevada"), where his father was a merchant. His family was non-religious, and Michelson himself was a lifelong [agnostic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agnostic "Agnostic"). He spent his high school years in San Francisco in the home of his aunt, Henriette Levy (née Michelson), who was the mother of author [Harriet Lane Levy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harriet_Lane_Levy "Harriet Lane Levy").
His sister was the novelist [Miriam Michelson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miriam_Michelson "Miriam Michelson").

### Education and career

President [Ulysses S. Grant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulysses_S._Grant "Ulysses S. Grant") awarded Michelson a special appointment to the [U.S. Naval Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Naval_Academy "U.S. Naval Academy") in 1869. During his four years as a [midshipman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midshipman "Midshipman") at the Academy, he excelled in [optics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optics "Optics"), heat, [climatology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climatology "Climatology") and [technical drawing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technical_drawing "Technical drawing"). After graduating in 1873 and two years at sea, he returned to the Naval Academy in 1875 to become an instructor in physics and chemistry until 1879, when he was posted to the Nautical Almanac Office, Washington (part of the [United States Naval Observatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Naval_Observatory "United States Naval Observatory")) to work with [Simon Newcomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simon_Newcomb "Simon Newcomb"). The following year, he took a leave of absence to continue his studies in Europe; he visited the universities of [Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Berlin "University of Berlin") and [Heidelberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Heidelberg "University of Heidelberg") in Germany, and the [Collège de France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collège_de_France "Collège de France") and [École polytechnique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/École_polytechnique "École polytechnique") in Paris, France.

Michelson was fascinated with the sciences, in particular the problem of measuring the [speed of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light "Speed of light"). While in [Annapolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annapolis "Annapolis"), he conducted his first experiments on the speed of light as part of a class demonstration in 1877. His Annapolis experiment was refined, and in 1879, he measured the speed of light in air to be 299864±51 kilometres per second, and estimated the speed of light in vacuum as 299940 km/s, or 186380 mi/s. After two years of studies in Europe, he resigned from the [Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Navy "United States Navy") in 1881. In 1883, he was appointed Professor of Physics at the [Case School of Applied Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Case_Western_Reserve_University "Case Western Reserve University") in [Cleveland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cleveland "Cleveland"), Ohio, and concentrated on developing an improved [interferometer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interferometer "Interferometer"). In 1887, he and [Edward Morley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Morley "Edward Morley") carried out the famous [Michelson–Morley experiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michelson–Morley_experiment "Michelson–Morley experiment"), which failed to detect evidence of the existence of the [luminiferous ether](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luminiferous_ether "Luminiferous ether"). He later moved on to use [astronomical interferometers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_interferometer "Astronomical interferometer") in the measurement of stellar diameters and in measuring the separations of binary stars.

In 1890, Michelson became a professor at [Clark University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clark_University "Clark University") in [Worcester, Massachusetts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worcester,_Massachusetts "Worcester, Massachusetts"). In 1892, he was appointed Professor and the first head of the Department of Physics at the newly-organized [University of Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Chicago "University of Chicago"), where he remained until his retirement in 1929.

Michelson returned to military service in the closing months of [World War I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I "World War I") as a [Lieutenant Commander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lieutenant_commander_(United_States) "Lieutenant commander (United States)") in the [Naval Reserve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Navy_Reserve "United States Navy Reserve"), serving in the [Bureau of Ordnance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bureau_of_Ordnance "Bureau of Ordnance"). He was promoted to [Commander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commander_(United_States) "Commander (United States)") in the reserve in May 1919 and was recalled briefly to active duty in the [9th Naval District](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/9th_Naval_District "9th Naval District") before being released from service on September 30, 1921.

Michelson died on May 9, 1931, in [Pasadena, California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pasadena,_California "Pasadena, California"), at the age of 78.

### Early measurements

Michelson was fascinated by light all his life. Once asked why he studied light, he reputedly said, "because it's so much fun".

As early as 1869, while serving as an officer in the [United States Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Navy "United States Navy"), Michelson started planning a repeat of the rotating-mirror method of [Léon Foucault](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Léon_Foucault "Léon Foucault") for measuring the speed of light, using improved optics and a longer baseline. He conducted some preliminary measurements using largely improvised equipment in 1878, about the same time that his work came to the attention of [Simon Newcomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simon_Newcomb "Simon Newcomb"), director of the Nautical Almanac Office who was already advanced in planning his own study.

Michelson's formal experiments took place in June and July 1879. He constructed a frame building along the north sea wall of the Naval Academy to house the machinery. Michelson published his result of 299,910 ± 50 km/s in 1879 before joining [Newcomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simon_Newcomb "Simon Newcomb") in Washington DC to assist with his measurements there. Thus began a long professional collaboration and friendship between the two.

[Simon Newcomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simon_Newcomb "Simon Newcomb"), with his more adequately funded project, obtained a value of 299,860 ± 30 km/s, just at the extreme edge of consistency with Michelson's. Michelson continued to "refine" his method and in 1883 published a measurement of 299,853 ± 60 km/s, rather closer to that of his mentor.

### Mount Wilson and Lookout Mountain

In 1906, a novel electrical method was used by [E. B. Rosa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Bennett_Rosa "Edward Bennett Rosa") and the [National Bureau of Standards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Bureau_of_Standards "National Bureau of Standards") to obtain a value for the [speed of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light "Speed of light") of 299,781 ± 10 km/s. Though this result has subsequently been shown to be severely biased by the poor electrical standards in use at the time, it seems to have set a fashion for rather lower measured values.

From 1920, Michelson started planning a definitive measurement from the [Mount Wilson Observatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Wilson_Observatory "Mount Wilson Observatory"), using a baseline to [Lookout Mountain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lookout_Mountain_(Los_Angeles_County,_California) "Lookout Mountain (Los Angeles County, California)"), a prominent bump on the south ridge of [Mount San Antonio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_San_Antonio "Mount San Antonio") ("Old Baldy"), some 22 miles distant.

In 1922, the [United States Coast and Geodetic Survey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Coast_and_Geodetic_Survey "United States Coast and Geodetic Survey") began two years of painstaking measurement of the baseline using the recently available [invar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invar "Invar") tapes. With the baseline length established in 1924, measurements were carried out over the next two years to obtain the published value of 299,796 ± 4 km/s.

Famous as the measurement is, it was beset by problems, not least of which was the haze created by the smoke from forest fires which blurred the mirror image. It is also probable that the intensively detailed work of the [geodetic survey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geodetic_survey "Geodetic survey"), with an estimated error of less than one part in 1 million, was compromised by a shift in the baseline arising from the [Santa Barbara earthquake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1925_Santa_Barbara_earthquake "1925 Santa Barbara earthquake") of June 29, 1925, which was an estimated magnitude of 6.3 on the [Richter scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richter_magnitude_scale "Richter magnitude scale").

The now-famous [Michelson–Morley experiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michelson–Morley_experiment "Michelson–Morley experiment") also influenced the affirmation attempts of peer [Albert Einstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Einstein "Albert Einstein")'s theory of [general relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_relativity "General relativity") and [special relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_relativity "Special relativity"), using similar optical instrumentation. These instruments and related collaborations included the participation of fellow physicists [Dayton Miller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dayton_Miller "Dayton Miller"), [Hendrik Lorentz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hendrik_Lorentz "Hendrik Lorentz"), and [Robert Shankland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_S._Shankland "Robert S. Shankland").

### Michelson, Pease, and Pearson

The period after 1927 marked the advent of new measurements of the speed of light using novel [electro-optic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electro-optics "Electro-optics") devices, all substantially lower than Michelson's 1926 value.

Michelson sought another measurement, but this time in an evacuated tube to avoid difficulties in interpreting the image owing to atmospheric effects. In 1929, he began a collaboration with [Francis G. Pease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_G._Pease "Francis G. Pease") and Fred Pearson to perform a measurement in a 1.6 km tube 3 feet in diameter at the Irvine Ranch near Santa Ana, California. In multiple reflections the light path was increased to 5 miles. For the first time in history the speed of light was measured in an almost perfect vacuum of 0.5 mm of mercury. Michelson died with only 36 of the 233 measurement series completed and the experiment was subsequently beset by geological instability and condensation problems before the result of 299774±11 km/s, consistent with the prevailing [electro-optic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electro-optics "Electro-optics") values, was published posthumously in 1935.

### Application of basic statistical principles in Michelson's study of speed of light

During June and early July 1879, Michelson refined experimental arrangements from those developed by [Hippolyte Fizeau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hippolyte_Fizeau "Hippolyte Fizeau") and [Léon Foucault](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Léon_Foucault "Léon Foucault"). The experimental setup was as follows: Light generated from a source is directed towards a rotating mirror through a slit on a fixed plate; the rotating mirror reflects the incoming light and at a certain angle, towards the direction where another fixed flat mirror is placed whose surface is perpendicular to the incoming ray of light; the rotating mirror should have rotated by an angle *α* by the time the ray of light travels back and is reflected again towards the fixed plate (the distance between the fixed mirror and the rotating one is recorded as *D*); a displacement from the slit is detected on the plate which measures *d*; the distance from the rotating mirror to the fixed plate is designated as the radius *r* while the number of revolutions per second of the mirror is recorded as *ω*. In this way, tan(2*α*) = *d*/*r*; Δ*t* = (*α*/2π)/*ω*; speed of light can be derived as *c* = 2*D*/Δ*t*.

While at plain sight, four measured quantities are involved: distance *D*, radius *r*, displacement *d* and rotating mirror revolution per second *ω*, which seems simple; yet based on the limitation of the measurement technology at that time, great efforts were made by Michelson to reduce [systematic errors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observational_error "Observational error") and apply subsequent corrections. For instance, he adopted a steel measuring tape with a said length of 100 feet and he intended to measure tens of times across the distance; still, he measured its length against a copy of the official standard yard to find out it as 100.006 feet, thus eliminating a systematic error, albeit small.

Aside from the efforts to reduce as much as possible the systematic errors, repeated measurements were performed at multiple levels to obtain more accurate results. As R.J. MacKay and R.W. Oldford remarked in their article, 'It is clear that Michelson appreciated the power of averaging to reduce variability in measurement', it is clear that Michelson had in mind the property that averages vary less which should be formally described as: the standard deviation of the average of *n* independent random variables is less than that of a single random variable by a factor of the square root of *n*. To realize that, he also strived to have each measurement not influencing each other, thus being mutually [independent random variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_random_variables "Independent random variables").

A [statistical model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_model "Statistical model") for repeated measurements with the assumption of independence or identical distributions is unrealistic. In the case of light speed study, each measurement is approached as the sum of quantity of interest and measurement error. In the absence of systematic error, the measurement error of speed of light can be modeled by a random sample from a distribution with unknown expectation and finite variance; thus, the speed of light is represented by the expectation of the model distribution and the ultimate goal is to estimate the expectation of the model distribution on the acquired dataset. The law of large numbers suggests to estimate the expectation by the sample mean.

### Michelson–Morley experiment

In 1887, he collaborated with colleague [Edward Williams Morley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Morley "Edward Morley") of Western Reserve University, now part of [Case Western Reserve University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Case_Western_Reserve_University "Case Western Reserve University"), in the [Michelson–Morley experiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michelson–Morley_experiment "Michelson–Morley experiment"). Their experiment for the expected motion of the [Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth "Earth") relative to the [aether](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luminiferous_aether "Luminiferous aether"), the hypothetical medium in which light was supposed to travel, resulted in a [null result](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Null_result "Null result"). Surprised, Michelson repeated the experiment with greater and greater precision over the next years, but continued to find no ability to measure the aether. The Michelson–Morley results were immensely influential in the physics community, leading [Hendrik Lorentz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hendrik_Lorentz "Hendrik Lorentz") to devise his now-famous [Lorentz contraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentz_contraction "Lorentz contraction") equations as a means of explaining the null result.

There has been some historical controversy over whether [Albert Einstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Einstein "Albert Einstein") was aware of the Michelson–Morley results when he developed his theory of [special relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_relativity "Special relativity"), which pronounced the aether to be "superfluous". In a later interview, Einstein said of the Michelson–Morley experiment, "I was not conscious it had influenced me directly ... I guess I just took it for granted that it was true." Regardless of Einstein's specific knowledge, the experiment is today considered the canonical experiment in regards to showing the lack of a detectable aether.

The precision of their equipment allowed Michelson and Morley to be the first to get precise values for the [fine structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fine_structure "Fine structure") in the atomic spectral lines for which in 1916 [Arnold Sommerfeld](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arnold_Sommerfeld "Arnold Sommerfeld") gave a theoretical explanation, introducing the [fine-structure constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fine-structure_constant "Fine-structure constant").

## Astronomical interferometry

### Optical

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/bb/Hooker_interferometer.jpg/330px-Hooker_interferometer.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Hooker_interferometer.jpg)

The horizontal structure mounted at the top of the [Hooker Telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hooker_Telescope "Hooker Telescope") implements Michelson's stellar interferometer (1920). Mirrors on that stage (not visible in picture) redirect starlight from two smaller apertures up to 20 feet (6m) apart into the telescope.

In 1920 Michelson and [Francis G. Pease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_G._Pease "Francis G. Pease") made the first measurement of the diameter of a star other than the Sun. Michelson had invented [astronomical interferometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_interferometry "Astronomical interferometry") and built such an instrument at the [Mount Wilson Observatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Wilson_Observatory "Mount Wilson Observatory") which was used to measure the diameter of the [red giant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_giant "Red giant") [Betelgeuse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Betelgeuse "Betelgeuse"). A periscope arrangement was used to direct light from two subpupils, separated by up to 20 feet (6m), into the main pupil of the 100 inch (2.5m) [Hooker Telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hooker_Telescope "Hooker Telescope"), producing interference fringes observed through the eyepiece. The measurement of stellar diameters and the separations of binary stars took up an increasing amount of Michelson's life after this.

Beginning in the 1970s, astronomical interferometry was revived, with the configurations using two (or more) separate apertures (with diameters small compared to their separation) being often referred to as "Michelson Stellar Interferometry". This was to distinguish it from [speckle interferometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speckle_interferometry "Speckle interferometry"), but should not be confused with the [Michelson interferometer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michelson_interferometer "Michelson interferometer") which is one common *laboratory* interferometer configuration of which the interferometer used in the Michelson–Morley experiment was an instance. Michelson's concept of interfering light from two relatively small apertures separated by a substantial distance (but with that distance, or *baseline*, now often as long as hundreds of meters) is employed at [modern operational observatories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_astronomical_interferometers_at_visible_and_infrared_wavelengths "List of astronomical interferometers at visible and infrared wavelengths") such as [VLTI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VLTI#Interferometry_and_the_VLTI "VLTI"), [CHARA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CHARA_array "CHARA array") and the U.S. Navy's [NPOI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navy_Prototype_Optical_Interferometer "Navy Prototype Optical Interferometer").

### Gravitational wave

[Gravitational waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_waves "Gravitational waves") are detected using a Michelson interferometer with a laser light source. In 2020 there were three Michelson interferometer gravitational wave detectors operational, and a fourth under construction. These Michelson interferometers have arms 4 kilometers in length, set at 90 degree angles to each other, with the light passing through 1 m diameter vacuum tubes running their entire length. A passing gravitational wave will slightly stretch one arm as it shortens the other. This is precisely the motion to which these Michelson interferometers are most sensitive. As of 2020 fifteen [gravitational wave events](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_gravitational_wave_observations#List_of_gravitational_wave_events "List of gravitational wave observations") had been observed using these Michelson interferometers.

## Harmonic analyzer

In the 1890s Michelson built a mechanical device called the harmonic analyzer, for computing coefficients of [Fourier series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourier_series "Fourier series") and drawing graphs of their partial sums. He and [S. W. Stratton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Wesley_Stratton "Samuel Wesley Stratton") published a paper about this machine in the *[American Journal of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Journal_of_Science "American Journal of Science")* in 1898.

## Family

In 1877, Michelson married Margaret Hemingway, daughter of a wealthy New York stockbroker and lawyer and the niece of his commander [William T. Sampson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_T._Sampson "William T. Sampson"). They had two sons and a daughter.

In 1899, Michelson married Edna Stanton. They raised three daughters.

He is a great uncle of physicist [Peter Michelson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Michelson "Peter Michelson").

A commemorative plaque in [Strzelno](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strzelno "Strzelno"), Poland, installed by the [Polish Physical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_Physical_Society "Polish Physical Society").

In Season 3 Episode 26 of the television series *[Bonanza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bonanza "Bonanza")* ("Look to the Stars", broadcast March 18, 1962), Ben Cartwright ([Lorne Greene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorne_Greene "Lorne Greene")) helps the 16-year-old Michelson (portrayed by 25-year-old Douglas Lambert (1936–1986)) obtain an appointment to the [U.S. Naval Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Naval_Academy "U.S. Naval Academy"), despite the opposition of the bigoted town schoolteacher (played by [William Schallert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Schallert "William Schallert")). *Bonanza* was set in and around [Virginia City, Nevada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virginia_City,_Nevada "Virginia City, Nevada"), where Michelson lived with his parents prior to leaving for the Naval Academy. In a voice-over at the end of the episode, Greene mentions Michelson's 1907 Nobel Prize.

The home in which Michelson lived as a child in [Murphys Camp, California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Murphys,_California "Murphys, California"), was in the store of his father, first on Main Street, across from the Sperry & Perry Hotel, and after the 1859 fire in a store next to the hotel. His aunt Bertha Meyers owned a house on Main Street toward the east end of town and Michelson probably visited her family there frequently.

New Beast Theater Works in collaboration with High Concept Laboratories produced a 'semi-opera' about Michelson, his obsessive working style and its effect on his family life. The production ran from February 11 to 26, 2011, in Chicago at The Building Stage. Michelson was portrayed by [Jon Stutzman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jon_Stutzman?action=edit&redlink=1 "Jon Stutzman"). The play was directed by [David Maral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Maral?action=edit&redlink=1 "David Maral") with music composed by [Joshua Dumas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joshua_Dumas?action=edit&redlink=1 "Joshua Dumas").

[Norman Fitzroy Maclean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_Fitzroy_Maclean "Norman Fitzroy Maclean") wrote an essay "Billiards Is a Good Game"; published in *The Norman Maclean Reader* (ed. O. Alan Weltzien, 2008), it is an appreciation of Michelson from Maclean's vantage point as a graduate student regularly watching him play billiards.

## Commemoration

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b3/Michaelson_experiment_annapolis.JPG/250px-Michaelson_experiment_annapolis.JPG?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Michaelson_experiment_annapolis.JPG)

A monument at [United States Naval Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Naval_Academy "United States Naval Academy") marks the path of Michelson's experiments measuring the speed of light.

The University of Chicago Residence Halls remembered Michelson and his achievements by dedicating 'Michelson House' in his honor. Case Western Reserve has dedicated a Michelson House to him, and Michelson Hall (an academic building of science classrooms, laboratories and offices) at the [United States Naval Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Naval_Academy "United States Naval Academy") also bears his name. Michelson Laboratory at [Naval Air Weapons Station China Lake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naval_Air_Weapons_Station_China_Lake "Naval Air Weapons Station China Lake") in Ridgecrest, California, is named for him. There is a display in the publicly accessible area of the Lab which includes facsimiles of Michelson's Nobel Prize medal, the prize document, and examples of his diffraction gratings. In 2017, a newly renovated physics research center at the University of Chicago was renamed in honor of Michelson as well.

Numerous awards, lectures, and honors have been created in Albert A. Michelson's name. Some of the current awards and lectures named for Michelson include the following: the Bomem-Michelson Award and Lecture annually presented until 2017 by the Coblentz Society; the [Michelson–Morley Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michelson–Morley_Award "Michelson–Morley Award") and Lecture, along with the Michelson Lecture Series, and the Michelson Postdoctoral Prize Lectureship, all of which are given annually by [Case Western Reserve University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Case_Western_Reserve_University "Case Western Reserve University"); the A.A. Michelson Award presented every year by the [Computer Measurement Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_Measurement_Group "Computer Measurement Group"); the Albert A. Michelson Award given by the [Navy League of the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navy_League_of_the_United_States "Navy League of the United States"); and the Michelson Memorial Lecture Series presented annually by the Division of Mathematics and Science at the [U.S. Naval Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Naval_Academy "United States Naval Academy"). The [Computer Measurement Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_Measurement_Group "Computer Measurement Group") gives the annual [A. A. Michelson Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_Measurement_Group#Awards "Computer Measurement Group").
