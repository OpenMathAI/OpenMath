**Sir Edward Victor Appleton** (6 September 1892 – 21 April 1965) was a British [physicist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physicist "Physicist") who received the [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics") in 1947 for his contributions to the knowledge of the [ionosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionosphere "Ionosphere"), which led to the development of [radar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radar "Radar") and [shortwave radio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shortwave_radio "Shortwave radio").

A graduate of the [University of Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Cambridge "University of Cambridge"), Appleton was commissioned as a [second lieutenant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_lieutenant "Second lieutenant") for the [Royal Engineers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Engineers "Royal Engineers") in 1915. During the [First World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_World_War "First World War"), he was an instructor at the Royal Engineers Signals Depot in [Fenny Stratford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fenny_Stratford "Fenny Stratford"), and served briefly on the [Western Front](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Front_(World_War_I) "Western Front (World War I)"). He brought a captured German [thermionic valve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermionic_valve "Thermionic valve") back with him. He became the Wheatstone Professor of Physics at [King's College London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King's_College_London "King's College London") in 1924 and a [fellow of the Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow_of_the_Royal_Society "Fellow of the Royal Society") in 1927. His primary research interests were how radio waves were created and propagated and unravelling the mystery of how they were reflected by the upper atmosphere.

Appleton returned to Cambridge in 1936 as [Jacksonian Professor of Natural Philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacksonian_Professor_of_Natural_Philosophy "Jacksonian Professor of Natural Philosophy") and, when [Ernest Rutherford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_Rutherford "Ernest Rutherford") died in October of that year, became acting director of the [Cavendish Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cavendish_Laboratory "Cavendish Laboratory"). In 1939, shortly before the outbreak of the [Second World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_World_War "Second World War"), Appleton became the Secretary of the [Department of Scientific and Industrial Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Department_of_Scientific_and_Industrial_Research_(United_Kingdom) "Department of Scientific and Industrial Research (United Kingdom)"). In this role, he marshalled the scientific knowledge of Britain in service of the war effort. He was also closely involved with the development of [radar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radar "Radar"), an outgrowth of his pre-war research into the ionosphere. He was also in charge of [Tube Alloys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tube_Alloys "Tube Alloys"), the British [nuclear weapons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_weapon "Nuclear weapon") project, which was merged into the American [Manhattan Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manhattan_Project "Manhattan Project") in 1943.

From 1948 until his death in 1965, he was the [Principal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_principals_of_the_University_of_Edinburgh "List of principals of the University of Edinburgh") and Vice-Chancellor of the [University of Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Edinburgh "University of Edinburgh"). As such, he oversaw its growth and was involved in the controversial redevelopment of the historic [George Square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Square,_Edinburgh "George Square, Edinburgh").

## Early life and education

Edward Victor Appleton was born on 6 September 1892 in [Bradford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bradford "Bradford"), England, the eldest child of Peter Appleton, a clerk who worked in a warehouse of Charles Senior & Co., and Mary Wilcock. He had two younger sisters, Isabel and Dorothy. He was named Edward after the [tenor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tenor "Tenor") [Edward Lloyd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Lloyd_(tenor) "Edward Lloyd (tenor)") but preferred to use his middle name; to his family he was always "Vic". He attended [Barkerend Primary School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barkerend#Education "Barkerend") until, at the age of 11, he was awarded a scholarship to [Hanson Boys' Grammar School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanson_Academy "Hanson Academy"), where he was captain of the school [cricket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cricket "Cricket") and [football](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Association_football "Association football") teams. When he was 16, he won a [City of Bradford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_of_Bradford "City of Bradford") scholarship to attend [Bradford Grammar School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bradford_Grammar_School "Bradford Grammar School") but did not take it up.

Appleton sat and passed the [London Matriculation Examination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matriculation#United_Kingdom "Matriculation") but then sat for and won the [Isaac Holden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Holden "Isaac Holden") scholarship in 1910. This was a three-year scholarship valued at £150 (equivalent to £15,276 in 2025) that enabled him to enter the [University of Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Cambridge "University of Cambridge"), although he had to wait another year because the university would not admit him until he turned 19. In 1911, he was awarded an [exhibition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exhibition_(scholarship) "Exhibition (scholarship)") at [St John's College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_John's_College,_Cambridge "St John's College, Cambridge"), where he obtained a [B.A.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B.A. "B.A.") in Natural Science in 1913, and an [M.A.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M.A. "M.A.") in Physics in 1914. The same year, he received the Hutchinson Research Studentship, which enabled him to study under [J. J. Thomson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._J._Thomson "J. J. Thomson") and [Ernest Rutherford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_Rutherford "Ernest Rutherford"). At Cambridge, he met Sir [Oliver Lodge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oliver_Lodge "Oliver Lodge") and [Guglielmo Marconi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guglielmo_Marconi "Guglielmo Marconi"), and during the long vacation, he assisted [Lawrence Bragg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawrence_Bragg "Lawrence Bragg") in his work on [X-ray crystallography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray_crystallography "X-ray crystallography") of metals in the [Cavendish Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cavendish_Laboratory "Cavendish Laboratory").

## Great War

While at Cambridge, Appleton had served in the [University Officers' Training Corps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_Officers'_Training_Corps "University Officers' Training Corps"). On the outbreak of the [First World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_World_War "First World War") in August 1914, Appleton applied for a commission in the [Royal Engineers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Engineers "Royal Engineers"). By September, he had tired of waiting for this and enlisted as a [private](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Private_(rank) "Private (rank)") in the 16th Battalion, [West Yorkshire Regiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Yorkshire_Regiment "West Yorkshire Regiment"), also known as the "1st Bradford Pals Battalion". He was soon promoted to [corporal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corporal "Corporal"). His application for a commission eventually came through, and he was commissioned as a [second lieutenant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_lieutenant "Second lieutenant") in the Royal Engineers on 9 January 1915. After engineer training at [Aldershot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aldershot "Aldershot"), he was sent to [Malvern, Worcestershire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malvern,_Worcestershire "Malvern, Worcestershire"), for the first general radio course for officers, which was taught by [Captain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Captain_(British_Army_and_Royal_Marines) "Captain (British Army and Royal Marines)") C. F. Trippe, who later played an important role in the industrial development of the [thermionic valve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermionic_valve "Thermionic valve"). Appleton topped the class on the course examination and was asked to conduct the second course when Trippe fell ill. He was then posted to the Royal Engineers Signals Depot in [Fenny Stratford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fenny_Stratford "Fenny Stratford"), where he was stationed for the rest of the war.

On 29 May 1915, Appleton married his distant cousin Jessie Longson, the daughter of Reverend John Longson, a Baptist minister. Jessie worked for [Lister Mills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lister_Mills "Lister Mills") as a textile designer, creating patterns in silk. After the war they had two daughters: Margery, born in 1920, and Rosalind, born in 1927. Appleton was promoted to the temporary rank of [lieutenant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lieutenant_(British_Army_and_Royal_Marines) "Lieutenant (British Army and Royal Marines)") on 23 August 1916, and captain on 1 January 1918. Married [subalterns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subaltern_(military) "Subaltern (military)") were uncommon in those days, and Appleton and Jessie shared a cottage in Fenny Stratford with another lieutenant and his wife.

In 1915, the British Army in France found that its ration parties were being engaged by German artillery, who apparently had [intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_intelligence "Military intelligence") of their activities. The mystery began to unravel after the French captured a German device that was handed over to the British. Appleton and Captains [Algernon Fuller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algernon_Fuller "Algernon Fuller") and Edward Stevens examined the device and found it consisted of a two-valve amplifier with two pairs of phones and several pairs of terminals. When the terminals were [earthed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ground_(electricity) "Ground (electricity)") the device could listen in on Army [field telephone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_telephone "Field telephone") traffic. The obvious countermeasure was to use [twisted pair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twisted_pair "Twisted pair") cabling but this was expensive. After discussion among the three men, Fuller came up with the [fullerphone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fullerphone "Fullerphone"), a device that worked with the existing telephone lines but was far more difficult to tap. In December 1917, Appleton went to the [Western Front](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Front_(World_War_I) "Western Front (World War I)") for a fortnight to demonstrate the use of radios, and he tested the fullerphone in the field, and found that it worked with as little as a micro-ampere of current. He brought a captured German thermionic valve back with him. He was fascinated with the properties of the valve, which he would research on returning to Cambridge. After the war ended, Appleton relinquished his commission on 17 January 1919.

## Between the wars

Appleton returned to Cambridge, where he was elected a [fellow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow#In_ancient_universities "Fellow") of St John's College. At first, he was a research student at the Cavendish Laboratory under Thomson, who was succeeded that year by Rutherford. In 1920, Appleton joined the staff of the Cavendish Laboratory as an assistant demonstrator in physics. He was initiated into [Freemasonry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freemasonry "Freemasonry") in 1922. Under Rutherford, the Cavendish Laboratory was drawn deeper into [nuclear physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_physics "Nuclear physics") research, but Appleton's interest was exploring radio: the way that radio waves were created by thermionic valves, how they propagated, and the mysterious fluctuations in their reception.

In his investigation of the properties of the thermionic valve, Appleton collaborated with [Balthasar van der Pol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balthasar_van_der_Pol "Balthasar van der Pol"), a Dutch scientist who had been a fellow research student at the Cavendish Laboratory before the war. Van der Pol considered returning to Cambridge, but Appleton warned him that lectureships paid no more than £250 (equivalent to £25,000 in 2025) per year. Appleton would be reduced to £650 (equivalent to £66,000 in 2025) when his fellowship expired. Van der Pol therefore decided to join [Philips](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philips "Philips") instead. Together, they wrote a textbook on the subject, *Thermionic Vacuum Tubes*, which was published in 1932.

### Ionosphere

In his atmospheric research, Appleton worked with the Radio Research Board, of which he became a member in 1926 and served on until 1939. The Radio Research Board provided him with grants to continue his work and its [Radio Research Station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_Research_Station_(UK) "Radio Research Station (UK)") at [Ditton Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ditton_Park "Ditton Park"), near [Slough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slough "Slough"), collaborated with the Cavendish Laboratory in its atmospheric research. He also established a field station near [Peterborough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peterborough "Peterborough") where his Cavendish Laboratory research student [Miles Barnett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miles_Barnett "Miles Barnett") conducted experiments under his direction. This station moved to [Hampstead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hampstead "Hampstead") in 1932. In October 1924, Appleton was appointed Wheatstone Professor of Physics at [King's College London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King's_College_London "King's College London"). He became a [fellow of the Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow_of_the_Royal_Society "Fellow of the Royal Society") in 1927.

Appleton had observed that the strength of [medium wave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medium_wave "Medium wave") radio signals was constant during the day but variable at night. He theorised that this was due to a signal bouncing off the [upper atmosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_atmosphere "Upper atmosphere"). This would result in two signals being received, which would interfere with each other. The resulting [wave interference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_interference "Wave interference") pattern would account for the variable strength of the signal. The existence of a reflecting atmospheric layer was not in itself a completely new idea; [Balfour Stewart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balfour_Stewart "Balfour Stewart") had suggested the idea in the late 19th century to explain rhythmic changes in the [Earth's magnetic field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_magnetic_field "Earth's magnetic field"). More recently, in 1902, [Oliver Heaviside](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oliver_Heaviside "Oliver Heaviside") and [Arthur E. Kennelly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_E._Kennelly "Arthur E. Kennelly") had suggested such an electromagnetic-reflecting stratum—now called the [Kennelly–Heaviside layer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kennelly–Heaviside_layer "Kennelly–Heaviside layer")—that may explain the success Marconi had in transmitting his signals across the Atlantic. Calculations had shown that natural bending of the [radio waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_wave "Radio wave") was not sufficient to stop them from simply "shooting off" into space before they reached the receiver.
Appleton thought the best place to look for evidence of the [ionosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionosphere "Ionosphere") was in the variation in the range of radio signal reception that occurred each night around sunset. It was sensible to suggest these variations were due to the interference of two waves but an extra step to show that the second wave causing the interference (the first being the [ground wave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ground_wave "Ground wave")) was coming down from the ionosphere. The experiment he designed had two methods to show ionospheric influence and both allowed the height of the lower boundary of reflection (thus the lower boundary of the reflecting layer) to be determined. As we know λ and λ', we can calculate *D*. Using the approximation that ABC is an isosceles triangle, we can use our value of *D* to calculate the height of the reflecting layer. This method is a slightly simplified version of the method used by Appleton and his student, [Miles Barnett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miles_Barnett "Miles Barnett"), to work out a first value for the height of the ionosphere in 1924. In their experiment, they used the [BBC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC "BBC") broadcasting station in [Bournemouth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bournemouth "Bournemouth") to vary the wavelengths of its emissions after the evening programmes had finished. They installed a receiving station in [Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford "Oxford") to monitor the interference effects. The receiving station had to be in Oxford as there was no suitable emitter at the right distance of about 62 miles (100 km) from [Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge "Cambridge") in those days.

Far from being conclusive, the success of the Oxford–Bournemouth experiment revealed a vast new field of study to be explored. It showed that there was indeed a reflecting layer high above the Earth but it also posed many new questions: What was the constitution of this layer? How did it reflect the waves? Was it the same all over the Earth? Why did its effects change so dramatically between day and night? Did it change throughout the year? Appleton would spend the rest of his life answering these questions. He developed a magneto-ionic theory based on the previous work of [Lorentz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hendrik_Lorentz "Hendrik Lorentz") and [Maxwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Clerk_Maxwell "James Clerk Maxwell") to model the workings of this part of the atmosphere. Using this theory and further experiments, he showed that the so-called Kennelly–Heaviside layer was heavily ionised and thus conducting. This led to the term ionosphere. He showed free electrons to be the ionising agents. He discovered that the layer could be penetrated by waves above a certain frequency and that this critical frequency could be used to calculate the [electron density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_density "Electron density") in the layer. However, these penetrating waves would also be reflected back, but from a much higher layer. This showed the ionosphere had a much more complex structure than first anticipated. The lower level was labelled "E layer," which reflected longer wavelengths and was found to be at approximately 100 kilometres (62 mi). The high level—which had much higher electron density—was labelled "F layer," and could reflect much shorter wavelengths that penetrated the lower layer. It is situated 300 to 400 kilometres (190 to 250 mi) above the Earth's surface. It is this, which is often referred to as the [Appleton layer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appleton_layer "Appleton layer"), that is responsible for enabling most long range [shortwave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shortwave "Shortwave") telecommunication.

The magneto-ionic theory also allowed Appleton to explain the origin of the mysterious fadings heard on the radio around sunset. During the day, the light from the Sun causes the molecules in the air to become ionised even at fairly low altitudes. At these low altitudes, the density of the air is great and thus the electron density of ionised air is very large. Due to this heavy [ionisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionisation "Ionisation"), there is strong absorption of [electromagnetic waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_wave "Electromagnetic wave") caused by "electron friction". Thus, in transmissions over any distance, there will be no reflections as any waves apart from the one at ground level will be absorbed rather than reflected. However, when the sun sets, the molecules slowly start to recombine with their electrons and the free electron density levels drop. This means absorption rates diminish and waves can be reflected with sufficient strengths to be noticed, leading to the interference phenomena we have mentioned. For these interference patterns to occur though, there must not simply be the presence of a reflected wave but a change in the reflected wave. Otherwise, the interference is constant and fadings would not be heard. The received signal would simply be louder or softer than during the day. This suggests the height at which reflection happens must slowly change as the sun sets. Appleton found in fact that it increased as the sun set and then decreased as the sun rose until the reflected wave was too weak to record. This variation is compatible with the theory that ionisation is due to the Sun's influence. At sunset, the intensity of the Sun's radiation will be much less at the surface of the Earth than it is high up in the atmosphere. This means ionic recombination will progress slowly from lower altitudes to higher ones and therefore the height at which waves are reflected slowly increases as the sun sets.

Appleton devoted almost all of his scientific career to the study of the ionosphere. Like many other fields, it grew in intricacy the more it was studied. By the end of his life, ionospheric observatories had been set up all over the world to provide a global map of the reflecting layers. Links were found to the 11-year sunspot cycle and the [aurora borealis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aurora_borealis "Aurora borealis"), the magnetic storms that occur in high latitudes. This became particularly relevant during the Second World War when the storms would lead to [radio blackouts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_blackout "Radio blackout"). Thanks to Appleton's research, the periods when these would occur could be predicted and communication could be switched to wavelengths that would be least affected. [Radar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radar "Radar"), another crucial wartime innovation, was one that came about thanks to Appleton's work. On a very general level, his research consisted in determining the distance of reflecting objects from radio signal transmitters. This is exactly the idea of radar and the flashing dots that appear on the screen (a cathode ray tube) scanned by the circulating 'searcher' bar. This system was developed partly by Appleton as a new method, called the pulse method, to make ionospheric measurements. It was later adapted by [Robert Watson-Watt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Watson-Watt "Robert Watson-Watt") to detect aeroplanes.

Appleton returned to Cambridge in 1936 as [Jacksonian Professor of Natural Philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacksonian_Professor_of_Natural_Philosophy "Jacksonian Professor of Natural Philosophy") and was re-elected as a fellow of St John's College. He brought several of his research students with him, including [William Roy Piggott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Roy_Piggott "William Roy Piggott"), and persuaded the university to build him a new field laboratory. When Rutherford died in October of that year, Appleton became acting director of the Cavendish Laboratory. Although he hoped to succeed Rutherford, in the summer of 1938 it was announced that [Lawrence Bragg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawrence_Bragg "Lawrence Bragg") would become the next director instead.

## Second World War

In October 1938, the Secretary of the [Department of Scientific and Industrial Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Department_of_Scientific_and_Industrial_Research_(United_Kingdom) "Department of Scientific and Industrial Research (United Kingdom)") (DSIR), Sir [Frank Edward Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Edward_Smith "Frank Edward Smith"), retired, and the British government appointed Appleton as his successor. Another major war was imminent, and preparations were already underway in the department. In February 1939, he moved with his family to [Putney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Putney "Putney") in London. Soon after the outbreak of the [Second World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_World_War "Second World War") in September, he had Piggott brought from Cambridge as his assistant. The many laboratories assisted the war effort in diverse ways: [National Physical Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Physical_Laboratory_(United_Kingdom) "National Physical Laboratory (United Kingdom)")'s ship tank was used to develop [landing craft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landing_craft "Landing craft"), the [Mulberry harbours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mulberry_harbour "Mulberry harbour") and the [bouncing bomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bouncing_bomb "Bouncing bomb"); the [Forest Products Research Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forest_Products_Research_Laboratory "Forest Products Research Laboratory") developed plywood and adhesives for use in aircraft; the Fuel Research Station developed fuel for [flame throwers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flame_throwers "Flame throwers"); and the [Water Pollution Research Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WRc "WRc") created a device for airmen to make sea water into drinking water. He was also closely involved with the development of [radar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radar "Radar"), and his pre-war research into the ionosphere also found a role in [high-frequency direction finding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-frequency_direction_finding "High-frequency direction finding").

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/58/Bushy_House_-_geograph.org.uk_-_6709252.jpg/250px-Bushy_House_-_geograph.org.uk_-_6709252.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Bushy_House_-_geograph.org.uk_-_6709252.jpg)

[Bushy House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bushy_House "Bushy House") in [Teddington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teddington "Teddington")

Appleton's home in Putney was damaged in [the Blitz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Blitz "The Blitz") in September 1940, and the family, now reduced to three after Rosalind married William Lamont, a Church of England minister who joined the Royal Navy in 1941, moved to the [Clarence Hotel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Park_Hotel,_Teddington "Park Hotel, Teddington") in [Teddington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teddington "Teddington"), near where DSIR headquarters had relocated to the Chemical Research Laboratory in Teddington and the National Physical Laboratory in [Bushy Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bushy_Park "Bushy Park"). The latter was targeted by German bombers, and the family spent nights in the air raid shelter under the hotel. In 1942, the National Physical Laboratory's director, Sir [Charles Galton Darwin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Galton_Darwin "Charles Galton Darwin") was seconded to the British Mission in [Washington, DC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington,_DC "Washington, DC"). Appleton became acting director in addition to his role as Secretary of the DSIR and the family moved into [Bushy House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bushy_House "Bushy House"). He was created a [Knight Commander of the Order of the Bath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knight_Commander_of_the_Order_of_the_Bath "Knight Commander of the Order of the Bath"), an honour customarily conferred on the Secretary of the DSIR, in the [1941 New Year Honours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1941_New_Year_Honours "1941 New Year Honours").

In September 1941, Appleton picked up another responsibility when the directorate of [Tube Alloys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tube_Alloys "Tube Alloys") was established as part of DSIR. The name was deliberately chosen to be meaningless. The directorate was responsible for the development of [nuclear weapons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_weapon "Nuclear weapon"). Sir [John Anderson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Anderson,_1st_Viscount_Waverley "John Anderson, 1st Viscount Waverley"), who, as [Lord President of the Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_President_of_the_Council "Lord President of the Council"), was the minister responsible for DSIR, appointed [Wallace Akers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wallace_Akers "Wallace Akers"), the research director of [Imperial Chemical Industries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_Chemical_Industries "Imperial Chemical Industries") (ICI), as the director of Tube Alloys. An advisory committee known as the Tube Alloys Consultative Council was created to oversee its work and handle policy matters, chaired by Anderson, with its other members being Appleton, [Lord Hankey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maurice_Hankey,_1st_Baron_Hankey "Maurice Hankey, 1st Baron Hankey"), [Lord Cherwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_Lindemann,_1st_Viscount_Cherwell "Frederick Lindemann, 1st Viscount Cherwell") and Sir [Henry Dale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Hallett_Dale "Henry Hallett Dale"). One of Appleton's first actions was to have [Charles Findlay Davidson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Findlay_Davidson "Charles Findlay Davidson") conduct a search for sources of [uranium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium "Uranium").

The [Quebec Agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quebec_Agreement "Quebec Agreement") in August 1943 merged Tube Alloys with the American [Manhattan Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manhattan_Project "Manhattan Project"). Anderson had intended that Akers would be the British representative, but the Americans considered him a *persona non grata* due to his commercial background. Anderson sent Appleton to Washington to negotiate a deal. When he arrived, he found that Colonel [John Llewellin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Llewellin,_1st_Baron_Llewellin "John Llewellin, 1st Baron Llewellin"), the British Minister of State for Supply in the United States, had appointed [James Chadwick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Chadwick "James Chadwick") as interim head of the [British mission to the Manhattan Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_contribution_to_the_Manhattan_Project "British contribution to the Manhattan Project"). Appleton supported this appointment and convinced Anderson to make the appointment permanent. This left only a residual Tube Alloys directorate in the UK under Akers, but in April 1945, Appleton recommended that [John Cockcroft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Cockcroft "John Cockcroft") be appointed to set up an [Atomic Energy Research Establishment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_Energy_Research_Establishment "Atomic Energy Research Establishment") in the UK, thereby laying the groundwork for the British [post-war nuclear weapons project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_Explosive_Research "High Explosive Research").

This was but one aspect of post-war reconstruction planning that Appleton reoriented DSIR towards in the last years of the war. He foresaw the need for science and industry to collaborate in rebuilding a nation where services and infrastructure had been damaged and dislocated by the war. To inform the public about the part DSIR had played in the war, he commissioned a book, *Science at War* (1947), by [James Crowther](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Crowther "James Crowther") and Richard Whiddington. He was created a [Knight Grand Cross of the Order of the British Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knight_Grand_Cross_of_the_Order_of_the_British_Empire "Knight Grand Cross of the Order of the British Empire") in the [1946 New Year Honours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1946_New_Year_Honours "1946 New Year Honours"). He also received foreign awards, including the [Medal for Merit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medal_for_Merit "Medal for Merit") from the United States, the [Legion of Honour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legion_of_Honour "Legion of Honour") from France and the [King Haakon VII Freedom Cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Haakon_VII_Freedom_Cross "King Haakon VII Freedom Cross") from Norway. He was made a Commander of the Norwegian [Order of Saint Olav](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_Saint_Olav "Order of Saint Olav") and a Knight Commander of the Icelandic [Order of the Falcon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_the_Falcon "Order of the Falcon"). The highest honour though was the award of the [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics") in 1947 "for his investigations of the physics of the upper atmosphere especially for the discovery of the so-called Appleton layer".

## Later life

### University of Edinburgh

In October 1948, the [Lord Provost of Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_Provost_of_Edinburgh "Lord Provost of Edinburgh"), Sir [Andrew Murray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Murray_(Scottish_politician) "Andrew Murray (Scottish politician)"), invited Appleton to become the [Principal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_principals_of_the_University_of_Edinburgh "List of principals of the University of Edinburgh") and Vice-Chancellor of the [University of Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Edinburgh "University of Edinburgh"), a position that had been vacant since the death of Sir [John Fraser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_John_Fraser,_1st_Baronet "Sir John Fraser, 1st Baronet") in December 1947. Appleton and Lady Appleton were reluctant owing to the poor state of the proposed Principal's residence, Abden House, but the Lord Provost agreed to renovate it, and Appleton formally assumed the position in May 1949. In 1952, Helen Allison, a former [Women's Royal Naval Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women's_Royal_Naval_Service "Women's Royal Naval Service") officer, became his private secretary.

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/3e/Abden_House%2C_Edinburgh_%2850934046857%29.jpg/250px-Abden_House%2C_Edinburgh_%2850934046857%29.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Abden_House,_Edinburgh_(50934046857).jpg)

Abden House, Edinburgh

Appleton's major task was managing the expansion of the university, which grew from 185 full-time staff and 3,716 students in 1939 to 612 full-time staff and 7,004 students twenty years later. Overcrowding was evident even in 1939, and a master plan called for the redevelopment of [George Square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Square,_Edinburgh "George Square, Edinburgh"), a historic district that had long since fallen on hard times, into a university precinct. The first step, an extension of the existing medical buildings, was approved by the Edinburgh Town Council in February 1949, shortly before Appleton arrived. This involved the demolition of much of the area's historic houses and erecting modern buildings such as [40 George Square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/40_George_Square "40 George Square"), the [Edinburgh University Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edinburgh_University_Library "Edinburgh University Library") and what became [Appleton Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appleton_Tower "Appleton Tower"). This was highly controversial and bitterly contested. Instead of sending form letters to those who wrote to him to process, Appleton wrote personal letters addressing the issues the sender raised. He lived to see the project carried through.

Appleton retained his interest in the physics of the ionosphere. He started the *[Journal of Atmospheric and Terrestrial Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Journal_of_Atmospheric_and_Solar-Terrestrial_Physics "Journal of Atmospheric and Solar-Terrestrial Physics")* in 1950 and remained its editor-in-chief until his death in 1965. He was president of the International Union of Scientific Radio (URSI) from 1934 to 1952, and attended its general assemblies in Zurich in 1950, Sydney in 1952, The Hague in 1954 and [Boulder, Colorado](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boulder,_Colorado "Boulder, Colorado"), in 1957. On the trip to Australia in 1952, he addressed audiences in Sydney, Canberra and Adelaide. In 1954, he noticed that values for the F2-Layer differed between Delhi and Baton Rouge. It was already known that the northern hemisphere differed from the southern, but these cities are on similar latitudes. He realised that what was important was latitude in relation to [Magnetic equator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_equator "Magnetic equator") and the geographic one. In 1956, the [BBC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC "BBC") invited Appleton to deliver the annual [Reith Lectures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reith_Lectures "Reith Lectures"). Across a series of six radio broadcasts, titled *Science and the Nation*, he explored the many facets of scientific activity in Britain at the time.

### Death

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/2f/Grave_of_Sir_Edward_Appleton_-_geograph.org.uk_-_1234267.jpg/250px-Grave_of_Sir_Edward_Appleton_-_geograph.org.uk_-_1234267.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Grave_of_Sir_Edward_Appleton_-_geograph.org.uk_-_1234267.jpg)

Appleton's grave in [Morningside Cemetery, Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morningside_Cemetery,_Edinburgh "Morningside Cemetery, Edinburgh")

Lady (Jessie) Appleton had a severe [stroke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stroke "Stroke") in 1961, and she died in 1964. On 24 March 1965, Appleton married Helen Allison. He died from a sudden [heart attack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heart_attack "Heart attack") on 21 April that year at Abden House. He is buried in Edinburgh's [Morningside Cemetery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morningside_Cemetery,_Edinburgh "Morningside Cemetery, Edinburgh") with Helen. His papers are held by the University of Edinburgh; the University of Cambridge holds his correspondence with John Ratcliffe.

## Commemoration

In 1973, the [Radio Research Station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_Research_Station "Radio Research Station") was renamed the Appleton Laboratory. In 1979, it merged with the Rutherford Laboratory to become the [Rutherford Appleton Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rutherford_Appleton_Laboratory "Rutherford Appleton Laboratory"). In 2008, the [Institute of Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_of_Physics "Institute of Physics")' Chree Medal and Prize (of which Appleton was a recipient) was renamed the [Edward Appleton Medal and Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Appleton_Medal_and_Prize "Edward Appleton Medal and Prize"). [Appleton Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appleton_Tower "Appleton Tower") at the [University of Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Edinburgh "University of Edinburgh"), [Appleton crater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appleton_(crater) "Appleton (crater)") on the Moon, and [Appleton Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appleton_Academy "Appleton Academy") in Bradford are also named after him.
