**Joseph Hooton Taylor Jr.** (born March 29, 1941) is an American [astrophysicist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrophysicist "Astrophysicist"). He shared the 1993 [Nobel Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize "Nobel Prize") in physics with [Russell Alan Hulse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russell_Alan_Hulse "Russell Alan Hulse") "for the discovery of a [new type](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PSR_B1913+16 "PSR B1913+16") of [pulsar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulsar "Pulsar"), a discovery that has opened up new possibilities for the study of [gravitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitation "Gravitation")". This was the first indirect detection of [gravitational waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_wave "Gravitational wave"), later directly detected by [Barry Barish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barry_Barish "Barry Barish"), [Kip Thorne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kip_Thorne "Kip Thorne") and [Rainer Weiss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rainer_Weiss "Rainer Weiss").

## Early life and education

Taylor was born in [Philadelphia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philadelphia "Philadelphia") to Joseph Hooton Taylor Sr. and Sylvia Evans Taylor, both of whom had [Quaker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quakers "Quakers") roots for many generations, and grew up in [Cinnaminson Township, New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cinnaminson_Township,_New_Jersey "Cinnaminson Township, New Jersey"). He attended the [Moorestown Friends School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moorestown_Friends_School "Moorestown Friends School") in [Moorestown Township, New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moorestown_Township,_New_Jersey "Moorestown Township, New Jersey"), where he excelled in math.

He received a [B.A.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_Arts "Bachelor of Arts") in [physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics "Physics") at [Haverford College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haverford_College "Haverford College") in 1963, and a [Ph.D.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Philosophy "Doctor of Philosophy") in [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy "Astronomy") at [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University "Harvard University") in 1968. After a brief research position at Harvard, Taylor went to the [University of Massachusetts Amherst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Massachusetts_Amherst "University of Massachusetts Amherst"), eventually becoming [Professor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Professor "Professor") of Astronomy and Associate Director of the [Five College Radio Astronomy Observatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Five_College_Radio_Astronomy_Observatory "Five College Radio Astronomy Observatory").

Taylor's thesis work was on [lunar occultation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occultation "Occultation") measurements. About the time he completed his Ph.D., [Jocelyn Bell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jocelyn_Bell "Jocelyn Bell") (who is also a Quaker) discovered the first radio pulsars with a telescope near [Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge "Cambridge"), [England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/England "England").

## Career

Taylor immediately went to the [National Radio Astronomy Observatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Radio_Astronomy_Observatory "National Radio Astronomy Observatory")'s telescopes in [Green Bank, West Virginia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_Bank,_West_Virginia "Green Bank, West Virginia"), and participated in the discovery of the first pulsars discovered outside Cambridge. Since then, he has worked on all aspects of pulsar astrophysics.

In 1974, Hulse and Taylor discovered the first [pulsar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulsar "Pulsar") in a [binary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_star "Binary star") system, named [PSR B1913+16](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PSR_B1913+16 "PSR B1913+16") after its position in the sky, during a survey for pulsars at the [Arecibo Observatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arecibo_Observatory "Arecibo Observatory") in [Puerto Rico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puerto_Rico "Puerto Rico"). Although it was not understood at the time, this was also the first of what are now called *recycled* pulsars: [Neutron stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron_star "Neutron star") that have been spun-up to fast spin rates by the transfer of mass onto their surfaces from a companion star.

The orbit of this binary system is slowly shrinking as it loses energy because of emission of [gravitational radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_radiation "Gravitational radiation"), causing its orbital period to speed up slightly. The rate of shrinkage can be precisely predicted from Einstein's [General Theory of Relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Theory_of_Relativity "General Theory of Relativity"), and over a thirty-year period Taylor and his colleagues have made measurements that match this prediction to much better than one percent accuracy. This was the first confirmation of the existence of gravitational radiation. There are now scores of binary pulsars known, and independent measurements have confirmed Taylor's results.

Taylor has used this first [binary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_star "Binary star") [pulsar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulsar "Pulsar") to make high-precision tests of [general relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_relativity "General relativity"). Working with his colleague [Joel Weisberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joel_Weisberg?action=edit&redlink=1 "Joel Weisberg"), Taylor has used observations of this pulsar to demonstrate the existence of [gravitational radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_radiation "Gravitational radiation") in the amount and with the properties first predicted by [Albert Einstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Einstein "Albert Einstein"). He and [Hulse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russell_Alan_Hulse "Russell Alan Hulse") shared the [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics") (1993) for the discovery of this object. In 1980, he moved to [Princeton University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University "Princeton University"), where he was the [James S. McDonnell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Smith_McDonnell "James Smith McDonnell") Distinguished University Professor in Physics, having also served for six years as [Dean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dean_(education) "Dean (education)") of [Faculty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faculty_(teaching_staff) "Faculty (teaching staff)"). He retired in 2006.

## Amateur radio

Joe Taylor first obtained his [amateur radio license](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amateur_radio_license "Amateur radio license") as a teenager, which led him to the field of radio astronomy. Taylor is well known in the field of [amateur radio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amateur_radio "Amateur radio") weak signal communication and has been assigned the [call sign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Call_sign "Call sign") K1JT by the [FCC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Communications_Commission "Federal Communications Commission"). He had previously held the callsigns K2ITP, WA1LXQ, W1HFV, and VK2BJX (the latter in [Australia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australia "Australia")).

His amateur radio accomplishments have included mounting an 'expedition' in April 2010 to use the [Arecibo Radio Telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arecibo_Radio_Telescope "Arecibo Radio Telescope") to conduct [moonbounce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EME_(communications) "EME (communications)") with amateurs around the world using voice, [Morse code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morse_code "Morse code"), and digital communications.

He has been active in developing several computer programs and communications protocols, including [WSPR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WSPR_(amateur_radio_software) "WSPR (amateur radio software)") and [WSJT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WSJT_(amateur_radio_software) "WSJT (amateur radio software)") ("Weak Signal/Joe Taylor"), a software package and protocol suite that utilizes computer-generated messages in conjunction with radio [transceivers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transceiver "Transceiver") to communicate over long distances with other amateur radio operators.

WSJT is useful for passing short messages via non-traditional radio communications methods, such as [moonbounce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EME_(communications) "EME (communications)") and [meteor scatter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meteor_scatter "Meteor scatter") and other low [signal-to-noise ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signal-to-noise_ratio "Signal-to-noise ratio") paths. It is also useful for extremely long-distance contacts using [very low power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/QRP_operation "QRP operation") transmissions.

Taylor is also the co-creator of the [FT8](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FT8 "FT8") mode.

Taylor was among the first group of [MacArthur Fellows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MacArthur_Fellowship "MacArthur Fellowship"). He has served on many boards, committees, and panels, co-chairing the Decadal Panel of that produced the report [*Astronomy and Astrophysics in the New Millennium*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy_and_Astrophysics_Decadal_Survey#New_Worlds,_New_Horizons_in_Astronomy_and_Astrophysics,_2010 "Astronomy and Astrophysics Decadal Survey") that established the [United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States "United States")'s national priorities in astronomy and astrophysics for the period 2000–2010. He was a guest of honor in the 2009 International Physics Olympiad.
