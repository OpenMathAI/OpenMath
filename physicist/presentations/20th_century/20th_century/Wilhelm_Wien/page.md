**Wilhelm Carl Werner Otto Fritz Franz Wien** (German: [[ˈvɪlhɛlm ˈviːn]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Standard_German "Help:IPA/Standard German") [ⓘ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:De-Wilhelm_Wien.ogg "File:De-Wilhelm Wien.ogg"); 13 January 1864 – 30 August 1928) was a German [physicist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physicist "Physicist") who used theories about [heat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat "Heat") and [electromagnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetism "Electromagnetism") to deduce [Wien's displacement law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wien's_displacement_law "Wien's displacement law"), which calculates the [emission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emission_(electromagnetic_radiation) "Emission (electromagnetic radiation)") of a [blackbody](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blackbody "Blackbody") at any temperature from the emission at any one reference temperature.

Wien also formulated an expression for the [black-body radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black-body_radiation "Black-body radiation"), which is correct in the [photon-gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon-gas "Photon-gas") limit. His arguments were based on the notion of [adiabatic invariance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adiabatic_invariant "Adiabatic invariant"), and were instrumental for the formulation of [quantum mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_mechanics "Quantum mechanics"). Wien received the [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics") in 1911 for his work on [heat radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_radiation "Heat radiation").

He was a cousin of [Max Wien](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Wien "Max Wien"), inventor of the [Wien bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wien_bridge "Wien bridge").

## Early life and education

Wien was born on 13 January 1864 in Gaffken (now [Parusnoye](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parusnoye,_Kaliningrad_Oblast "Parusnoye, Kaliningrad Oblast"), Russia), [Prussia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prussia "Prussia"), the son of landowner Carl Wien. In 1866, his family moved to Drachenstein (now [Smokowo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smokowo,_Warmian-Masurian_Voivodeship "Smokowo, Warmian-Masurian Voivodeship"), Poland).

In 1879, Wien went to school in Rastenburg (now [Kętrzyn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kętrzyn "Kętrzyn"), Poland) and from 1880 to 1882 he attended the city school of [Heidelberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heidelberg "Heidelberg"). In 1882, he attended the [University of Göttingen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Göttingen "University of Göttingen") and the [University of Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Berlin "University of Berlin"). From 1883 to 1885, he worked in the laboratory of [Hermann von Helmholtz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann_von_Helmholtz "Hermann von Helmholtz") and, in 1886, he received his [Ph.D.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Philosophy "Doctor of Philosophy") with a thesis on the [diffraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffraction "Diffraction") of light upon metals and on the influence of various materials upon the color of refracted light. From 1896 to 1899, Wien lectured at [RWTH Aachen University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RWTH_Aachen_University "RWTH Aachen University"). He became twice successor of [Wilhelm Röntgen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilhelm_Röntgen "Wilhelm Röntgen"), in 1900 at the [University of Würzburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Würzburg "University of Würzburg") and in 1920 at the [Ludwig-Maximilians-Universität München](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludwig-Maximilians-Universität_München "Ludwig-Maximilians-Universität München"). Wien was very active in science politics representing conservative and nationalistic positions though being not as extreme as sharing the attitude of those going to develop the *[Deutsche Physik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deutsche_Physik "Deutsche Physik")*. He appreciated both [Albert Einstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Einstein "Albert Einstein") and [relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theory_of_relativity "Theory of relativity").

## Career

In 1896, Wien empirically determined a distribution law of [blackbody radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blackbody_radiation "Blackbody radiation"), later named after him: [Wien's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wien_approximation "Wien approximation"). [Max Planck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Planck "Max Planck"), who was a colleague of Wien's, did not believe in empirical laws, so using electromagnetism and thermodynamics, he proposed a theoretical basis for Wien's law, which became the [Wien–Planck law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wien–Planck_law "Wien–Planck law"). However, Wien's law was only valid at high frequencies, and underestimated the radiancy at low frequencies. Planck corrected the theory and proposed what is now called [Planck's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planck's_law "Planck's law"), which led to the development of [quantum theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_mechanics "Quantum mechanics"). Wien developed the [Wien filter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wien_filter "Wien filter") (also known as velocity selector) in 1898 for the study of anode rays. It is a device consisting of perpendicular electric and magnetic fields that can be used as a velocity filter for charged particles, for example in electron microscopes and spectrometers. It is used in accelerator mass spectrometry to select particles based on their speed. The device is composed of orthogonal electric and magnetic fields, such that particles with the correct speed will be unaffected while other particles will be deflected. It can be configured as a charged particle energy analyzer, monochromator, or mass spectrometer.

While studying streams of [ionized gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionized_gas "Ionized gas"), Wien, in 1898, identified a positive particle equal in mass to the [hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen "Hydrogen") [atom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atom "Atom"). Wien, with this work, laid the foundation of [mass spectrometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_spectrometry "Mass spectrometry"). [J. J. Thomson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._J._Thomson "J. J. Thomson") refined Wien's apparatus and conducted further experiments in 1913 then, after work by [Ernest Rutherford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_Rutherford "Ernest Rutherford") in 1919, Wien's particle was accepted and named the [proton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proton "Proton").

In 1911, Wien was awarded the [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics") "for his discoveries regarding the laws governing the radiation of heat". He delivered the [Ernest Kempton Adams Lecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_Kempton_Adams_Lectures "Ernest Kempton Adams Lectures") at [Columbia University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia_University "Columbia University") in 1913.
