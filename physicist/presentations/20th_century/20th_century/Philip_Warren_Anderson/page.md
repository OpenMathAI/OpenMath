**Philip Warren Anderson** (December 13, 1923 – March 29, 2020) was an American [theoretical physicist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theoretical_physicist "Theoretical physicist") who shared the 1977 [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics") with [Nevill Mott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nevill_Mott "Nevill Mott") and [John Van Vleck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Van_Vleck "John Van Vleck") "for their fundamental theoretical investigations of the electronic structure of magnetic and disordered systems."

Anderson made contributions to the theories of [localization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anderson_localization "Anderson localization"), [antiferromagnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antiferromagnetism "Antiferromagnetism"), [symmetry breaking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetry_breaking "Symmetry breaking") (including a paper in 1962 discussing symmetry breaking in [particle physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_physics "Particle physics"), leading to the development of the [Standard Model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_Model "Standard Model") around 10 years later), and [high-temperature superconductivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-temperature_superconductivity "High-temperature superconductivity"), and to the [philosophy of science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_science "Philosophy of science") through his writings on [emergent phenomena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emergent_phenomena "Emergent phenomena"). He is also responsible for naming the field of physics that is now known as [condensed matter physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condensed_matter_physics "Condensed matter physics").

## Education

Philip Warren Anderson was born on December 13, 1923, in [Indianapolis, Indiana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indianapolis,_Indiana "Indianapolis, Indiana"), and grew up in [Urbana, Illinois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urbana,_Illinois "Urbana, Illinois"). His father, Harry Warren Anderson, was a professor of plant pathology at the [University of Illinois at Urbana–Champaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Illinois_at_Urbana–Champaign "University of Illinois at Urbana–Champaign"); his maternal grandfather was a mathematician at [Wabash College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wabash_College "Wabash College"), where Anderson's father studied; and his maternal uncle was a [Rhodes Scholar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhodes_Scholarship "Rhodes Scholarship") who became a professor of English, also at Wabash College.

Anderson graduated from [University Laboratory High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_Laboratory_High_School_(Urbana,_Illinois) "University Laboratory High School (Urbana, Illinois)") in Urbana in 1940. Under the encouragement of a math teacher by the name of Miles Hartley, he enrolled at [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University "Harvard University") to study under a fully-funded scholarship. He concentrated in "Electronic Physics" and received his [B.S.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B.S. "B.S.") in 1943, after which he was drafted into the war effort and built antennas in the [Naval Research Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Naval_Research_Laboratory "United States Naval Research Laboratory") until the end of the [Second World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II") in 1945. As an undergraduate, his close associates included particle-nuclear physicist [H. Pierre Noyes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H._Pierre_Noyes "H. Pierre Noyes"), philosopher and historian of science [Thomas Kuhn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Kuhn "Thomas Kuhn"), and molecular physicist Henry Silsbee.

After the war, Anderson returned to Harvard to pursue graduate studies in physics, obtaining an [M.A.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M.A. "M.A.") in 1947 and a [Ph.D.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ph.D. "Ph.D.") in 1949. His doctoral thesis, written under [John Van Vleck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Van_Vleck "John Van Vleck"), was titled *The Theory of Pressure Broadening of Spectral Lines in the Microwave and Infrared Regions*.

## Career and research

From 1949 to 1984, Anderson was employed by [Bell Telephone Laboratories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell_Telephone_Laboratories "Bell Telephone Laboratories") in [New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Jersey "New Jersey"), where he worked on a wide variety of problems in [condensed matter physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condensed_matter_physics "Condensed matter physics"). During this period, he developed what is now called [Anderson localization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anderson_localization "Anderson localization") (the idea that extended states can be localized by the presence of disorder in a system) and [Anderson's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anderson's_theorem_(superconductivity) "Anderson's theorem (superconductivity)") (concerning impurity scattering in superconductors); invented the [Anderson Hamiltonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anderson_Hamiltonian "Anderson Hamiltonian"), which describes the site-wise interaction of electrons in a [transition metal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transition_metal "Transition metal"); proposed [symmetry breaking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetry_breaking "Symmetry breaking") within [particle physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_physics "Particle physics") (this played a role in the development of the [Standard Model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_Model "Standard Model") and the development of the theory behind the [Higgs mechanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higgs_mechanism "Higgs mechanism"), which in turn generates [mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass "Mass") in some [elementary particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elementary_particle "Elementary particle")); created the pseudospin approach to the [BCS theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BCS_theory "BCS theory") of [superconductivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superconductivity "Superconductivity"); made seminal studies of [non-s-wave pairing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_wave_analysis "Partial wave analysis") (both symmetry-breaking and microscopic mechanism) in the superfluidity of [helium-3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helium-3 "Helium-3"), and helped found the area of [spin-glasses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spin_glass "Spin glass").

Anderson spent a year as lecturer at the [University of Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Cambridge "University of Cambridge") in 1961–1962, and recalled that having [Brian Josephson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brian_Josephson "Brian Josephson") in a class was "a disconcerting experience for a lecturer, I can assure you, because everything had to be right or he would come up and explain it to me after class."

From 1967 to 1975, Anderson was a professor of theoretical physics at Cambridge. In 1977 Anderson was awarded the [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics") for his investigations into the electronic structure of magnetic and disordered systems, which allowed for the development of electronic switching and memory devices in computers. Co-researchers [Nevill Mott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nevill_Mott "Nevill Mott") and [John Van Vleck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Van_Vleck "John Van Vleck") shared the award with him. In 1982, he was awarded the [National Medal of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Medal_of_Science "National Medal of Science"). He retired from Bell Labs in 1984 and was Joseph Henry Professor Emeritus of Physics at [Princeton University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University "Princeton University").

Anderson's writings included *Concepts in Solids*, *Basic Notions of Condensed Matter Physics* and *The Theory of Superconductivity in the High-Tc Cuprates*. Anderson served on the board of advisors of [Scientists and Engineers for America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientists_and_Engineers_for_America "Scientists and Engineers for America"), an organization focused on promoting sound science in American government.

In response to the discovery of high-temperature superconductors in the 1980s, Anderson proposed [Resonating valence bond (RVB) theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resonating_valence_bond_theory "Resonating valence bond theory") to explain the phenomenon. While many found the idea unconvincing, RVB theory proved instrumental in the study of [spin liquids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_spin_liquid "Quantum spin liquid").

Anderson also made conceptual contributions to the philosophy of science through his explication of [emergent phenomena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emergent_phenomena "Emergent phenomena"), which became an inspiration for the science of complex systems. In 1972, he wrote an article called "More is Different" in which he emphasized the limitations of reductionism and the existence of hierarchical levels of science, each of which requires its own fundamental principles for advancement.

In 1984, he participated in the founding workshops of the [Santa Fe Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santa_Fe_Institute "Santa Fe Institute"), a multidisciplinary research institute dedicated to the science of complex systems. Anderson also co-chaired the institute's 1987 conference on economics with [Kenneth Arrow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenneth_Arrow "Kenneth Arrow") and [W. Brian Arthur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W._Brian_Arthur "W. Brian Arthur"), and participated in its 2007 workshop on models of emergent behavior in complex systems.

In 1987, Anderson testified to the US Congress, "against the construction of the [Superconducting Super Collider](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superconducting_Super_Collider "Superconducting Super Collider") (SSC), a 40 TeV proton-proton collider in Texas that would have been the biggest experiment in particle physics. Anderson's opposition to the SSC did not directly lead to its cancellation in 1993—spiralling costs were the main factor—but he was perhaps its most high-profile opponent." He was, "skeptical of the supposed boost it would provide to science in the US and the claim that the spin-offs would provide great return on investment."

A 2006 statistical analysis of scientific research papers by José Soler, comparing the number of references in a paper to the number of citations, declared Anderson to be the "most creative" amongst ten most cited physicists in the world. In 2021, Oxford University Press published the biography *A Mind over Matter: Philip Anderson and the Physics of the Very Many* by Andrew Zangwill.

## Personal life

Anderson was an [atheist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atheist "Atheist") and was one of 22 Nobel Laureates who signed the [Humanist Manifesto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humanism_and_Its_Aspirations "Humanism and Its Aspirations"). Anderson was also interested in Japanese culture, living there for a time and becoming a 1st [Dan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dan_(rank) "Dan (rank)") master of the board game [Go](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Go_(game) "Go (game)"). The [Nihon Ki-in](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nihon_Ki-in "Nihon Ki-in") awarded him a lifetime achievement award in 2007, and Anderson joked that there were only four people in Japan who could beat him.

Anderson married Joyce Gothwaite in 1947 and they had a daughter, Susan. He died on March 29, 2020, in [Princeton, New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton,_New_Jersey "Princeton, New Jersey"), at the age of 96.
