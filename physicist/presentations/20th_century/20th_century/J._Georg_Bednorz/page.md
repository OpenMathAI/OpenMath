**Johannes Georg Bednorz** (German: [[ˈɡeːɔʁk ˈbɛdnɔʁt͡s]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Standard_German "Help:IPA/Standard German") [ⓘ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:De-Georg_Bednorz.ogg "File:De-Georg Bednorz.ogg"); born 16 May 1950) is a German [physicist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physicist "Physicist") who, together with [K. Alex Müller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K._Alex_Müller "K. Alex Müller"), discovered [high-temperature superconductivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-temperature_superconductivity "High-temperature superconductivity") in [ceramics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ceramic "Ceramic"), for which they shared the 1987 [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics").

## Biography

Johannes Georg Bednorz was born on 16 May 1950 in [Neuenkirchen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neuenkirchen,_Westphalia "Neuenkirchen, Westphalia"), [North Rhine-Westphalia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Rhine-Westphalia "North Rhine-Westphalia"), the fourth child of Anton Bednorz, an elementary school teacher, and his wife Elisabeth, a piano teacher. His parents were both from [Silesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silesia "Silesia") in Central Europe, but [were forced to move westwards in turbulences of World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flight_and_expulsion_of_Germans_(1944-1950) "Flight and expulsion of Germans (1944-1950)").

As a child, his parents tried to get him interested in classical music, but he was more practically inclined, preferring to work on motorcycles and cars. (Although as a teenager he did eventually learn to play the violin and trumpet.) In high school he developed an interest in the natural sciences, focusing on [chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemistry "Chemistry"), which he could learn in a hands-on manner through experiments.

In 1968, Bednorz enrolled at the [University of Münster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Münster "University of Münster") to study chemistry. However, he soon felt lost in the large body of students, and opt to switch to the much less popular subject of [crystallography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystallography "Crystallography"), a subfield of [mineralogy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mineralogy "Mineralogy") at the interface of chemistry and physics. In 1972, his teachers Wolfgang Hoffmann and Horst Böhm arranged for him to spend the summer at the [IBM Zurich Research Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM_Zurich_Research_Laboratory "IBM Zurich Research Laboratory") as a visiting student. The experience here would shape his further career: not only did he meet his later collaborator [K. Alex Müller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K._Alex_Müller "K. Alex Müller"), the head of the physics department, but he also experienced the atmosphere of creativity and freedom cultivated at the IBM lab, which he credits as a strong influence on his way of conducting science.

After another visit in 1973, Bednorz came to Zurich in 1974 for six months to do the experimental part of his [master’s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master's_degree "Master's degree") thesis. Here he grew crystals of SrTiO3, a ceramic material belonging to the family of [perovskites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perovskites "Perovskites"). Müller, himself interested in perovskites, urged him to continue his research. After graduating from Münster in 1976, Bednorz started a [Ph.D](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ph.D "Ph.D") at [ETH Zurich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ETH_Zurich "ETH Zurich") under the supervision of Heini Gränicher and Alex Müller. In 1978, his future wife, Mechthild Wennemer, whom he had met in Münster, followed him to Zurich to start her own Ph.D.

After obtaining his Ph.D. in 1982, Bednorz joined the IBM lab. There, he joined Müller's ongoing research on [superconductivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superconductivity "Superconductivity"). The following year, Bednorz and Müller began a systematic study of the electrical properties of ceramics formed from [transition metal oxides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transition_metal_oxide "Transition metal oxide"), and in 1986 they succeeded in inducing superconductivity in a [lanthanum barium copper oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lanthanum_barium_copper_oxide "Lanthanum barium copper oxide") (LaBaCuO, also known as LBCO). The oxide's [critical temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critical_point_(thermodynamics) "Critical point (thermodynamics)") (Tc) was 35 K, a full 12 K higher than the previous record. This discovery stimulated a great deal of additional research in [high-temperature superconductivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-temperature_superconductivity "High-temperature superconductivity") on cuprate materials with structures similar to LBCO, soon leading to the discovery of compounds such as [BSCCO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BSCCO "BSCCO") (Tc 107K) and [YBCO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/YBCO "YBCO") (Tc 92K).

In 1987, Bednorz and Müller were jointly awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics "for their important break-through in the discovery of superconductivity in ceramic materials." The same year, Bednorz was appointed an [IBM Fellow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM_Fellow "IBM Fellow").
