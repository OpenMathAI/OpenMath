**Martin Lewis Perl** (June 24, 1927 – September 30, 2014) was an American [chemical engineer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_engineer "Chemical engineer") and [physicist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physicist "Physicist") who won the [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics") in 1995 for his [discovery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discovery_(observation) "Discovery (observation)") of the [tau lepton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tau_lepton "Tau lepton").

## Life and career

Perl was born in New York City, New York. His parents, Fay (née Resenthal), a secretary and bookkeeper, and Oscar Perl, a stationery salesman who founded a printing and advertising company, were [Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish "Jewish") [immigrants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immigration "Immigration") to the US from the [Polish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_people "Polish people") area of [Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russia "Russia").

Perl was a 1948 [chemical engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_engineering "Chemical engineering") graduate of Brooklyn Polytechnic Institute (now known as [NYU-Tandon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_University_Tandon_School_of_Engineering "New York University Tandon School of Engineering")) in Brooklyn. After graduation, Perl worked for the [General Electric Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Electric_Company "General Electric Company"), as a chemical engineer in a factory producing electron vacuum tubes. To learn about how the electron tubes worked, Perl signed up for courses in atomic physics and advanced calculus at [Union College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_College "Union College") in [Schenectady, New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schenectady,_New_York "Schenectady, New York"), which led to his growing interest in physics, and eventually to becoming a graduate student in physics in 1950.

He received his Ph.D. from [Columbia University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia_University "Columbia University") in 1955, where his thesis advisor was [I.I. Rabi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I.I._Rabi "I.I. Rabi"). Perl's thesis described measurements of the nuclear quadrupole moment of [sodium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium "Sodium"), using the atomic beam resonance method that Rabi had won the Nobel Prize in Physics for in 1944.

Following his Ph.D., Perl spent 8 years at the [University of Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Michigan "University of Michigan"), where he worked on the physics of [strong interactions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strong_interaction "Strong interaction"), using [bubble chambers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bubble_chamber "Bubble chamber") and [spark chambers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spark_chamber "Spark chamber") to study the scattering of [pions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pion "Pion") and later [neutrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron "Neutron") on [protons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proton "Proton").
While at Michigan, Perl and [Lawrence W. Jones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawrence_W._Jones "Lawrence W. Jones") served as co-advisors to [Samuel C. C. Ting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_C._C._Ting "Samuel C. C. Ting"), who earned the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1976.

Seeking a simpler interaction mechanism to study, Perl started to consider [electron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron "Electron") and [muon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muon "Muon") interactions.
He had the opportunity to start planning experimental work in this area when he moved in 1963 to the [Stanford Linear Accelerator Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford_Linear_Accelerator_Center "Stanford Linear Accelerator Center") (SLAC), then being built in [California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California "California"). He was particularly interested in understanding the [muon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muon "Muon"): why it should interact almost exactly like the electron but be 206.8 times heavier, and why it should decay through the route that it does. Perl chose to look for answers to these questions in experiments on high-energy charged [leptons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lepton "Lepton"). In addition, he considered the possibility of finding a third [generation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generation_(particle_physics) "Generation (particle physics)") of lepton through electron-[positron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positron "Positron") collisions.

Perl is one of the 20 American recipients of the Nobel Prize in Physics to sign a letter addressed to President [George W. Bush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_W._Bush "George W. Bush") in May 2008, urging him to "reverse the damage done to basic science research in the Fiscal Year 2008 Omnibus Appropriations Bill" by requesting additional emergency funding for the [Department of Energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Energy "United States Department of Energy")'s [Office of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Office_of_Science "Office of Science"), the [National Science Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Science_Foundation "National Science Foundation"), and the [National Institute of Standards and Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Institute_of_Standards_and_Technology "National Institute of Standards and Technology").

He died after a heart attack at Stanford University Hospital on September 30, 2014, at the age of 87. His son, [Jed Perl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jed_Perl "Jed Perl"), is an author and art critic for *[The New Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_Republic "The New Republic")*.

### Discovery of the tau particle

The [tau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tau_(particle) "Tau (particle)") lepton (τ, also called the tau particle, tauon or simply tau) is an [elementary particle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elementary_particle "Elementary particle") similar to the electron, with negative [electric charge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_charge "Electric charge") and a [spin of 1⁄2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spin-½ "Spin-½"), but with 3477 times the mass. Together with the [electron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron "Electron"), the [muon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muon "Muon"), and the three [neutrinos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutrino "Neutrino"), it is classified as a [lepton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lepton "Lepton").

The tau was first detected in a series of experiments between 1974 and 1977 by Perl with his colleagues at the [SLAC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SLAC "SLAC")-[LBL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawrence_Berkeley_National_Laboratory "Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory") group. Their equipment consisted of [SLAC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SLAC "SLAC")'s then-new e+
–e−
 colliding ring, called [SPEAR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SPEAR "SPEAR"), and the [LBL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawrence_Berkeley_National_Laboratory "Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory") magnetic detector. They could detect and distinguish between leptons, hadrons and [photons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon "Photon"). SPEAR was able to collide electrons and positrons at higher energies than had previously been possible, initially at up to 4.8 GeV and eventually at 8 GeV, energies high enough to lead to the production of a tau/antitau pair. The tau has a lifetime of only 2.9×10−13 s and so these particles decayed within a few millimetres of the collision. Hence Perl and his coworkers did not detect the tau directly, but rather discovered anomalous events where they detected either an electron and a muon, or a positron and an antimuon:

> We have discovered 64 events of the form
>
> :   e+
>      + e−
>      → e±
>      + μ∓
>      + at least two undetected particles
>
> for which we have no conventional explanation.

The need for at least two undetected particles was shown by the inability to conserve energy and momentum with only one. However, no other muons, electrons, photons, or hadrons were detected. It was proposed that this event was the production and subsequent decay of a new particle pair:

:   e+
     + e−
     → τ+
     + τ−
     → e±
     + μ∓
     + 4ν

This was difficult to verify, because the energy to produce the τ+
τ−
 pair is similar to the threshold for [D meson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D_meson "D meson") production. Work done at [DESY](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DESY "DESY")-Hamburg, and with the Direct Electron Counter (DELCO) at [SPEAR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SPEAR "SPEAR"), subsequently confirmed the discovery and established the mass and spin of the tau.

The symbol τ was derived from the Greek *τρίτον* (*triton*, meaning "third" in English), since it was the third charged lepton discovered.

### Nobel Prize and later career

Perl won the Nobel Prize in 1995 jointly with [Frederick Reines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_Reines "Frederick Reines"). The prize was awarded "for pioneering experimental contributions to lepton physics". Perl received half "for the discovery of the tau lepton" while Reines received his share "for the detection of the [neutrino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutrino "Neutrino")". In 1996 he published *Reflections on Experimental Science*, which consists of "comments, scientific reprints, reflections, and a memoir ...".

He joined [University of Liverpool](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Liverpool "University of Liverpool") as a visiting professor. He served on the board of advisors of [Scientists and Engineers for America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientists_and_Engineers_for_America "Scientists and Engineers for America"), an organization focused on promoting sound science in American government. In 1996, he received the Golden Plate Award of the [American Academy of Achievement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academy_of_Achievement "Academy of Achievement"). In 2009, Perl received an honorary doctorate from the [University of Belgrade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Belgrade "University of Belgrade").
