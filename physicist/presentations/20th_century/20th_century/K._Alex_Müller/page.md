**Karl Alexander Müller** (20 April 1927 – 9 January 2023) was a Swiss [physicist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physicist "Physicist"). He received the [Nobel Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize "Nobel Prize") in Physics in 1987 with [Georg Bednorz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georg_Bednorz "Georg Bednorz") for their discovery of [superconductivity in ceramic materials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-temperature_superconductivity "High-temperature superconductivity").

## Early life and education

Karl Alexander Müller was born on 20 April 1927 in [Basel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basel "Basel"), Switzerland, the son of Paul Rudolf Müller and Irma Feigenbaum. His mother was [Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jews "Jews"). His family immediately moved to [Salzburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salzburg "Salzburg"), Austria, where his father was studying music. Alex and his mother then moved to [Dornach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dornach "Dornach"), the home of his maternal grandparents. Then they moved to [Lugano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lugano "Lugano"), in the [Italian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_language "Italian language")-speaking part of Switzerland, where he learned to speak Italian fluently. His mother died when he was 11.

Müller was sent to school at the Evangelical College in [Schiers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schiers "Schiers"), in the eastern part of Switzerland. Here he studied from 1938 to 1945, obtaining his baccalaureate (*[Matura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matura "Matura")*). He then enrolled in the Physics and Mathematics Department of [ETH Zurich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ETH_Zurich "ETH Zurich"). He took courses by [Wolfgang Pauli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolfgang_Pauli "Wolfgang Pauli"), who made a deep impression on him. After receiving his *[Diplom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diplom "Diplom")* in 1952, he worked for one year, then returned to ETH Zurich for a [Ph.D.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ph.D. "Ph.D."), submitting his thesis at the end of 1957.

## Career

After graduating in 1958, Müller joined the [Battelle Memorial Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battelle_Memorial_Institute "Battelle Memorial Institute") as the manager of a magnetic resonance group. During this time, he became a lecturer at the [University of Zürich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Zürich "University of Zürich"). In 1963, he accepted an offer as a research staff member at the [IBM Zurich Research Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM_Zurich_Research_Laboratory "IBM Zurich Research Laboratory") in [Rüschlikon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rüschlikon "Rüschlikon"), where he remained until his retirement. In parallel, he maintained his affiliation with the University of Zurich, where he was appointed a professor in 1970. He became an [IBM Fellow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM_Fellow "IBM Fellow") in 1982.

## Research

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f3/Karl_Alex_Muller_LANL.jpg/250px-Karl_Alex_Muller_LANL.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Karl_Alex_Muller_LANL.jpg)

Müller in 2002

For his undergraduate diploma work, Müller studied under G. Busch. He worked on the [Hall Effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hall_Effect "Hall Effect") in [gray tin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tin "Tin"), a [semimetal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semimetal "Semimetal").

Between his [undergraduate degree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Undergraduate_degree "Undergraduate degree") and beginning his [graduate studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graduate_student "Graduate student"), he worked for one year in the Department of Industrial Research at ETH on the [Eidophor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eidophor "Eidophor") large-scale display system.

At [IBM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM "IBM") his research for almost 15 years centered on SrTiO3 ([strontium titanate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strontium_titanate "Strontium titanate")) and related [perovskite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perovskite "Perovskite") compounds. He studied their [photochromic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photochromism "Photochromism") properties when doped with various [transition-metal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transition_metal "Transition metal") [ions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ion "Ion"); their [chemical binding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_bond "Chemical bond"), [ferroelectric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferroelectricity "Ferroelectricity") and soft-mode properties; and the critical and multicritical phenomena of their structural [phase transitions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_transition "Phase transition"). Important highlights of this research have been published in a book written together with [Tom Kool](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tom_Kool?action=edit&redlink=1 "Tom Kool") from the [University of Amsterdam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Amsterdam "University of Amsterdam") (publisher: [World Scientific](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Scientific "World Scientific")).

## Personal life and death

In the spring of 1956, Müller married Ingeborg Marie Louise Winkler. They had a son, Eric, in the summer of 1957, and a daughter, Sylvia, in 1960.

Müller died on 9 January 2023 in [Zurich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zurich "Zurich") at the age of 95.

## Nobel Prize-winning work

In the early 1980s, Müller began searching for substances that would become superconductive at higher temperatures. The highest [critical temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superconductivity "Superconductivity") (*T*c) attainable at that time was about 23 [K](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kelvin "Kelvin"). In 1983 Müller recruited Georg Bednorz to IBM, to help systematically test various oxides. A few recent studies had indicated these materials might superconduct, but experts who knew about Müller's idea thought it was “crazy”. In 1986 the two researchers succeeded in achieving superconductivity in [lanthanum barium copper oxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lanthanum_barium_copper_oxide "Lanthanum barium copper oxide") (LBCO) at a temperature of 35 [K](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kelvin "Kelvin"). Over the previous 75 years the critical temperature had risen from 11 K in 1911 to 23 [K](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kelvin "Kelvin") in 1973 where it had remained for 13 years. Thus 35 K was incredibly high by the prevailing standards of superconductivity research. This discovery stimulated a great deal of additional research in [high-temperature superconductivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-temperature_superconductivity "High-temperature superconductivity"), leading to the discovery of compounds such as [BSCCO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BSCCO "BSCCO") (*T*c = 107 K) and [YBCO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/YBCO "YBCO") (*T'*c = 92 K).

They reported their discovery in the June 1986 issue of *[Zeitschrift für Physik B](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeitschrift_für_Physik_B "Zeitschrift für Physik B")*. Before the end of the year, [Shoji Tanaka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shoji_Tanaka?action=edit&redlink=1 "Shoji Tanaka") at the [University of Tokyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Tokyo "University of Tokyo") and then [Paul Chu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_C._W._Chu "Paul C. W. Chu") at the [University of Houston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Houston "University of Houston") had each independently confirmed their result. A couple of months later Chu achieved superconductivity at 93 K in [YBCO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/YBCO "YBCO"), triggering a stampede of scientific interest exemplified by the 1987 "[Woodstock of physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woodstock_of_physics "Woodstock of physics")", at which Müller was a featured presenter.

In 1987, Müller and Bednorz were jointly awarded the [Nobel Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize "Nobel Prize") in physics—the shortest time between the discovery and the prize award for any scientific Nobel.
