**Jack Steinberger** (born **Hans Jakob Steinberger**; May 25, 1921 – December 12, 2020) was a German-born American [physicist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physicist "Physicist") noted for his work with [neutrinos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutrino "Neutrino"), the subatomic particles considered to be elementary constituents of matter. He was a recipient of the 1988 [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics"), along with [Leon M. Lederman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leon_M._Lederman "Leon M. Lederman") and [Melvin Schwartz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melvin_Schwartz "Melvin Schwartz"), for the discovery of the [muon neutrino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muon_neutrino "Muon neutrino"). Through his career as an experimental particle physicist, he held positions at the [University of California, Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Berkeley "University of California, Berkeley"), [Columbia University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia_University "Columbia University") (1950–68), and the [CERN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CERN "CERN") (1968–86). He was also a recipient of the United States [National Medal of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Medal_of_Science "National Medal of Science") in 1988, and the [Matteucci Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matteucci_Medal "Matteucci Medal") from the [Italian Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_Academy_of_Sciences "Italian Academy of Sciences") in 1990.

## Early life and education

Steinberger was born in the city of [Bad Kissingen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bad_Kissingen "Bad Kissingen") in [Bavaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bavaria "Bavaria"), [Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germany "Germany"), on May 25, 1921, into a [Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jews "Jews") family. The rise of [Nazism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazism "Nazism") in Germany, with its open [anti-Semitism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-Semitism "Anti-Semitism"), prompted his parents, Ludwig Lazarus (a [cantor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cantor "Cantor") and religious teacher) and Berta May Steinberger, to send him out of the country.

Steinberger emigrated to the [United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States "United States") at the age of 13, making the trans-Atlantic trip with his brother Herbert. Jewish charities in the U.S. arranged for Barnett Farroll to care for him as a foster child. Steinberger attended [New Trier Township High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Trier_Township_High_School "New Trier Township High School"), in [Winnetka, Illinois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winnetka,_Illinois "Winnetka, Illinois"). He was reunited with his parents and younger brother in 1938.

Steinberger studied chemical engineering at Armour Institute of Technology (now [Illinois Institute of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illinois_Institute_of_Technology "Illinois Institute of Technology")) but left after his scholarship ended to help supplement his family's income. He obtained a bachelor's degree in chemistry from the [University of Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Chicago "University of Chicago"), in 1942. Shortly thereafter, he joined the [Signal Corps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signal_Corps_(United_States_Army) "Signal Corps (United States Army)") at [MIT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MIT "MIT"). With the help of the [G.I. Bill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G.I._Bill "G.I. Bill"), he returned to graduate studies at the [University of Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Chicago "University of Chicago") in 1946, where he studied under [Edward Teller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Teller "Edward Teller") and [Enrico Fermi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enrico_Fermi "Enrico Fermi"). His Ph.D. thesis concerned the energy spectrum of electrons emitted in [muon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muon "Muon") [decay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_decay "Particle decay"); his results showed that this was a three-body decay, and implied the participation of two neutral particles in the decay (later identified as the [electron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_neutrino "Electron neutrino") (

## Career

### Early research

After receiving his doctorate, Steinberger attended the [Institute for Advanced Study](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_for_Advanced_Study "Institute for Advanced Study") in [Princeton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton,_New_Jersey "Princeton, New Jersey") for a year. In 1949 he published a calculation of the lifetime of the neutral [pion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pion "Pion"), which anticipated the study of anomalies in quantum field theory.

Following Princeton, in 1949, Steinberger went to the [Radiation Lab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiation_Lab "Radiation Lab") at the [University of California at Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California_at_Berkeley "University of California at Berkeley"), where he performed an experiment which demonstrated the production of neutral pions and their decay to photon pairs. This experiment utilized the 330 MeV synchrotron and the newly invented scintillation counters. Despite this and other achievements, he was asked to leave the Radiation Lab at Berkeley in 1950, due to his refusal to sign the so-called non-Communist Oath.

Steinberger accepted a faculty position at [Columbia University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia_University "Columbia University") in 1950. The newly commissioned [meson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meson "Meson") beam at [Nevis Labs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nevis_Laboratories "Nevis Laboratories") provided the tool for several important experiments. Measurements of the production cross-section of pions on various nuclear targets showed that the pion has odd parity. A direct measurement of the production of pions on a [liquid hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid_hydrogen "Liquid hydrogen") target, then not a common tool, provided the data needed to show that the pion has spin zero. The same target was used to observe the relatively rare decay of neutral pions to a photon, an electron, and a [positron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positron "Positron"). A related experiment measured the mass difference between the charged and neutral pions based on the angular correlation between the neutral pions produced when the negative pion is captured by the proton in the hydrogen nucleus. Other important experiments studied the angular correlation between electron–positron pairs in neutral pion decays, and established the rare decay of a charged pion to an electron and neutrino; the latter required use of a liquid-hydrogen [bubble chamber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bubble_chamber "Bubble chamber").

### Investigations of strange particles

During 1954–1955, Steinberger contributed to the development of the [bubble chamber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bubble_chamber "Bubble chamber") with the construction of a 15 cm device for use with the [Cosmotron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmotron "Cosmotron") at [Brookhaven National Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brookhaven_National_Laboratory "Brookhaven National Laboratory"). The experiment used a pion beam to produce pairs of [hadrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadrons "Hadrons") with [strange quarks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strange_quarks "Strange quarks") to elucidate the puzzling production and decay properties of these particles. In 1956, he used a 30 cm chamber outfitted with three cameras to discover the neutral [Sigma hyperon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigma_baryon "Sigma baryon") and measure its mass. This observation was important for confirming the existence of the SU(3) flavor symmetry which hypothesizes the existence of the strange quark.

An important characteristic of the [weak interaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weak_interaction "Weak interaction") is its violation of [parity symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parity_symmetry "Parity symmetry"). This characteristic was established through the measurement of the spins and parities of many [hyperons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperons "Hyperons"). Steinberger and his collaborators contributed several such measurements using large (75 cm) liquid-hydrogen bubble chambers and separated hadron beams at Brookhaven. One example is the measurement of the invariant mass distribution of electron–positron pairs produced in the decay of [Sigma-zero hyperons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigma_baryon "Sigma baryon") to [Lambda-zero hyperons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lambda_baryon "Lambda baryon").

### Neutrinos and the weak neutral current

In the 1960s, the emphasis in the study of the weak interaction shifted from strange particles to neutrinos. Leon Lederman, Steinberger and [Schwartz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melvin_Schwartz "Melvin Schwartz") built large spark chambers at [Nevis Labs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nevis_Labs "Nevis Labs") and exposed them in 1961 to neutrinos produced in association with muons in the decays of charged pions and kaons. They used the [Alternating Gradient Synchrotron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alternating_Gradient_Synchrotron "Alternating Gradient Synchrotron") (AGS) at Brookhaven, and obtained a number of convincing events in which muons were produced, but no electrons. This result, for which they received the Nobel Prize in 1988, proved the existence of a type of neutrino associated with the muon, distinct from the neutrino produced in beta decay.

### Study of CP violation

The [CP violation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CP_violation "CP violation") ([charge conjugation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charge_conjugation "Charge conjugation") and [parity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parity_(physics) "Parity (physics)")) was established in the neutral kaon system in 1964. Steinberger recognized that the phenomenological parameter epsilon (*ε*) which quantifies the degree of CP violation could be measured in interference phenomena (See CP violation). In collaboration with [Carlo Rubbia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carlo_Rubbia "Carlo Rubbia"), he performed an experiment while on sabbatical at [CERN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CERN "CERN") during 1965 which demonstrated robustly the expected interference effect, and also measured precisely the difference in mass of the short-lived and long-lived neutral kaon masses.

Back in the United States, Steinberger conducted an experiment at Brookhaven to observe CP violation in the semi-leptonic decays of neutral kaons. The charge asymmetry relates directly to the epsilon parameter, which was thereby measured precisely. This experiment also allowed the deduction of the phase of epsilon, and confirmed that [CPT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CPT_symmetry "CPT symmetry") is a good symmetry of nature.

### CERN

In 1968, Steinberger left Columbia University and accepted a position as a department director at [CERN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CERN "CERN"). He constructed an experiment there utilizing multi-wire proportional chambers ([MWPC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MWPC "MWPC")), recently invented by [Georges Charpak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georges_Charpak "Georges Charpak"). The MWPCs, augmented by micro-electronic amplifiers, allowed much larger samples of events to be recorded. Several results for neutral kaons were obtained and published in the early 1970s, including the observation of the rare decay of the neutral kaon to a muon pair, the time dependence of the asymmetry for semi-leptonic decays, and a more-precise measurement of the neutral kaon mass difference. A new era in experimental technique was opened.

These new techniques proved crucial for the first demonstration of direct [CP-violation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CP-violation "CP-violation"). The [NA31 experiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NA31_experiment "NA31 experiment") at CERN was built in the early 1980s using the CERN [SPS 400 GeV proton synchrotron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Super_Proton_Synchrotron "Super Proton Synchrotron"). As well as banks of MWPCs and a hadron calorimeter, it featured a liquid [argon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argon "Argon") electromagnetic calorimeter with exceptional spatial and energy resolution. NA31 showed that direct CP violation is real.

Steinberger worked on the [ALEPH experiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ALEPH_experiment "ALEPH experiment") at the [Large Electron–Positron Collider](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Large_Electron–Positron_Collider "Large Electron–Positron Collider") (LEP), where he served as the experiment's spokesperson. Among the ALEPH experiment's initial accomplishments was the precise measurement of the number of families of [leptons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lepton "Lepton") and [quarks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quark "Quark") in the [Standard Model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_Model "Standard Model") through the measurement of the decays of the [Z boson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Z_boson "Z boson").

He retired from CERN in 1986, and went on to become a professor at the [Scuola Normale Superiore di Pisa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scuola_Normale_Superiore_di_Pisa "Scuola Normale Superiore di Pisa") in Italy. He continued his association with the CERN laboratory through his visits into his 90s.

### Nobel Prize

Steinberger was awarded the [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics") in 1988, "for the neutrino beam method and the demonstration of the doublet structure of the leptons through the discovery of the muon neutrino". He shared the prize with [Leon M. Lederman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leon_M._Lederman "Leon M. Lederman") and [Melvin Schwartz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melvin_Schwartz "Melvin Schwartz"); at the time of the research, all three experimenters were at [Columbia University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia_University "Columbia University").

The experiment used charged [pion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pion "Pion") beams generated with the Alternating Gradient Synchrotron at [Brookhaven National Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brookhaven_National_Laboratory "Brookhaven National Laboratory"). The pions decayed to [muons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muons "Muons") which were detected in front of a steel wall; the [neutrinos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutrinos "Neutrinos") were detected in spark chambers installed behind the wall. The coincidence of muons and neutrinos demonstrated that a second kind of neutrino was created in association with muons. Subsequent experiments proved this neutrino to be distinct from the first kind (electron-type). Steinberger, Lederman and Schwartz published their work in *[Physical Review Letters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_Review_Letters "Physical Review Letters")* in 1962.

He gave his Nobel medal to [New Trier High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Trier_High_School "New Trier High School") in [Winnetka, Illinois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winnetka,_Illinois "Winnetka, Illinois") (USA), of which he was an alumnus.

He was also awarded the [National Medal of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Medal_of_Science "National Medal of Science") in 1988, by the then US president, [Ronald Reagan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Reagan "Ronald Reagan") and was the recipient of the [Matteucci Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matteucci_Medal "Matteucci Medal") in 1990, from the [Italian Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_Academy_of_Sciences "Italian Academy of Sciences").

## Personal life

Steinberger's first marriage to Joan Beauregard ended in a divorce, after which he married his former student, biologist Cynthia Alff. He had four children, two from each of his marriages. His son [Ned Steinberger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ned_Steinberger "Ned Steinberger") is the founder of the [eponymous company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steinberger "Steinberger") for headless guitars and basses, and his daughter [Julia Steinberger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julia_Steinberger "Julia Steinberger") is an ecological economist at the [University of Lausanne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Lausanne "University of Lausanne"). As an [atheist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atheist "Atheist") and a [humanist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humanism "Humanism"), Steinberger was a Humanist Laureate in the [International Academy of Humanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Academy_of_Humanism "International Academy of Humanism"). In his own words, he is noted to have enjoyed tennis, mountaineering and sailing.

In the 1980s Steinberger resumed relations with his native town Bad Kissingen. He often visited Bad Kissingen after that. The school he had attended there was named *Jack-Steinberger-Gymnasium* in 2001. In 2006 Steinberger was made honorary citizen of Bad Kissingen. "I feel welcome in Bad Kissingen. This is my hometown and I was raised there. I feel as a German again now" he told the Bavarian broadcasting company *[Bayerischer Rundfunk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayerischer_Rundfunk "Bayerischer Rundfunk")* in 2013.

He died on December 12, 2020, at his home in [Geneva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geneva "Geneva"). He was aged 99.
