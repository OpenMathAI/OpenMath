**Charles Glover Barkla** (7 June 1877 – 23 October 1944) was a British [physicist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physicist "Physicist") who received the [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics") in 1917 for his discovery of [characteristic X-rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Characteristic_X-ray "Characteristic X-ray").

## Biography

Charles Glover Barkla was born on 7 June 1877 in [Widnes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Widnes "Widnes"), England, the son of John Martin Barkla, originally from [Wendron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wendron "Wendron"), [Cornwall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornwall "Cornwall"), a secretary for the Atlas Chemical Company, and Sarah Glover.

Barkla studied at the [Liverpool Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liverpool_Institute_for_Boys "Liverpool Institute for Boys"), and then proceeded to University College Liverpool (now the [University of Liverpool](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Liverpool "University of Liverpool")) with a County Council Scholarship and a Bibby Scholarship. He initially studied mathematics, but later specialised in physics under [Oliver Lodge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oliver_Lodge "Oliver Lodge"). During the absence of Lodge due to ill health, Barkla replaced him in lectures. In 1898, he graduated with [First Class Honours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Class_Honours "First Class Honours") in Physics, and received his [master's degree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master's_degree "Master's degree") the following year.

In 1899, Barkla was admitted to [Trinity College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College,_Cambridge "Trinity College, Cambridge"), with an [1851 Research Fellowship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1851_Research_Fellowship "1851 Research Fellowship") to study under [J. J. Thomson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._J._Thomson "J. J. Thomson") in the [Cavendish Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cavendish_Laboratory "Cavendish Laboratory"). During his first two years at Cambridge, under the direction of Thomson, he studied the velocity of [electromagnetic waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_wave "Electromagnetic wave") along wires of different widths and materials. After a year and a half at Trinity College, his love of music led him to transfer to [King's College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King's_College,_Cambridge "King's College, Cambridge"), in order to sing in [its choir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Choir_of_King's_College,_Cambridge "Choir of King's College, Cambridge"). His voice was of remarkable beauty and his solo performances were always fully attended.

In 1902, Barkla returned to the University of Liverpool as an Oliver Lodge Fellow. In 1909, he was appointed Wheatstone Professor of Physics at the [University of London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_London "University of London"). In 1913, he became Professor of Natural Philosophy at the [University of Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Edinburgh "University of Edinburgh"), where he remained until his death.

## Research

In 1903, Barkla studied secondary [X-rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray "X-ray") from gases radiated by other X-rays, developing a new experimental setup. This topic was relevant to the question of whether X-rays were indeed a type of [electromagnetic radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_radiation "Electromagnetic radiation") as many physicists suspected, because [Lionel Wilberforce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lionel_Wilberforce "Lionel Wilberforce") proposed to use these secondary rays to generate tertiary ones and prove the existence of polarisation by rotating the detecting part of his experimental apparatus. Tertiary radiation was too feeble to measure, so he assembled a slightly different setup. Using his new setup, he was able to prove that X-rays can indeed be polarised and are therefore electromagnetic. He published a brief summary of his findings in *[Nature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nature_(journal) "Nature (journal)")* in March 1904 and a more detailed account in the *[Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophical_Transactions_of_the_Royal_Society "Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society")* in 1905.

Barkla made significant progress in developing and refining the laws of [X-ray scattering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray_scattering "X-ray scattering"), [X-ray spectroscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray_spectroscopy "X-ray spectroscopy"), the principles governing the transmission of X-rays through matter, and especially the principles of the excitation of secondary X-rays.

Barkla proposed the J-phenomenon as a hypothetical form of X-ray behaviour similar to [X-ray fluorescence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray_fluorescence "X-ray fluorescence"). However, other scientists were not persuaded that this was a different mechanism from other known effects such as [Compton scattering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compton_scattering "Compton scattering"), so the theory was not successful.

## Personal life and death

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/01/Hermitage_of_Braid%2C_Edinburgh.jpg/330px-Hermitage_of_Braid%2C_Edinburgh.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Hermitage_of_Braid,_Edinburgh.jpg)

Hermitage of Braid, Edinburgh

In 1907, Barkla married Mary Esther Cowell, with whom he had two sons and one daughter.

A religious man, Barkla was a [Methodist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methodist "Methodist") and considered his work to be "part of the quest for God, the Creator."

From 1922 to 1938, Barkla lived at Hermitage of Braid in [Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edinburgh "Edinburgh").

Barkla died at his home in Edinburgh on 23 October 1944 at the age of 67.

## Commemoration

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/cf/Plaque_to_C_G_Barkla%2C_Hermitage_of_Braid.png/330px-Plaque_to_C_G_Barkla%2C_Hermitage_of_Braid.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Plaque_to_C_G_Barkla,_Hermitage_of_Braid.png)

Plaque to C. G. Barkla, Hermitage of Braid

[Barkla crater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barkla_(crater) "Barkla (crater)") on the Moon is named in his honour.

A [plaque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commemorative_plaque "Commemorative plaque") was placed on Hermitage of Braid to commemorate Barkla's 16-year stay there. A plaque has also been installed in the vicinity of the Canongate, near the Faculty of Education Buildings at the [University of Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Edinburgh "University of Edinburgh").

Additionally, a lecture theatre at the [University of Liverpool](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Liverpool "University of Liverpool")'s Physics department, as well as a Biophysics Laboratory in the Biological Science Department, are named after him. In 2012, a [gritter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gritter "Gritter") in Barkla's hometown of [Widnes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Widnes "Widnes") was named in his honour, following a competition run by the local newspaper. In Widnes, they have a retirement housing complex named the Barkla Fields.
