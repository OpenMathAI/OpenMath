**Paul Adrien Maurice Dirac** ([/dɪ.ˈræk/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English"), [*dih-RAK*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:Pronunciation_respelling_key "Help:Pronunciation respelling key"); 8 August 1902 – 20 October 1984) was an English theoretical [physicist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physicist "Physicist") who is considered to be one of the founders of [quantum mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_mechanics "Quantum mechanics"). Dirac laid the foundations for both [quantum electrodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_electrodynamics "Quantum electrodynamics") and [quantum field theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_field_theory "Quantum field theory"), coining the former term. He was [Lucasian Professor of Mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucasian_Professor_of_Mathematics "Lucasian Professor of Mathematics") at the [University of Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Cambridge "University of Cambridge") from 1932 to 1969, and a professor of physics at [Florida State University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_State_University "Florida State University") from 1970 to 1984. Dirac shared the 1933 [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics") with [Erwin Schrödinger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erwin_Schrödinger "Erwin Schrödinger") "for the discovery of new productive forms of [atomic theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_theory "Atomic theory")."

Dirac graduated from the [University of Bristol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Bristol "University of Bristol") with a [Bachelor of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_Science "Bachelor of Science") in Electrical Engineering in 1921, and a [Bachelor of Arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_Arts "Bachelor of Arts") in Mathematics in 1923. Dirac then graduated from [St John's College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_John's_College,_Cambridge "St John's College, Cambridge"), with a [Doctor of Philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Philosophy "Doctor of Philosophy") in Physics in 1926, writing the first ever thesis on quantum mechanics.

Dirac formulated the [Dirac equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirac_equation "Dirac equation"), one of the most important results in physics, in 1928. The equation connected [special relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_relativity "Special relativity") and quantum mechanics and predicted the existence of [antimatter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antimatter "Antimatter"). The equation has been regarded by some physicists as "the real seed of modern physics". Dirac wrote a famous paper in 1931, which further predicted the existence of antimatter. Dirac also contributed greatly to the reconciliation of [general relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_relativity "General relativity") with quantum mechanics. He contributed to [Fermi–Dirac statistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermi–Dirac_statistics "Fermi–Dirac statistics"), which describes the behaviour of [fermions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermion "Fermion"), particles with half-integer [spin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spin_(physics) "Spin (physics)"). His 1930 monograph, [*The* *Principles of Quantum Mechanics*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Principles_of_Quantum_Mechanics "The Principles of Quantum Mechanics"), is one of the most influential texts on the subject.

In 1987, [Abdus Salam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abdus_Salam "Abdus Salam") declared that "Dirac was undoubtedly one of the greatest physicists of this or any century ... No man except [Einstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Einstein "Albert Einstein") has had such a decisive influence, in so short a time, on the course of physics in this century." In 1995, [Stephen Hawking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Hawking "Stephen Hawking") stated that "Dirac has done more than anyone this century, with the exception of Einstein, to advance physics and change our picture of the universe." [Antonino Zichichi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonino_Zichichi "Antonino Zichichi") asserted that Dirac had a greater impact on modern physics than Einstein while [Stanley Deser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanley_Deser "Stanley Deser") remarked that "We all stand on Dirac's shoulders."

## Early life

Paul Adrien Maurice Dirac was born on 8 August 1902 at his parents' home in [Bristol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol "Bristol"), England, and grew up in the [Bishopston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bishopston,_Bristol "Bishopston, Bristol") area of the city. His father, Charles Adrien Ladislas Dirac, was an immigrant from [Saint-Maurice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint-Maurice,_Switzerland "Saint-Maurice, Switzerland"), Switzerland, of French descent, who worked in Bristol as a French teacher. His mother, Florence Hannah Holten, was born to a [Cornish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornish_people "Cornish people") [Methodist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methodism "Methodism") family in [Liskeard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liskeard "Liskeard"), [Cornwall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornwall "Cornwall"). She was named after [Florence Nightingale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florence_Nightingale "Florence Nightingale") by her father, a ship's captain, who had met Nightingale while he was a soldier during the [Crimean War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crimean_War "Crimean War"). His mother moved to Bristol as a young woman, where she worked as a librarian at the [Bristol Central Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Central_Library "Bristol Central Library"); despite this she still considered her identity to be Cornish rather than English. Paul had a younger sister, Béatrice Isabelle Marguerite, known as Betty, and an older brother, Reginald Charles Félix, known as Felix, who died by suicide in March 1925. Dirac later recalled: "My parents were terribly distressed. I didn't know they cared so much ... I never knew that parents were supposed to care for their children, but from then on I knew."

Charles and the children were officially Swiss nationals until they became [naturalised](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naturalised "Naturalised") on 22 October 1919. Dirac's father was strict and authoritarian, although he disapproved of corporal punishment. Dirac had a strained relationship with his father, so much so that after his father's death, Dirac wrote, "I feel much freer now, and I am my own man." Charles forced his children to speak to him only in French so that they might learn the language. When Dirac found that he could not express what he wanted to say in French, he chose to remain silent.

## Education

Dirac was educated first at [Bishop Road Primary School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bishop_Road_Primary_School "Bishop Road Primary School") and then at the all-boys [Merchant Venturers'](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Society_of_Merchant_Venturers "Society of Merchant Venturers") Technical College (later [Cotham School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cotham_School "Cotham School")), where his father was a French teacher. The school was an institution attached to the [University of Bristol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Bristol "University of Bristol"), which shared grounds and staff. It emphasised technical subjects like bricklaying, shoemaking and metalwork, and modern languages. This was unusual at a time when secondary education in Britain was still dedicated largely to the classics, and something for which Dirac would later express his gratitude. One of his peers at Bishop Road School was Archibald Leach, later famous as [Cary Grant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cary_Grant "Cary Grant").

Dirac studied [electrical engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_engineering "Electrical engineering") on a City of Bristol University Scholarship at the University of Bristol's engineering faculty, which was co-located with the Merchant Venturers' Technical College. Shortly before he completed his degree in 1921, he sat for the entrance examination for [St John's College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_John's_College,_Cambridge "St John's College, Cambridge"). He passed and was awarded a £70 scholarship, but this fell short of the amount of money required to live and study at Cambridge. Despite having graduated with a [first class honours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_class_honours "First class honours") [B.Sc.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B.Sc. "B.Sc.") in electrical engineering, the economic climate of the [post-war depression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post–World_War_I_recession "Post–World War I recession") was such that he was unable to find work as an engineer. Instead, he took up an offer to study for a [B.A.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B.A. "B.A.") in mathematics at the University of Bristol free of charge. He was permitted to skip the first year of the course owing to his engineering degree. Under the influence of Peter Fraser, whom Dirac called the best mathematics teacher, he had the most interest in [projective geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_geometry "Projective geometry"), and began applying it to [Hermann Minkowski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann_Minkowski "Hermann Minkowski")'s geometrical version of [special relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_relativity "Special relativity").

In 1923, Dirac graduated, once again with first class honours, and received a £140 scholarship from the [Department of Scientific and Industrial Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Department_of_Scientific_and_Industrial_Research_(United_Kingdom) "Department of Scientific and Industrial Research (United Kingdom)"). Along with his £70 scholarship from St John's College, this was enough to live at Cambridge. There, Dirac pursued his interests in the theory of [general relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_relativity "General relativity"), an interest he had gained earlier as a student in Bristol, and in the nascent field of [quantum physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_physics "Quantum physics"), under the supervision of [Ralph Fowler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ralph_Fowler "Ralph Fowler"). From 1925 to 1928, he held an [1851 Research Fellowship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1851_Research_Fellowship "1851 Research Fellowship") from the [Royal Commission for the Exhibition of 1851](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Commission_for_the_Exhibition_of_1851 "Royal Commission for the Exhibition of 1851"). He completed his [Ph.D.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ph.D. "Ph.D.") in June 1926 with the first thesis on [quantum mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_mechanics "Quantum mechanics") to be submitted anywhere. He then continued his research in [Copenhagen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copenhagen "Copenhagen") and [Göttingen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Göttingen "Göttingen"). In the spring of 1929, he was a visiting professor at the [University of Wisconsin–Madison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Wisconsin–Madison "University of Wisconsin–Madison").

## Personal life

### Family

Paul and Manci Dirac in [Copenhagen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copenhagen "Copenhagen"), July 1963

In 1937, Dirac married Margit Wigner, the sister of physicist [Eugene Wigner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugene_Wigner "Eugene Wigner") and a divorcee. Dirac raised Margit's two children, Judith and [Gabriel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gabriel_Andrew_Dirac "Gabriel Andrew Dirac"), as if they were his own. Paul and Margit Dirac also had two daughters together, Mary Elizabeth and Florence Monica.

Margit, known as Manci, had visited her brother in 1934 in [Princeton, New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton,_New_Jersey "Princeton, New Jersey"), from their native Hungary and, while at dinner at the Annex Restaurant, met the "lonely-looking man at the next table". This account from a Korean physicist, [Y. S. Kim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Young_Suh_Kim "Young Suh Kim"), who met and was influenced by Dirac, also says: "It is quite fortunate for the physics community that Manci took good care of our respected Paul A. M. Dirac. Dirac published eleven papers during the period 1939–46. Dirac was able to maintain his normal research productivity only because Manci was in charge of everything else".

### Personality

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/17/Clara_Ewald_-_Paul_Dirac.jpg/250px-Clara_Ewald_-_Paul_Dirac.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Clara_Ewald_-_Paul_Dirac.jpg)

Portrait of Paul Dirac by [Clara Ewald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clara_Ewald "Clara Ewald"), 1939

Dirac was regarded by his friends and colleagues as unusual in character. In a 1926 letter to [Paul Ehrenfest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Ehrenfest "Paul Ehrenfest"), [Albert Einstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Einstein "Albert Einstein") wrote of a Dirac paper, "I am toiling over Dirac. This balancing on the dizzying path between genius and madness is awful." In another letter concerning the [Compton effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compton_scattering "Compton scattering") he wrote, "I don't understand the details of Dirac at all."
Dirac was known among his colleagues for his precise and taciturn nature. His colleagues in Cambridge jokingly defined a unit called a "dirac", which was one word per hour. When [Niels Bohr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niels_Bohr "Niels Bohr") complained that he did not know how to finish a sentence in a scientific article he was writing, Dirac replied, "I was taught at school never to start a sentence without knowing the end of it." He criticised the physicist [J. Robert Oppenheimer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._Robert_Oppenheimer "J. Robert Oppenheimer")'s interest in poetry: "The aim of science is to make difficult things understandable in a simpler way; the aim of poetry is to state simple things in an incomprehensible way. The two are incompatible." Bohr called Dirac "a complete logical genius" and also the "strangest man" who had ever visited his Institute.

Dirac himself wrote in his diary during his postgraduate years that he concentrated solely on his research, and stopped only on Sunday when he took long strolls alone.

An anecdote recounted in a review of the 2009 biography tells of [Werner Heisenberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Werner_Heisenberg "Werner Heisenberg") and Dirac travelling with a rigid airship to a conference in Japan in August 1929. "Both still in their twenties, and unmarried, they made an odd couple. Heisenberg was a ladies' man who constantly flirted and danced, while Dirac—'an Edwardian geek', as biographer [Graham Farmelo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graham_Farmelo "Graham Farmelo") puts it—suffered agonies if forced into any kind of socializing or small talk. 'Why do you dance?' Dirac asked his companion. 'When there are nice girls, it is a pleasure,' Heisenberg replied. Dirac pondered this notion, then blurted out: 'But, Heisenberg, how do you know beforehand that the girls are nice?'"

Margit Dirac told both [George Gamow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Gamow "George Gamow") and Anton Capri in the 1960s that her husband had said to a house visitor, "Allow me to present Wigner's sister, who is now my wife."

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f2/Paul_Dirac_and_Richard_Feynman_at_Jab%C5%82onna_1962.png/250px-Paul_Dirac_and_Richard_Feynman_at_Jab%C5%82onna_1962.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Paul_Dirac_and_Richard_Feynman_at_Jabłonna_1962.png)

Paul Dirac and Richard Feynman at [Jabłonna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jabłonna_Palace "Jabłonna Palace"), Poland. July 1962.

Another story told of Dirac is that when he first met the young [Richard Feynman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Feynman "Richard Feynman") at a conference, he said after a long silence, "I have an equation. Do you have one too?"

After he presented a lecture at a conference, one colleague raised his hand and said: "I don't understand the equation on the top-right-hand corner of the blackboard". After a long silence, the moderator asked Dirac if he wanted to answer the question, to which Dirac replied: "That was not a question, it was a comment."

Dirac was also noted for his personal modesty. He called the equation for the [time evolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_evolution "Time evolution") of a quantum-mechanical operator, which he was the first to write down, the "Heisenberg equation of motion". Most physicists speak of [Fermi–Dirac statistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermi–Dirac_statistics "Fermi–Dirac statistics") for half-integer-spin particles ([fermions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermions "Fermions")) and [Bose–Einstein statistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bose–Einstein_statistics "Bose–Einstein statistics") for integer-spin particles ([bosons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bosons "Bosons")). While lecturing later in life, Dirac always insisted on calling the former "Fermi statistics". He referred to the latter as "Bose statistics" for reasons, he explained, of "symmetry".

### Philosophy of physics

While visiting [Moscow State University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moscow_State_University "Moscow State University") in 1956, Dirac was asked to summarize his philosophy of physics. He wrote on the blackboard "Physical laws should have [mathematical beauty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_beauty "Mathematical beauty")." As is traditional with inscriptions left by distinguished visitors, the [phrase](http://theorphys.phys.msu.ru/about/dirac.jpg) has never been erased.

Dirac repeatedly emphasized the role of mathematical beauty in physics. For Dirac, mathematical beauty was both a quality of nature and a useful methodological guide for the physicist. When trying to mathematically formulate a law of nature, he thought that physicists should aim for beauty. When evaluating whether a theory should be accepted, he thought that mathematical beauty could and did play a role. For example, in a 1939 lecture, he argued that the mathematically beautiful statement of the [general theory of relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_theory_of_relativity "General theory of relativity") was one of the reasons it was accepted.

Dirac was famously not bothered by [issues of interpretation in quantum theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interpretations_of_quantum_mechanics "Interpretations of quantum mechanics"). In fact, in a paper published in a book in his honour, he wrote: "The interpretation of quantum mechanics has been dealt with by many authors, and I do not want to discuss it here. I want to deal with more fundamental things."

### Views on religion

Werner Heisenberg recollected a conversation among young participants at the 1927 [Solvay Conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solvay_Conference "Solvay Conference") about Einstein and [Max Planck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Planck "Max Planck")'s views on religion between [Wolfgang Pauli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolfgang_Pauli "Wolfgang Pauli"), Heisenberg and Dirac. Dirac's contribution was a criticism of the political purpose of religion, which Bohr regarded as quite lucid when hearing it from Heisenberg later. Heisenberg's view was tolerant. Pauli, raised as a Catholic, had kept silent after some initial remarks, but when finally he was asked for his opinion, said: "Well, our friend Dirac has got a religion and its guiding principle is 'There is no God, and Paul Dirac is His prophet.'" Everybody, including Dirac, burst into laughter.

Later in life, in an article mentioning God that appeared in the May 1963 edition of *[Scientific American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_American "Scientific American")*, Dirac wrote:

> It seems to be one of the fundamental features of nature that fundamental [physical laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_law "Physical law") are described in terms of a [mathematical theory of great beauty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_theory_of_great_beauty "Mathematical theory of great beauty") and power, needing quite a high standard of mathematics for one to understand it. You may wonder: Why is nature constructed along these lines? One can only answer that our present knowledge seems to show that nature is so constructed. We simply have to accept it. One could perhaps describe the situation by saying that God is a mathematician of a very high order, and He used very advanced mathematics in constructing the universe. Our feeble attempts at mathematics enable us to understand a bit of the universe, and as we proceed to develop higher and higher mathematics we can hope to understand the universe better.

In 1971, at a conference meeting, Dirac described the possibilities for scientifically answering the question of God. Dirac explained that

> ... if physical laws are such that to start off life involves an excessively small chance so that it will not be reasonable to suppose that life would have started just by blind chance, then there must be a god,... On the other hand, if life can start very easily and does not need any divine influence, then I will say that there is no god.

## Research

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/2e/DIAS_1942_photo.jpg/250px-DIAS_1942_photo.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:DIAS_1942_photo.jpg)

Dirac discovered the [relativistic equation for the electron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirac_equation "Dirac equation"), which now bears his name. The remarkable notion of an antiparticle to each fermion particle – e.g. the positron as antiparticle to the electron – stems from his equation. He is credited as being the one to create [quantum field theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_field_theory "Quantum field theory"), which underlies all theoretical work on sub-atomic or "elementary" particles today, work that is fundamental to our understanding of the forces of nature, alongside creating quantum electrodynamics and coining the term. He proposed and investigated the concept of a [magnetic monopole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_monopole "Magnetic monopole"), an object not yet known empirically, as a means of bringing even greater symmetry to [James Clerk Maxwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Clerk_Maxwell "James Clerk Maxwell")'s equations of [electromagnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetism "Electromagnetism"). Dirac also coined the terms "[fermion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermion "Fermion")" (particles with half-integer spin) and "[boson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boson "Boson")" (particles with whole-integer spin).

Throughout his career, Dirac was motivated by the principles of [mathematical beauty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_beauty "Mathematical beauty"), with [Peter Goddard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Goddard_(physicist) "Peter Goddard (physicist)") stating that "Dirac cited mathematical beauty as the ultimate criterion for selecting the way forward in theoretical physics". Dirac was recognised for being mathematically gifted, as during his time in university, academics had affirmed that Dirac had an "ability of the highest order in mathematical physics", with [Ebenezer Cunningham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ebenezer_Cunningham "Ebenezer Cunningham") stating that Dirac was "quite the most original student I have met in the subject of mathematical physics". Therefore, Dirac was known for his "astounding physical intuition combined with the ability to invent new mathematics to create new physics". During his career, Dirac made numerous important contributions to mathematical subjects, including the [Dirac delta function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirac_delta_function "Dirac delta function"), [Dirac algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirac_algebra "Dirac algebra") and the [Dirac operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirac_operator "Dirac operator").

### Quantum theory

Dirac's first step into a new quantum theory was taken late in September 1925. [Ralph Fowler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ralph_Fowler "Ralph Fowler"), his research supervisor, had received a proof copy of an [exploratory paper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Über_quantentheoretischer_Umdeutung "Über quantentheoretischer Umdeutung") by [Werner Heisenberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Werner_Heisenberg "Werner Heisenberg") in the framework of the old quantum theory of Bohr and [Sommerfeld](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arnold_Sommerfeld "Arnold Sommerfeld"). Heisenberg leaned heavily on Bohr's correspondence principle but changed the equations so that they involved directly observable quantities, leading to the [matrix formulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_mechanics "Matrix mechanics") of quantum mechanics. Fowler sent Heisenberg's paper on to Dirac, who was on vacation in Bristol, asking him to look into this paper carefully.

Dirac's attention was drawn to a mysterious mathematical relationship, at first sight unintelligible, that Heisenberg had established. Several weeks later, back in Cambridge, Dirac suddenly recognised that this mathematical form had the same structure as the [Poisson brackets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poisson_bracket "Poisson bracket") that occur in the [classical dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_mechanics "Classical mechanics") of particle motion. At the time, his memory of Poisson brackets was rather vague, but he found [E. T. Whittaker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E._T._Whittaker "E. T. Whittaker")'s *[Analytical Dynamics of Particles and Rigid Bodies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytical_Dynamics_of_Particles_and_Rigid_Bodies "Analytical Dynamics of Particles and Rigid Bodies")* illuminating. From his new understanding, he developed a quantum theory based on [non-commuting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative_property "Commutative property") dynamical variables. This led him to the most profound and significant general formulation of quantum mechanics to date. His novel formulation using [Dirac brackets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirac_bracket "Dirac bracket") allowed him to obtain the [quantisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantization_(physics) "Quantization (physics)") rules in a [novel and more illuminating manner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canonical_quantization "Canonical quantization"). For this work, published in 1926, Dirac received a PhD from Cambridge.

### Fermi–Dirac statistics

Shortly after [Wolfgang Pauli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolfgang_Pauli "Wolfgang Pauli") proposed his [Pauli exclusion principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pauli_exclusion_principle "Pauli exclusion principle") (that two electrons cannot occupy the same quantum energy level), [Enrico Fermi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enrico_Fermi "Enrico Fermi") and Dirac both realized the principle would dramatically alter the statistical mechanics of electron systems. This work became the basis for [Fermi–Dirac statistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermi–Dirac_statistics "Fermi–Dirac statistics"). This applies to systems consisting of many identical [spin-1/2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spin-1/2 "Spin-1/2") particles, or [fermions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermions "Fermions") (i.e. that obey the Pauli exclusion principle), e.g. electrons in solids and liquids, and importantly to the field of conduction in [semiconductors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiconductor "Semiconductor").

### Dirac equation

In 1928, building on 2×2 spin matrices which he purported to have discovered independently of [Wolfgang Pauli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolfgang_Pauli "Wolfgang Pauli")'s work on non-relativistic [spin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spin_(physics) "Spin (physics)") systems (Dirac told [Abraham Pais](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraham_Pais "Abraham Pais"), "I believe I got these [matrices] independently of Pauli and possibly Pauli got these independently of me."), he proposed the [Dirac equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirac_equation "Dirac equation") as a [relativistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_relativity "Special relativity") [equation of motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equation_of_motion "Equation of motion") for the [wave function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_function "Wave function") of the [electron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron "Electron"). This work led Dirac to predict the existence of the [positron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positron "Positron"), the electron's [antiparticle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antiparticle "Antiparticle"), which he interpreted in terms of what came to be called the *[Dirac sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirac_sea "Dirac sea")*. The positron was observed by [Carl Anderson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_David_Anderson "Carl David Anderson") in 1932. Dirac's equation also contributed to explaining the origin of [quantum spin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spin_(physics) "Spin (physics)") as a relativistic phenomenon.

The necessity of [fermions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermion "Fermion") (matter) being created and destroyed in [Enrico Fermi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enrico_Fermi "Enrico Fermi")'s 1934 theory of [beta decay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beta_decay "Beta decay") led to a reinterpretation of Dirac's equation as a "classical" [field equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_equation "Field equation") for any [point particle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_particle "Point particle") of spin *[ħ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planck_constant "Planck constant")*/2, itself subject to quantisation conditions involving [anti-commutators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anticommutativity "Anticommutativity"). Thus reinterpreted, in 1934 by [Werner Heisenberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Werner_Heisenberg "Werner Heisenberg"), as a (quantum) field equation accurately describing all elementary matter particles – today [quarks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quark "Quark") and [leptons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lepton "Lepton") – this [Dirac field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirac_field "Dirac field") equation is as central to theoretical physics as the [Maxwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxwell_equations "Maxwell equations"), [Yang–Mills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yang–Mills_theory "Yang–Mills theory") and [Einstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_relativity "General relativity") field equations. Dirac is regarded as the founder of [quantum electrodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_electrodynamics "Quantum electrodynamics"), being the first to use that term. He also introduced the idea of [vacuum polarisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacuum_polarisation "Vacuum polarisation") in the early 1930s. This work was key to the development of quantum mechanics by the next generation of theorists, in particular [Julian Schwinger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julian_Schwinger "Julian Schwinger"), [Richard Feynman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Feynman "Richard Feynman"), [Sin-Itiro Tomonaga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sin-Itiro_Tomonaga "Sin-Itiro Tomonaga") and [Freeman Dyson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freeman_Dyson "Freeman Dyson") in their formulation of quantum electrodynamics.

Dirac's [*The* *Principles of Quantum Mechanics*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Principles_of_Quantum_Mechanics "The Principles of Quantum Mechanics"), published in 1930, is a landmark in the [history of science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_science "History of science"). It quickly became one of the standard textbooks on the subject and is still used today. In that book, Dirac incorporated the previous work of Heisenberg on [matrix mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_mechanics "Matrix mechanics") and of [Erwin Schrödinger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erwin_Schrödinger "Erwin Schrödinger") on [wave mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schrödinger_equation "Schrödinger equation") into a single mathematical formalism that associates measurable quantities to operators acting on the [Hilbert space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilbert_space "Hilbert space") of vectors that describe the state of a [physical system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_system "Physical system"). The book also introduced the [Dirac delta function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirac_delta_function "Dirac delta function"). Following his 1939 article, he also included the [bra–ket notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bra–ket_notation "Bra–ket notation") in the third edition of his book, thereby contributing to its universal use nowadays.

### Quantum electrodynamics

Dirac's [quantum electrodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_electrodynamics "Quantum electrodynamics") (QED) included terms with infinite [self-energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-energy "Self-energy"). A workaround known as [renormalisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renormalisation "Renormalisation") was developed, but Dirac never accepted this. "I must say that I am very dissatisfied with the situation", he said in 1975, "because this so-called 'good theory' does involve neglecting infinities which appear in its equations, neglecting them in an arbitrary way. This is just not sensible mathematics. Sensible mathematics involves neglecting a quantity when it is small – not neglecting it just because it is infinitely great and you do not want it!" His refusal to accept renormalisation resulted in his work on the subject moving increasingly out of the mainstream. [Shin'ichirō Tomonaga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shin'ichirō_Tomonaga "Shin'ichirō Tomonaga"), [Schwinger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julian_Schwinger "Julian Schwinger") and [Feynman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Feynman "Richard Feynman") mastered this approach, producing a QED with unprecedented accuracy, resulting in formal recognition by being awarded the 1965 Nobel Prize in Physics.

In the 1950s in his search for a better QED, Paul Dirac developed the Hamiltonian theory of constraints based on lectures that he delivered at the 1949 [International Mathematical Congress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Congress_of_Mathematicians "International Congress of Mathematicians") in Canada. Dirac had also solved the problem of putting the [Schwinger–Tomonaga equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schwinger–Tomonaga_equation "Schwinger–Tomonaga equation") into the Schrödinger representation and given explicit expressions for the [scalar meson field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scalar_field "Scalar field") ([spin zero pion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pion "Pion") or [pseudoscalar meson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoscalar_meson "Pseudoscalar meson")), the vector meson field (spin one rho meson), and the electromagnetic field (spin one massless boson, photon).

### Magnetic monopoles

In 1931, Dirac proposed that the existence of a single magnetic monopole in the universe would suffice to explain the quantisation of electrical charge.
No such monopole has been detected, despite numerous attempts and preliminary claims. (See also: [Searches for magnetic monopoles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_monopole#Searches_for_magnetic_monopoles "Magnetic monopole").)

### War work

Dirac contributed to the [Tube Alloys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tube_Alloys "Tube Alloys") project, the British programme to research and construct atomic bombs during [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II"). Dirac made fundamental contributions to the process of [uranium enrichment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enriched_uranium "Enriched uranium") and the [gas centrifuge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas_centrifuge "Gas centrifuge"). This work was deemed to be "probably the most important theoretical result in centrifuge technology".

### Gravity

Dirac quantised the gravitational field. His work laid the foundations for [canonical quantum gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canonical_quantum_gravity "Canonical quantum gravity").
In his 1959 lecture at the [Lindau Meetings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lindau_Nobel_Laureate_Meetings "Lindau Nobel Laureate Meetings"), Dirac discussed why [gravitational waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_wave "Gravitational wave") have "physical significance". Dirac predicted gravitational waves would have well defined energy density in 1964. Dirac reintroduced the term "[graviton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graviton "Graviton")" in a number of lectures in 1959, noting that the energy of the gravitational field should come in quanta.

### Cosmology

Dirac contributed to [cosmology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmology "Cosmology"), putting forth his [large numbers hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirac_large_numbers_hypothesis "Dirac large numbers hypothesis").

### String theory

Dirac is seen as having anticipated [string theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/String_theory "String theory"), with his work on the [Dirac membrane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirac_membrane "Dirac membrane") and [Dirac–Born–Infeld action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Born–Infeld_model "Born–Infeld model"), both of which he proposed in a 1962 paper, along with other contributions. He also developed a general theory of the quantum field with dynamical constraints, which forms the basis of the [gauge theories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauge_theory "Gauge theory") and [superstring theories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superstring_theory "Superstring theory") of today.

### Other work

Dirac wrote an influential paper in 1933 regarding the [Lagrangian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagrangian_(physics) "Lagrangian (physics)") in quantum mechanics. The paper served as the basis for [Julian Schwinger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julian_Schwinger "Julian Schwinger") and his [quantum action principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schwinger's_quantum_action_principle "Schwinger's quantum action principle"), and laid the foundations for [Richard Feynman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Feynman "Richard Feynman")'s development of a completely new approach to quantum mechanics, the [path integral formulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Path_integral_formulation "Path integral formulation").

In a 1963 paper, Dirac initiated the study of field theory on [anti-de Sitter space (AdS)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-de_Sitter_space "Anti-de Sitter space"). The paper contains the mathematics of combining special relativity with the quantum mechanics of quarks inside hadrons, and lays the foundations of [two-mode squeezed states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Squeezed_coherent_state "Squeezed coherent state") that are essential to [modern quantum optics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_optics "Quantum optics"), though Dirac did not realize it at the time. Dirac previously worked on AdS during the 1930s, publishing a paper in 1935.

In 1930, [Victor Weisskopf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victor_Weisskopf "Victor Weisskopf") and [Eugene Wigner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugene_Wigner "Eugene Wigner") published their famous and now standard calculation of spontaneous radiation emission in atomic and molecular physics. Remarkably, in a letter to [Niels Bohr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niels_Bohr "Niels Bohr") in February 1927, Dirac had come to the same calculation, but he did not publish it.

In 1938, Dirac renormalized the mass in the theory of Abraham-Lorentz electron, leading to the [Abraham–Lorentz–Dirac force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraham–Lorentz_force#Abraham–Lorentz–Dirac_force "Abraham–Lorentz force"), which is the relativistic-classical electron model; however, this model has solutions that suggest force increase exponentially with time.

[Fermi's golden rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermi's_golden_rule "Fermi's golden rule"), the formula for computing quantum transitions in time dependent systems, declared a "golden rule" by [Enrico Fermi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enrico_Fermi "Enrico Fermi"), was derived by Dirac. Dirac was the one to initiate the development of [time-dependent perturbation theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perturbation_theory_(quantum_mechanics)#Time-dependent_perturbation_theory "Perturbation theory (quantum mechanics)") in his early work on semi-classical atoms interacting with an electromagnetic field. Dirac, with [Werner Heisenberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Werner_Heisenberg "Werner Heisenberg"), [John Archibald Wheeler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Archibald_Wheeler "John Archibald Wheeler"), Richard Feynman, and [Freeman Dyson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freeman_Dyson "Freeman Dyson") ultimately developed this concept into an invaluable tool for modern physics, used in the calculation of the properties of any physical system and a wide array of phenomena.

## Career

On 19 August 1929 Dirac travelled together with [Werner Heisenberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Werner_Heisenberg "Werner Heisenberg") with the [Graf Zeppelin LZ 127](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LZ_127 "LZ 127") at its first round-the-world flight to [Tokyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo "Tokyo"), where both held lectures.

From 1932 to 1969, Dirac was [Lucasian Professor of Mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucasian_Professor_of_Mathematics "Lucasian Professor of Mathematics") at the [University of Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Cambridge "University of Cambridge"). In 1934, he conceived the [Helikon vortex isotope separation process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helikon_vortex_separation_process "Helikon vortex separation process"). In 1937, he proposed a speculative [cosmological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_cosmology "Physical cosmology") model based on the [large numbers hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirac_large_numbers_hypothesis "Dirac large numbers hypothesis"). During World War II, he conducted important theoretical work on [uranium enrichment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium_enrichment "Uranium enrichment") by [gas centrifuge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas_centrifuge "Gas centrifuge"). He introduced the [separative work unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Separative_work_units "Separative work units") (SWU) in 1941. He contributed to the [Tube Alloys project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tube_Alloys "Tube Alloys"), the British programme to research and construct atomic bombs during World War II.

### Students

Amongst his many students were [Homi J. Bhabha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homi_J._Bhabha "Homi J. Bhabha"), [Fred Hoyle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fred_Hoyle "Fred Hoyle"), [John Polkinghorne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Polkinghorne "John Polkinghorne") and [Freeman Dyson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freeman_Dyson "Freeman Dyson"). In 1930, [Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subrahmanyan_Chandrasekhar "Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar") attended Dirac's course on quantum mechanics four times, describing it as "just like a piece of music you want to hear over and over again."

## Later life

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/95/Paul_Dirac_Bust_at_FSU.jpg/250px-Paul_Dirac_Bust_at_FSU.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Paul_Dirac_Bust_at_FSU.jpg)

A bust of Paul Dirac at Florida State University

In 1969, Dirac was forced to retire from his chair at Cambridge, due to his age (67). Before his retirement, he was offered a visiting position at the [University of Miami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Miami "University of Miami") in [Coral Gables, Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coral_Gables,_Florida "Coral Gables, Florida"); he accepted, joining its newly formed [Center for Theoretical Studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_for_Theoretical_Studies,_University_of_Miami "Center for Theoretical Studies, University of Miami"). In September 1970 he also accepted a visiting professor position at [Florida State University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_State_University "Florida State University") in [Tallahassee, Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tallahassee,_Florida "Tallahassee, Florida"), and moved his family to Tallahassee. He accepted a position at FSU as a full professor in 1972.

Contemporary accounts of his time in Tallahassee describe it as happy, except that he apparently found the summer heat oppressive and liked to escape from it to Cambridge. He would walk about a mile to work each day and was fond of swimming in one of the two nearby lakes (Silver Lake and Lost Lake), and was also more sociable than he had been at the University of Cambridge, where he mostly worked at home apart from giving classes and seminars. At Florida State, he would usually eat lunch with his colleagues before taking a nap.

Dirac published over 60 papers at FSU during those last twelve years of his life, including a short book on general relativity. His last paper (1984), entitled "The inadequacies of quantum field theory," contains his final judgment on quantum field theory: "These rules of renormalisation give surprisingly, excessively good agreement with experiments. Most physicists say that these working rules are, therefore, correct. I feel that is not an adequate reason. Just because the results happen to be in agreement with observation does not prove that one's theory is correct." The paper ends with the words: "I have spent many years searching for a Hamiltonian to bring into the theory and have not yet found it. I shall continue to work on it as long as I can and other people, I hope, will follow along such lines."

In 1975, Dirac gave a series of five lectures at the [University of New South Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_New_South_Wales "University of New South Wales") which were subsequently published as a book, *Directions in Physics* (1978). He donated the royalties from this book to the university for the establishment of Dirac Lecture Series. The [Silver Dirac Medal for the Advancement of Theoretical Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirac_Medal_(UNSW) "Dirac Medal (UNSW)") is awarded by the University of New South Wales to commemorate the lecture.

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/26/Nima_visits_dirac.jpg/250px-Nima_visits_dirac.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Nima_visits_dirac.jpg)

The tombstone of Dirac and his wife in Roselawn Cemetery, [Tallahassee, Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tallahassee,_Florida "Tallahassee, Florida"). Their daughter Mary Elizabeth Dirac, who died 20 January 2007, is buried next to them

Dirac died on 20 October 1984 in [Tallahassee, Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tallahassee,_Florida "Tallahassee, Florida"), at the age of 82, and was buried at Tallahassee's Roselawn Cemetery.

## Commemoration

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/63/Dirac%27s_commemorative_marker.jpg/250px-Dirac%27s_commemorative_marker.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Dirac's_commemorative_marker.jpg)

The commemorative marker in [Westminster Abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westminster_Abbey "Westminster Abbey")

Dirac's childhood home in [Bishopston, Bristol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bishopston,_Bristol "Bishopston, Bristol"), is commemorated with a [blue plaque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_plaque "Blue plaque"), and the nearby Dirac Road is named in recognition of his links with the city of [Bristol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol "Bristol"). A commemorative stone was erected in a garden in [Saint-Maurice, Switzerland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint-Maurice,_Switzerland "Saint-Maurice, Switzerland"), the town of origin of his father's family, on 1 August 1991. On 13 November 1995 a commemorative marker, made from Burlington green [slate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slate "Slate") and inscribed with the [Dirac equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirac_equation "Dirac equation"), was unveiled in [Westminster Abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westminster_Abbey "Westminster Abbey"). The [Dean of Westminster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dean_of_Westminster "Dean of Westminster"), [Edward Carpenter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Carpenter_(priest) "Edward Carpenter (priest)"), had initially refused permission for the memorial, thinking Dirac to be anti-Christian, but was eventually (over a five-year period) persuaded to relent.

After his death, two organisations of professional physicists established annual awards in Dirac's memory. The [Institute of Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_of_Physics "Institute of Physics"), the United Kingdom's professional body for physicists, awards the [Paul Dirac Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirac_Medal_(IOP) "Dirac Medal (IOP)") for "outstanding contributions to theoretical (including mathematical and computational) physics". The first three recipients were [Stephen Hawking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Hawking "Stephen Hawking") (1987), [John Stewart Bell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Stewart_Bell "John Stewart Bell") (1988), and [Roger Penrose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roger_Penrose "Roger Penrose") (1989). Since 1985, the [International Centre for Theoretical Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Centre_for_Theoretical_Physics "International Centre for Theoretical Physics") awards the [Dirac Medal of the ICTP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirac_Medal_(ICTP) "Dirac Medal (ICTP)") each year on Dirac's birthday (8 August).

The Dirac–Hellman Award at [Florida State University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florida_State_University "Florida State University") was endowed by Bruce P. Hellman in 1997 to reward outstanding work in theoretical physics by FSU researchers. The Paul A.M. Dirac Science Library at Florida State University, which Manci opened in December 1989, is named in his honour, and his papers are held there. Outside is a statue of him by Gabriella Bollobás. The street on which the [National High Magnetic Field Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_High_Magnetic_Field_Laboratory "National High Magnetic Field Laboratory") in Innovation Park of Tallahassee, Florida, is located is named Paul Dirac Drive. As well as in his hometown of Bristol, there is also a road named after him, Dirac Place, in [Didcot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Didcot "Didcot"), Oxfordshire. The Dirac-Higgs Science Centre in Bristol is also named in his honour.

The BBC named a [video codec](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_codec "Video codec"), [Dirac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirac_(video_compression_format) "Dirac (video compression format)"), in his honour. An [asteroid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/5997_Dirac "5997 Dirac") discovered in 1983 was named after Dirac. The Distributed Research utilising Advanced Computing ([DiRAC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DiRAC "DiRAC")) and [Dirac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirac_(software) "Dirac (software)") software are named in his honour.

### Praise

Dirac is widely considered to be on par with [Isaac Newton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Newton "Isaac Newton"), [James Clerk Maxwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Clerk_Maxwell "James Clerk Maxwell"), and [Albert Einstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Einstein "Albert Einstein"). Einstein wrote that to Dirac "we owe the most logically perfect presentation of [quantum mechanics]."

On the occasion of the 100th anniversary of Dirac's birth, [Richard Dalitz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Dalitz "Richard Dalitz") wrote "The influence and importance of Dirac's work have increased with the decades, and physicists use daily the concepts and equations that he developed."

In [Lev Landau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lev_Landau "Lev Landau")'s [logarithmic scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lev_Landau#Landau's_ranking_of_physicists "Lev Landau") of physicists from 0 to 5 based on productivity and genius, (0 being the highest and 5 the lowest) he ranked Dirac a 1, along with other fathers of quantum mechanics such as Schrödinger and [Werner Heisenberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Werner_Heisenberg "Werner Heisenberg").

[John Polkinghorne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Polkinghorne "John Polkinghorne") wrote: "Not only was Dirac the greatest theoretical physicist known to me personally, his purity of spirt and modesty of demeanour (he never emphasized in the slightest degree his own immense contributions to the fundamentals of the subject) made him an inspiring figure and a kind of scientific saint."

## Books

- *[The Principles of Quantum Mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Principles_of_Quantum_Mechanics "The Principles of Quantum Mechanics")* (1930): This book summarises the ideas of quantum mechanics using the modern formalism that was largely developed by Dirac himself. Towards the end of the book, he also discusses the relativistic theory of the electron (the [Dirac equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirac_equation "Dirac equation")), which was also pioneered by him. This work does not refer to any other writings then available on quantum mechanics.
- *Lectures on Quantum Mechanics* (1966): Much of this book deals with quantum mechanics in [curved space-time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curved_space-time "Curved space-time").
- *Lectures on Quantum Field Theory* (1966): This book lays down the foundations of [quantum field theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_field_theory "Quantum field theory") using the [Hamiltonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamiltonian_mechanics "Hamiltonian mechanics") formalism.
- *Spinors in Hilbert Space* (1974): This book based on lectures given in 1969 at the University of Miami deals with the basic aspects of [spinors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spinor "Spinor") starting with a real [Hilbert space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilbert_space "Hilbert space") formalism.
- *General Theory of Relativity* (1975): Based on Dirac's 1975 lectures at Florida State University, this 69-page work summarises Einstein's general theory of relativity.
