**Leon Max Lederman** (July 15, 1922 – October 3, 2018) was an American experimental physicist who received the [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics") in 1988, along with [Melvin Schwartz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melvin_Schwartz "Melvin Schwartz") and [Jack Steinberger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jack_Steinberger "Jack Steinberger"), for research on [neutrinos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutrino "Neutrino"). He also received the [Wolf Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolf_Prize_in_Physics "Wolf Prize in Physics") in 1982, along with [Martin Lewis Perl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Lewis_Perl "Martin Lewis Perl"), for research on [quarks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quark "Quark") and [leptons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lepton "Lepton"). Lederman was director emeritus of [Fermi National Accelerator Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermilab "Fermilab") (Fermilab) in [Batavia, Illinois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Batavia,_Illinois "Batavia, Illinois"). He founded the [Illinois Mathematics and Science Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illinois_Mathematics_and_Science_Academy "Illinois Mathematics and Science Academy"), in [Aurora, Illinois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aurora,_Illinois "Aurora, Illinois") in 1986, where he was resident scholar emeritus from 2012 until his death in 2018.

An accomplished scientific writer, he became known for his 1993 book *[The God Particle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_God_Particle_(book) "The God Particle (book)")* establishing the popularity of the term for the [Higgs boson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higgs_boson "Higgs boson").

## Early life and education

Lederman was born in New York City, New York, to Morris and Minna (Rosenberg) Lederman. His parents were [Ukrainian-Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukrainian-Jewish "Ukrainian-Jewish") immigrants from [Kyiv](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyiv "Kyiv") and [Odesa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odesa "Odesa"). Lederman graduated from [James Monroe High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Monroe_High_School_(New_York_City) "James Monroe High School (New York City)") in the [South Bronx](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Bronx "South Bronx"), and received his [bachelor's degree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor's_degree "Bachelor's degree") from the [City College of New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_College_of_New_York "City College of New York") in 1943.

Lederman enlisted in the [United States Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Army "United States Army") [Signal Corps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Army_Signal_Corps "United States Army Signal Corps") during [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II"), intending to become a physicist after his service. Following his discharge in 1946, he enrolled at [Columbia University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia_University "Columbia University")'s graduate school, receiving his [Ph.D.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Philosophy "Doctor of Philosophy") in 1951.

## Academic career

Lederman became a faculty member at Columbia University, and he was promoted to full professor in 1958 as [Eugene Higgins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugene_Higgins "Eugene Higgins") Professor of Physics. In 1960, on leave from Columbia, he spent time at [CERN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CERN "CERN") in Geneva as a [Ford Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ford_Foundation "Ford Foundation") Fellow. He took an extended leave of absence from Columbia in 1979 to become director of Fermilab. Resigning from Columbia (and retiring from Fermilab) in 1989, he then taught briefly at the [University of Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Chicago "University of Chicago"). He then moved to the [physics department](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IIT_Physics_Department "IIT Physics Department") of the [Illinois Institute of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illinois_Institute_of_Technology "Illinois Institute of Technology"), where he served as the Pritzker Professor of Science. In 1992, Lederman served as president of the [American Association for the Advancement of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Association_for_the_Advancement_of_Science "American Association for the Advancement of Science").

Lederman, rare for a Nobel Prize winning professor, took it upon himself to teach physics to non-physics majors at The University of Chicago.

Lederman served as president of the board of sponsors of the [Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bulletin_of_the_Atomic_Scientists "Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists"), and at the time of his death was chair emeritus. He also served on the board of trustees for Science Service, now known as [Society for Science & the Public](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Society_for_Science_&_the_Public "Society for Science & the Public"), from 1989 to 1992, and was a member of the [JASON defense advisory group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JASON_(advisory_group) "JASON (advisory group)"). Lederman was also one of the main proponents of the "[Physics First](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics_First "Physics First")" movement. Also known as "Right-side Up Science" and "Biology Last," this movement seeks to rearrange the current high school science curriculum so that physics precedes chemistry and biology.

Lederman was an early supporter of [Science Debate 2008](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_Debate_2008 "Science Debate 2008"), an initiative to get the then-candidates for president, [Barack Obama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barack_Obama "Barack Obama") and [John McCain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_McCain "John McCain"), to debate the nation's top science policy challenges. In October 2010, Lederman participated in the [USA Science and Engineering Festival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USA_Science_and_Engineering_Festival "USA Science and Engineering Festival")'s Lunch with a Laureate program where middle and high school students engaged in an informal conversation with a Nobel Prize-winning scientist over a brown-bag lunch. Lederman was also a member of the [USA Science and Engineering Festival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USA_Science_and_Engineering_Festival "USA Science and Engineering Festival")'s advisory board.

## Academic work

In 1956, Lederman worked on [parity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parity_(physics) "Parity (physics)") violation in weak interactions. [R. L. Garwin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Garwin "Richard Garwin"), Leon Lederman, and R. Weinrich modified an existing cyclotron experiment, and they immediately verified the [parity violation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parity_violation "Parity violation"). They delayed publication of their results until after [Wu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chien-Shiung_Wu "Chien-Shiung Wu")'s group was ready, and the two papers appeared back-to-back in the same physics journal. Among his achievements are the discovery of the [muon neutrino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muon_neutrino "Muon neutrino") in 1962 and the [bottom quark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bottom_quark "Bottom quark") in 1977. These helped establish his reputation as among the top particle physicists.

In 1976, a group of physicists, the [E288 experiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E288_experiment?action=edit&redlink=1 "E288 experiment") team, led by Lederman announced that a particle with a mass of about 6.0 GeV was being produced by the Fermilab particle accelerator. After taking further data, the group discovered that this particle did not actually exist, and the "discovery" was named "[Oops-Leon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oops-Leon "Oops-Leon")" as a pun on the original name, upsilon, and Lederman's first name. The name was reused for the [upsilon meson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upsilon_meson "Upsilon meson"), which the group discovered from subsequent data in 1977 at a higher mass of 9.5 GeV.

As the director of [Fermilab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermilab "Fermilab"), Lederman was a prominent supporter of the [Superconducting Super Collider](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superconducting_Super_Collider "Superconducting Super Collider") project, which was endorsed around 1983, and was a major proponent and advocate throughout its lifetime. Also at Fermilab, he oversaw the construction of the [Tevatron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tevatron "Tevatron"), for decades the world's highest-energy particle collider. Lederman later wrote his 1993 [popular science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Popular_science "Popular science") book *[The God Particle: If the Universe Is the Answer, What Is the Question?](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_God_Particle:_If_the_Universe_Is_the_Answer,_What_Is_the_Question%3F "The God Particle: If the Universe Is the Answer, What Is the Question?")* – which sought to promote awareness of the significance of such a project – in the context of the project's last years and the changing political climate of the 1990s. The increasingly moribund project was finally shelved that same year after some $2 billion of expenditures. In *The God Particle* he wrote, "The history of atomism is one of reductionism – the effort to reduce all the operations of nature to a small number of laws governing a small number of primordial objects" while stressing the importance of the [Higgs boson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higgs_boson "Higgs boson").

In 1988, Lederman received the [Nobel Prize for Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_for_Physics "Nobel Prize for Physics") along with [Melvin Schwartz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melvin_Schwartz "Melvin Schwartz") and [Jack Steinberger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jack_Steinberger "Jack Steinberger") "for the neutrino beam method and the demonstration of the [doublet structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doublet_structure?action=edit&redlink=1 "Doublet structure") of the leptons through the discovery of the muon neutrino". Lederman also received the National Medal of Science (1965), the [Elliott Cresson Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliott_Cresson_Medal "Elliott Cresson Medal") for Physics (1976), the Wolf Prize for Physics (1982) and the [Enrico Fermi Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enrico_Fermi_Award "Enrico Fermi Award") (1992). In 1995, he received the [Chicago History Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicago_History_Museum "Chicago History Museum") "Making History Award" for Distinction in Science Medicine and Technology.

## Personal life and death

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e1/HD.3F.002_%2811086394916%29.jpg/250px-HD.3F.002_%2811086394916%29.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:HD.3F.002_(11086394916).jpg)

Lederman in May 2007

Lederman's best friend during his college years, [Martin J. Klein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_J._Klein "Martin J. Klein"), convinced him of "the splendors of physics during a long evening over many beers". He was known for his sense of humor in the physics community. On August 26, 2008, Lederman was video-recorded by a science focused organization called ScienCentral, on the street in New York City, answering questions from passersby. He answered questions such as "What is the strong force?" and "What happened before the Big Bang?".

He had three children with his first wife, Florence Gordon, and toward the end of his life lived with his second wife, Ellen (Carr), in [Driggs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Driggs,_Idaho "Driggs, Idaho"), [Idaho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idaho "Idaho").

Lederman was an [atheist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atheist "Atheist"). Lederman began to suffer from memory loss in 2011 and, after struggling with [medical bills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Healthcare_in_the_United_States "Healthcare in the United States"), he had to sell his Nobel Prize medal for $765,000 to cover the costs in 2015. He died of complications from dementia on October 3, 2018, at a care facility in [Rexburg, Idaho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rexburg,_Idaho "Rexburg, Idaho"), at the age of 96.
