**Leo James Rainwater** (December 9, 1917 – May 31, 1986) was an American physicist who shared the [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics") in 1975 for his part in determining the asymmetrical shapes of certain [atomic nuclei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_nuclei "Atomic nuclei").

During [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II"), he worked on the [Manhattan Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manhattan_Project "Manhattan Project") that developed the first [atomic bombs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_bomb "Atomic bomb"). In 1949, he began developing his theory that, contrary to what was then believed, not all [atomic nuclei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_nuclei "Atomic nuclei") are spherical. His ideas were later tested and confirmed by [Aage Bohr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aage_Bohr "Aage Bohr")'s and [Ben Mottelson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ben_Mottelson "Ben Mottelson")'s experiments. He also contributed to the scientific understanding of [X-rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray "X-ray") and participated in the [United States Atomic Energy Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Atomic_Energy_Commission "United States Atomic Energy Commission") and naval research projects.

Rainwater joined the physics faculty at Columbia in 1946, where he reached the rank of full professor in 1952 and was named Pupin Professor of Physics in 1982. He received the [Ernest Orlando Lawrence Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_Orlando_Lawrence_Award "Ernest Orlando Lawrence Award") for Physics in 1963 and in 1975 was awarded the [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics"), "for the discovery of the connection between collective motion and particle motion in atomic nuclei and the development of the theory of the structure of the [atomic nucleus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_nucleus "Atomic nucleus") based on this connection".

## Early life

Leo James Rainwater was born on December 9, 1917, in [Council, Idaho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council,_Idaho "Council, Idaho"), the son of a former civil engineer who ran the local general store, Leo Jaspar Rainwater and his wife Edna Eliza née Teague. He never used his first name and was always referred to as James or Jim. His father died in the [great influenza epidemic of 1918](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_flu "Spanish flu") and Rainwater and his mother moved to [Hanford, California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanford,_California "Hanford, California"), where she married George Fowler, a widower with two sons, Freeman and John. In time he also acquired a half-brother, George Fowler Jr., who became naval officer. At high school he excelled in mathematics, chemistry and physics and was admitted to the [California Institute of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California_Institute_of_Technology "California Institute of Technology") on the strength of a chemistry competition. He received his [Bachelor of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_Science "Bachelor of Science") degree as a physics major in 1939.

## Manhattan Project

Rainwater then chose to undertake postgraduate studies at [Columbia University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia_University "Columbia University"). At the time this was an unusual move for a scholar from California, as Columbia was not then renowned for its physics; but this had recently changed. [George B. Pegram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_B._Pegram "George B. Pegram") had recently built up the physics department, and hired scientists like [Enrico Fermi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enrico_Fermi "Enrico Fermi"). At Columbia Rainwater studied under [Isidor Isaac Rabi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isidor_Isaac_Rabi "Isidor Isaac Rabi"), Enrico Fermi, [Edward Teller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Teller "Edward Teller") and [John R. Dunning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_R._Dunning "John R. Dunning"). Fermi was engaged in [neutron moderator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron_moderator "Neutron moderator") studies that would lead to the construction of the first nuclear reactor, while Dunning and [Eugene T. Booth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugene_T._Booth "Eugene T. Booth") had built Columbia's first [cyclotron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclotron "Cyclotron"), in the basement of the [Pupin Physics Laboratories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pupin_Hall "Pupin Hall"). Rainwater received his [Master of Arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master_of_Arts "Master of Arts") in 1941. For his [Doctor of Philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Philosophy "Doctor of Philosophy") thesis on "Neutron beam spectrometer studies of boron, cadmium, and the energy distribution from paraffin", written under Dunning's supervision, he built a [neutron spectrometer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron_spectroscopy "Neutron spectroscopy") and developed techniques for its use. Rainwater married Emma Louise Smith in March 1942. They had three sons, James, Robert and William and a daughter, Elizabeth Ann, who died from leukaemia when she was nine.

Fermi's reactor group moved to the [Metallurgical Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metallurgical_Laboratory "Metallurgical Laboratory") at the [University of Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Chicago "University of Chicago") in 1942. Rainwater remained at Columbia, where he joined the [Manhattan Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manhattan_Project "Manhattan Project")'s Substitute Alloy Materials (SAM) Laboratories. The Manhattan Project was the Allied effort during [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II") to develop [atomic bombs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_bomb "Atomic bomb"). The SAM Laboratories' primary task was the development of [gaseous diffusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaseous_diffusion "Gaseous diffusion") technology for [uranium enrichment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium_enrichment "Uranium enrichment"), to produce [fissile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fissile "Fissile") [uranium-235](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranium-235 "Uranium-235") for use in atomic bombs. Rainwater worked with [William W. Havens Jr.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_W._Havens_Jr. "William W. Havens Jr.") and [Chien-Shiung Wu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chien-Shiung_Wu "Chien-Shiung Wu"), mostly on studies of [neutron cross sections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron_cross_section "Neutron cross section"), using the neutron spectrometer. After the war, a dozen papers by Dunning, Havens, Rainwater and Wu would be declassified and published. So too was his thesis, published in the [Physical Review](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_Review "Physical Review") in two parts with Havens's thesis, and he was awarded his doctorate in 1946. In 1963 he was awarded the [United States Atomic Energy Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Atomic_Energy_Commission "United States Atomic Energy Commission")'s [Ernest Orlando Lawrence Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_Orlando_Lawrence_Award "Ernest Orlando Lawrence Award"), for his work on the Manhattan Project.

## Later life

Rainwater remained at Columbia as an instructor. In 1948, he began teaching courses on nuclear structure. [Niels Bohr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niels_Bohr "Niels Bohr") and [John Wheeler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Archibald_Wheeler "John Archibald Wheeler") had developed a theoretical treatment for [nuclear fission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_fission "Nuclear fission") in 1939 that they based on the [liquid drop model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid_drop_model "Liquid drop model") of the nucleus. This was superseded in 1949 by [Maria Goeppert Mayer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maria_Goeppert_Mayer "Maria Goeppert Mayer")'s [nuclear shell model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_shell_model "Nuclear shell model"), which could explain more about the structure of heavy elements than the older theory but it still had limits. At a colloquium at Columbia in 1949, [Charles H. Townes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_H._Townes "Charles H. Townes") reported experimental results that indicated [quadrupole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadrupole "Quadrupole") moments greater than those indicated by the shell model. It occurred to Rainwater that this could be explained and the differences between the liquid drop and nuclear shell models could be reconciled, if the [atomic nucleus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_nucleus "Atomic nucleus") were not spherical, as had been assumed but could assume other shapes. Rainwater published his theoretical paper in 1950. By chance, that year he was sharing an office with [Aage Bohr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aage_Bohr "Aage Bohr"), who had independently come to a similar idea. Bohr and [Ben Mottelson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ben_Mottelson "Ben Mottelson") published their results in three papers in 1952 and 1953 that demonstrated convincing agreement with available data. Rainwater felt that his model was overlooked. He later recalled that:

> When I made my proposal for use of a spheroidal nuclear model, it seemed to be an obvious answer which would immediately be simultaneously suggested by all theorists in the field. I do not understand why it was not. I was also surprised and dismayed to hear one or more respected theorists announce in every Nuclear Physics Conference which I attended through 1955 some such comment as, "Although the Nuclear Shell Model seems empirically to work very well, there is at present no theoretical justification as to why it should apply."

With funding from the [Office of Naval Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Office_of_Naval_Research "Office of Naval Research"), Rainwater built a [synchrocyclotron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synchrocyclotron "Synchrocyclotron"), which became operational in 1950, at the [Nevis Laboratories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nevis_Laboratories "Nevis Laboratories"), on an estate on the [Hudson River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hudson_River "Hudson River") at [Irvington, New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irvington,_New_York "Irvington, New York"), willed to Columbia University by the [DuPont](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DuPont "DuPont") family. He became a full professor in 1952 and was the director of Nevis Laboratories from 1951 to 1954 and again from 1957 to 1961. He worked with his student [Val Fitch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Val_Fitch "Val Fitch") on studies of [muonic atoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muonic_atom "Muonic atom"), atoms where an [electron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron "Electron") is replaced by a [muon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muon "Muon"). After 1965, he worked on turning the Nevis synchrocyclotron into a [meson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meson "Meson") facility. When a reporter rang in 1975 to inform him that he had won the [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics"), he initially thought that it was for his work on muonic atoms. Several hours passed before he discovered that it was for his work on nuclear structure, the Nobel Prize being shared with Bohr and Mottelson.

He was a fellow of the [American Physical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Physical_Society "American Physical Society"), the [New York Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_Academy_of_Sciences "New York Academy of Sciences") and the [Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_of_Electrical_and_Electronics_Engineers "Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers"), and was a member of the [National Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Academy_of_Sciences "National Academy of Sciences"), the [American Institute of Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Institute_of_Physics "American Institute of Physics"), the [American Association of Physics Teachers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Association_of_Physics_Teachers "American Association of Physics Teachers") and the [Optical Society of America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_Society_of_America "Optical Society of America").

Rainwater succeeded [Robert R. Wilson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_R._Wilson "Robert R. Wilson") as Michael I. Pupin Professor of Physics in 1983.

Rainwater collapsed after a lecture at the Pupin Laboratories in 1985 but was revived by a student who knew how to administer [CPR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CPR "CPR"). In declining health, he retired and became a professor emeritus in February 1986. He died from cardiopulmonary arrest at St. John's Riverside Hospital in [Yonkers, New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yonkers,_New_York "Yonkers, New York") on May 31, 1986. He was survived by his wife, three sons and half-brother George.
