**Sir Charles Kuen Kao** (November 4, 1933 – September 23, 2018) was a Hong Kong [electrical engineer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_engineer "Electrical engineer") who contributed to the development and use of [fibre optics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibre_optics "Fibre optics") in telecommunications. In the 1960s, Kao created various methods to combine [glass fibres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glass_fibre "Glass fibre") with [lasers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laser "Laser") in order to transmit [digital data](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_data "Digital data"), which laid the groundwork for the evolution of the [Internet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet "Internet") and the eventual creation of the [World Wide Web](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Wide_Web "World Wide Web").

Born in 1933 in [Shanghai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shanghai "Shanghai"), Kao and his family settled in [Hong Kong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Hong_Kong "British Hong Kong") in 1949. He graduated from [St. Joseph's College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Joseph's_College,_Hong_Kong "St. Joseph's College, Hong Kong") in Hong Kong in 1952 and went to London to study electrical engineering. In the 1960s, he worked at [Standard Telecommunication Laboratories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_Telecommunication_Laboratories "Standard Telecommunication Laboratories"), the research center of [Standard Telephones and Cables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_Telephones_and_Cables "Standard Telephones and Cables") (STC) in [Harlow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harlow "Harlow"), and it was here in 1966 that he laid the groundwork for fibre optics in communication. Known as the "godfather of broadband," the "father of fibre optics," and the "father of fibre optic communications," he continued his work in Hong Kong at the [Chinese University of Hong Kong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_University_of_Hong_Kong "Chinese University of Hong Kong"), and in the United States at [ITT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ITT_Inc. "ITT Inc.") (the parent corporation of STC) and [Yale University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yale_University "Yale University").

In 2009, Kao was awarded the [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics") "for groundbreaking achievements concerning the transmission of light in fibres for [optical communication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_communication "Optical communication")." The following year, he was knighted by [Queen Elizabeth II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_II "Elizabeth II") "for services to fibre-optic communications."

Kao was a [permanent resident of Hong Kong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Residents_of_Hong_Kong "Residents of Hong Kong"), and a citizen of the [United Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_nationality_law "British nationality law") and the [United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citizenship_of_the_United_States "Citizenship of the United States").

## Early life and education

Kao Kuen was born on November 4, 1933, in [Shanghai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shanghai "Shanghai"), China, and lived with his parents in the [Shanghai French Concession](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shanghai_French_Concession "Shanghai French Concession"). He studied [Chinese classics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_classics "Chinese classics") at home with his brother, under a tutor. He also studied English and French at the Shanghai World School (上海世界學校) that was founded by a number of progressive Chinese educators, including [Cai Yuanpei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cai_Yuanpei "Cai Yuanpei").

After the [Communist Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_Communist_Revolution "Chinese Communist Revolution") in 1949, Kao's family settled in [Hong Kong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Hong_Kong "British Hong Kong"), which at the time was a British [crown colony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crown_colony "Crown colony"). Much of his mother's siblings moved to Hong Kong in the late 1930s. Among them, his mother's youngest brother took good care of him.

Kao's family lived on Lau Sin Street at the edge of the [North Point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Point,_Hong_Kong "North Point, Hong Kong"), a neighbourhood of Shanghai immigrants. During Kao's time in Hong Kong, he studied at [St. Joseph's College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Joseph's_College,_Hong_Kong "St. Joseph's College, Hong Kong") for five years and graduated in 1952.

Kao obtained high score in the Hong Kong School Certificate Examination, which at the time was the territory's matriculation examination, qualifying him for admission to the [University of Hong Kong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Hong_Kong "University of Hong Kong") (HKU). However, [electrical engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_engineering "Electrical engineering") wasn't a programme available at HKU, the territory's then only [tertiary education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tertiary_education "Tertiary education") institution.

Hence, in 1953, Kao went to [London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London "London") to continue his studies in secondary school and obtained his [A-Level](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A-level_(United_Kingdom) "A-level (United Kingdom)") in 1955. He was later admitted to Woolwich Polytechnic (now the [University of Greenwich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Greenwich "University of Greenwich")), where he obtained a [B.Sc.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_Science "Bachelor of Science") in 1957. He then pursued doctoral research under [Harold Barlow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Barlow "Harold Barlow") at [University College London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_College_London "University College London") (UCL) while working at [Standard Telecommunication Laboratories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_Telecommunication_Laboratories "Standard Telecommunication Laboratories") in [Harlow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harlow "Harlow"), the research centre of [Standard Telephones and Cables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_Telephones_and_Cables "Standard Telephones and Cables") (STC). In 1965, Kao received his [Ph.D.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Philosophy "Doctor of Philosophy") in Electrical Engineering from UCL with a thesis on [waveguides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waveguide "Waveguide").

### Ancestry and family

Kao's father [Kao Chun-Hsiang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kao_Chun-Hsiang?action=edit&redlink=1 "Kao Chun-Hsiang") (高君湘), originally from [Jinshan City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jinshan_City?action=edit&redlink=1 "Jinshan City") (now a district of Shanghai City), obtained his [Juris Doctor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juris_Doctor "Juris Doctor") from the [University of Michigan Law School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Michigan_Law_School "University of Michigan Law School") in 1925. He was a judge at the [Shanghai Concession](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shanghai_International_Settlement "Shanghai International Settlement") and later a professor at [Soochow University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soochow_University_(1900–1952) "Soochow University (1900–1952)") (then in Shanghai) Comparative Law School of China.

His grandfather [Kao Hsieh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gao_Xie "Gao Xie") was a scholar, poet, and artist. Several writers including [Kao Hsü](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gao_Xu "Gao Xu"), [Yao Kuang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yao_Guang_(poet)?action=edit&redlink=1 "Yao Guang (poet)") (姚光), and [Kao Tseng](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kao_Tseng?action=edit&redlink=1 "Kao Tseng") (高增) were also Kao's close relatives.

His father's cousin was astronomer [Kao Ping-tse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kao_Ping-tse "Kao Ping-tse") ([Kao crater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kao_(crater) "Kao (crater)") is named after him). Kao's younger brother Timothy Wu Kao (高鋙) is a civil engineer and professor emeritus at the [Catholic University of America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_University_of_America "Catholic University of America"); his research is in [hydrodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrodynamics "Hydrodynamics").

Kao met his future wife Gwen May-Wan Kao (née [Wong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huang_(surname) "Huang (surname)"); 黃美芸) in London after graduation, when they worked together as engineers at STC. She was [British Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Chinese "British Chinese"). They were married in 1959 in London, and had a son and a daughter, both of whom reside and work in [Silicon Valley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon_Valley "Silicon Valley"), California. According to Kao's autobiography, Kao was a Catholic who attended [Catholic Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Church "Catholic Church"), while his wife attended the [Anglican Communion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglican_Communion "Anglican Communion").

## Academic career

### Fibre optics and communications

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/49/Fibreoptic.jpg/250px-Fibreoptic.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Fibreoptic.jpg)

A bundle of [silica glass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silica_glass "Silica glass") fibres for optical communication, which are the de facto worldwide standard. Kao also first publicly suggested that [silica glass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silica_glass "Silica glass") of high purity is an ideal material for long range optical communication.

In the 1960s at [Standard Telecommunication Laboratories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_Telecommunication_Laboratories "Standard Telecommunication Laboratories") (STL) based in [Harlow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harlow "Harlow"), Essex, England, Kao and his coworkers did their pioneering work in creating [fibre optics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiber_optics "Fiber optics") as a telecommunications medium, by demonstrating that the high-loss of existing fibre optics arose from impurities in the glass, rather than from an underlying problem with the technology itself.

In 1963, when Kao first joined the optical communications research team he made notes summarising the background situation and available technology at the time, and identifying the key individuals involved. Initially Kao worked in the team of [Antoni E. Karbowiak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antoni_E._Karbowiak?action=edit&redlink=1 "Antoni E. Karbowiak") (Toni Karbowiak), who was working under [Alec Reeves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alec_Reeves "Alec Reeves") to study optical waveguides for communications. Kao's task was to investigate fibre [attenuation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attenuation "Attenuation"), for which he collected samples from different fibre manufacturers and also investigated the properties of bulk glasses carefully. Kao's study primarily convinced him that the impurities in material caused the high light losses of those fibres. Later that year, Kao was appointed head of the electro-optics research group at STL. He took over the optical communication program of STL in December 1964, because his supervisor, Karbowiak, left to take the chair in Communications in the School of Electrical Engineering at the [University of New South Wales (UNSW)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_New_South_Wales "University of New South Wales"), Sydney, Australia.

Although Kao succeeded Karbowiak as manager of optical communications research, he immediately decided to abandon Karbowiak's plan (thin-film waveguide) and overall change research direction with his colleague [George Hockham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Hockham "George Hockham"). They not only considered [optical physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_optics "Physical optics") but also the material properties. The results were first presented by Kao to the [IEE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institution_of_Electrical_Engineers "Institution of Electrical Engineers") in January 1966 in London, and further published in July with George Hockham (1964–1965 worked with Kao). This study proposed the use of glass fibres for optical communication. The concepts described, especially the electromagnetic theory and performance parameters, are the basis of today's optical fibre communications.

> "What Kao did in Harlow transformed the world and provided a backbone for the internet. He was the father of fiber optics."

— Harlow Museum's David Devine on Kao's pioneering work in fiber optics at [STC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_Telephones_and_Cables "Standard Telephones and Cables")'s [Standard Telecommunication Laboratories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_Telecommunication_Laboratories "Standard Telecommunication Laboratories") in Harlow

In 1965, Kao with Hockham concluded that the fundamental limitation for glass light attenuation is below 20 *dB/km* (*[decibels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decibels "Decibels") per kilometer*, is a measure of the attenuation of a signal over a distance), which is a key threshold value for optical communications. However, at the time of this determination, optical fibres commonly exhibited light loss as high as 1,000 dB/km and even more. This conclusion opened the intense race to find low-loss materials and suitable fibres for reaching such criteria.

Kao, together with his new team (members including T. W. Davies, M. W. Jones and C. R. Wright), pursued this goal by testing various materials. They precisely measured the attenuation of light with different wavelengths in glasses and other materials. During this period, Kao pointed out that the high purity of fused silica (SiO2) made it an ideal candidate for optical communication. Kao also stated that the impurity of glass material is the main cause for the dramatic decay of light transmission inside glass fibre, rather than fundamental physical effects such as [scattering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scattering "Scattering") as many physicists thought at that time, and such impurity could be removed. This led to a worldwide study and production of high-purity glass fibres. When Kao first proposed that such glass fibre could be used for long-distance information transfer and could replace copper wires which were used for telecommunication during that era, his ideas were widely disbelieved; later people realized that Kao's ideas revolutionized the whole communication technology and industry.

Kao also played a leading role in the early stage of engineering and commercial realization of optical communication. In spring 1966, Kao traveled to the U.S. but failed to interest [Bell Labs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell_Labs "Bell Labs"), which was a competitor of STL in [communication technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_and_communication_technologies "Information and communication technologies") at that time. He subsequently traveled to Japan and gained support. Kao visited many glass and [polymer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polymer "Polymer") factories, discussed with various people including engineers, scientists, businessmen about the techniques and improvement of glass fibre manufacture. In 1969, Kao with M. W. Jones measured the intrinsic loss of bulk-fused silica at 4 dB/km, which is the first evidence of ultra-transparent glass. Bell Labs started considering fibre optics seriously. As of 2017, fibre optic losses (from both bulk and intrinsic sources) are as low as 0.1419 dB/km at the 1.56 μm wavelength.

Kao developed important techniques and configurations for glass fibre waveguides, and contributed to the development of different fibre types and system devices which met both civil and [military](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_communications "Military communications") application requirements, and peripheral supporting systems for optical fibre communication. In mid-1970s, he did seminal work on glass fibre [fatigue strength](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fatigue_limit "Fatigue limit"). When named the first ITT Executive Scientist, Kao launched the "[Terabit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terabit "Terabit") Technology" program in addressing the high frequency limits of signal processing, so Kao is also known as the "father of the terabit technology concept". Kao has published more than 100 papers and was granted over 30 patents, including the water-resistant high-strength fibres (with M. S. Maklad).

At an early stage of developing optic fibres, Kao already strongly preferred [single-mode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single-mode_optical_fiber "Single-mode optical fiber") for long-distance optical communication, instead of using [multi-mode systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multi-mode_optical_fiber "Multi-mode optical fiber"). His vision later was followed and now is applied almost exclusively. Kao was also a visionary of modern [submarine communications cables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submarine_communications_cable "Submarine communications cable") and largely promoted this idea. He predicted in 1983 that world's seas would be littered with fibre optics, five years ahead of the time that such a trans-oceanic fibre-optic cable first became serviceable.

[Ali Javan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ali_Javan "Ali Javan")'s introduction of a steady [helium–neon laser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helium–neon_laser "Helium–neon laser") and Kao's discovery of fibre light-loss properties now are recognized as the two essential milestones for the development of fibre-optic communications.

### Later work

Kao joined the [Chinese University of Hong Kong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_University_of_Hong_Kong "Chinese University of Hong Kong") (CUHK) in 1970 to found the Department of Electronics, which later became the Department of Electronic Engineering. During this period, Kao was the [reader](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reader_(academic_rank) "Reader (academic rank)") and then the [chair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chair_(academic) "Chair (academic)") Professor of Electronics at CUHK; he built up both undergraduate and graduate study programs of electronics and oversaw the graduation of his first students. Under his leadership, the School of Education and other new research institutes were established. He returned to [ITT Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ITT_Corporation "ITT Corporation") in 1974 (the parent corporation of STC at that time) in the United States and worked in [Roanoke, Virginia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roanoke,_Virginia "Roanoke, Virginia"), first as Chief Scientist and later as Director of Engineering. In 1982, he became the first ITT Executive Scientist and was stationed mainly at the Advanced Technology Center in [Connecticut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connecticut "Connecticut"). While there, he served as an adjunct professor and Fellow of Trumbull College at [Yale University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yale_University "Yale University"). In 1985, Kao spent one year in [West Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Germany "West Germany"), at the SEL Research Center. In 1986, Kao was the Corporate Director of Research at ITT.

Kao was one of the earliest to study the environmental effects of [land reclamation in Hong Kong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_reclamation_in_Hong_Kong "Land reclamation in Hong Kong"), and presented one of his first related studies at the conference of the [Association of Commonwealth Universities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Association_of_Commonwealth_Universities "Association of Commonwealth Universities") (ACU) in [Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edinburgh "Edinburgh") in 1972.

Kao was Vice-Chancellor of the Chinese University of Hong Kong from 1987 to 1996. From 1991, Kao was an Independent Non-Executive Director and a member of the Audit Committee of the Varitronix International Limited in Hong Kong. From 1993 to 1994, he was the President of the Association of Southeast Asian Institutions of Higher Learning (ASAIHL). In 1996, Kao donated to [Yale University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yale_University "Yale University"), and the *Charles Kao Fund Research Grants* was established to support Yale's studies, research and creative projects in Asia. The fund currently is managed by Yale University Councils on East Asian and Southeast Asian Studies. After his retirement from CUHK in 1996, Kao spent his six-month sabbatical leave at the Department of Electrical and Electronic Engineering of [Imperial College London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_College_London "Imperial College London"); from 1997 to 2002, he also served as visiting professor in the same department.

Kao was chairman and member of the Energy Advisory Committee (EAC) of Hong Kong for two years, and retired from the position on July 15, 2000. Kao was a member of the Council of Advisors on Innovation and Technology of Hong Kong, appointed on April 20, 2000. In 2000, Kao co-founded the [Independent Schools Foundation Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_Schools_Foundation_Academy "Independent Schools Foundation Academy"), which is located in [Cyberport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyberport "Cyberport"), Hong Kong. He was its founding chairman in 2000, and stepped down from the board of the ISF in December 2008. Kao was the [keynote speaker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keynote_speaker "Keynote speaker") at [IEEE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IEEE "IEEE") [GLOBECOM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GLOBECOM "GLOBECOM") 2002 in [Taipei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taipei "Taipei"), Taiwan. In 2003, Kao was named a Chair Professor by special appointment at the Electronics Institute of the College of Electrical Engineering and Computer Science, [National Taiwan University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Taiwan_University "National Taiwan University"). Kao then worked as the chairman and CEO of Transtech Services Ltd., a telecommunication consultancy in Hong Kong. He was the founder, chairman and CEO of ITX Services Limited. From 2003 to January 30, 2009, Kao was an independent [non-executive director](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-executive_director "Non-executive director") and member of the audit committee of [Next Media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Next_Media "Next Media").

## Later life and death

Kao's international travels led him to opine that he belonged to the world instead of any country. An open letter published by Kao and his wife in 2010 later clarified that "Charles studied in Hong Kong for his high schooling, he has taught here, he was the Vice-Chancellor of [CUHK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_University_of_Hong_Kong "Chinese University of Hong Kong") and retired here too. So he is a [Hong Kong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hong_Kong "Hong Kong") belonger."

[Pottery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_ceramics "Chinese ceramics") making was a hobby of Kao's. Kao also enjoyed reading [Wuxia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wuxia "Wuxia") (Chinese martial fantasy) novels.

Kao suffered from [Alzheimer's disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alzheimer's_disease "Alzheimer's disease") from early 2004 and had [speech difficulty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speech_disorder "Speech disorder"), but had no problem recognising people or addresses. His father suffered from the same disease. Beginning in 2008, he resided in [Mountain View, California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mountain_View,_California "Mountain View, California"), [United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States "United States"), where he moved from Hong Kong in order to live near his children and grandchild.

On October 6, 2009, when Kao was awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics for his contributions to the study of the transmission of light in optical fibres and for fibre [communication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communication "Communication"), he said, "I am absolutely speechless and never expected such an honor." Kao's wife Gwen told the press that the prize will primarily be used for Charles's medical expenses. In 2010 Charles and Gwen Kao founded the Charles K. Kao Foundation for Alzheimer's Disease to raise public awareness about the disease and provide support for the patients.

In 2016, Kao lost the ability to maintain his balance. At the end-stage of his [dementia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dementia "Dementia") he was cared for by his wife and intended not to be kept alive with life support or have CPR performed on him. Kao died at [Bradbury Hospice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bradbury_Hospice "Bradbury Hospice") in [Hong Kong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hong_Kong "Hong Kong") on September 23, 2018, at the age of 84.

## Commemoration

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/bc/HK_SciencePark_Auditorium.JPG/250px-HK_SciencePark_Auditorium.JPG?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:HK_SciencePark_Auditorium.JPG)

- 1996 (November 7): The north wing of the Chinese University of Hong Kong Science Center was named the Charles Kuen Kao Building.

- 2014 (September): Sir Charles Kao UTC (now known as [BMAT STEM Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BMAT_STEM_Academy "BMAT STEM Academy")) was opened.
- 2014: Kao Data, a data center operator based on the former site of Sir Charles Kao's work on fibre optics cables, was founded.
