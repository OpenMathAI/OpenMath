**Geoffrey Everest Hinton** (born 6 December 1947) is a British-Canadian [computer scientist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_scientist "Computer scientist"), [cognitive scientist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_scientist "Cognitive scientist"), [cognitive psychologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_psychologist "Cognitive psychologist") and [Nobel Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics") laureate known for his work on [artificial neural networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_neural_networks "Artificial neural networks"), which earned him the title "the Godfather of AI". He is University Professor Emeritus at the [University of Toronto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Toronto "University of Toronto").

From 2013 to 2023, he divided his time working for [Google Brain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google_Brain "Google Brain") and the University of Toronto before publicly announcing his departure from Google in May 2023, citing concerns about the many risks of [artificial intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence "Artificial intelligence") (AI) technology. In 2017, he co-founded and became the chief scientific advisor of the [Vector Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_Institute_(Canada) "Vector Institute (Canada)") in Toronto.

With [David Rumelhart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Rumelhart "David Rumelhart") and [Ronald J. Williams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_J._Williams "Ronald J. Williams"), Hinton was co-author of a highly cited paper published in 1986 that popularised the [backpropagation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Backpropagation "Backpropagation") algorithm for training multi-layer neural networks, although they were not the first to propose the approach. Hinton is viewed as a leading figure in the [deep learning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deep_learning "Deep learning") community. The image-recognition milestone of the [AlexNet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AlexNet "AlexNet") designed in collaboration with his students [Alex Krizhevsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alex_Krizhevsky "Alex Krizhevsky") and [Ilya Sutskever](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ilya_Sutskever "Ilya Sutskever") for the [ImageNet challenge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ImageNet_challenge "ImageNet challenge") 2012 was a breakthrough in the field of computer vision.

Hinton received the 2018 [Turing Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turing_Award "Turing Award"), together with [Yoshua Bengio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoshua_Bengio "Yoshua Bengio") and [Yann LeCun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yann_LeCun "Yann LeCun") for their work on deep learning. They are sometimes referred to as the "Godfathers of Deep Learning" and have continued to give public talks together. He was also awarded, along with [John Hopfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Hopfield "John Hopfield"), the 2024 [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics") for "foundational discoveries and inventions that enable [machine learning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machine_learning "Machine learning") with artificial neural networks".

In May 2023, Hinton announced his resignation from Google to be able to "freely speak out about the risks of AI". He has voiced concerns about [deliberate misuse by malicious actors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AI_safety#Detecting_malicious_use "AI safety"), [technological unemployment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technological_unemployment#Artificial_intelligence "Technological unemployment"), and [existential risk from artificial general intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Existential_risk_from_artificial_general_intelligence "Existential risk from artificial general intelligence"). He noted that establishing safety guidelines will require cooperation among those competing in use of AI in order to avoid the worst outcomes. After receiving the Nobel Prize, he called for urgent research into [AI safety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AI_safety "AI safety") to figure out how to control [AI systems smarter than humans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AI_superintelligence "AI superintelligence").

## Education

Hinton was born on 6 December 1947 in [Wimbledon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wimbledon,_London "Wimbledon, London") in the United Kingdom and was educated at [Clifton College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clifton_College "Clifton College") in Bristol. In 1967, he matriculated as an undergraduate student at [King's College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King's_College,_Cambridge "King's College, Cambridge") and, after switching between different fields such as [natural sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_Sciences_(Cambridge) "Natural Sciences (Cambridge)"), [history of art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_art "History of art"), and [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy "Philosophy"), eventually graduated with a [Bachelor of Arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_Arts "Bachelor of Arts") in [experimental psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Experimental_psychology "Experimental psychology") in 1970. He spent a year apprenticing [carpentry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carpentry "Carpentry") before returning to academic studies. From 1972 to 1975, he continued his study at the [University of Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Edinburgh "University of Edinburgh"), where he was awarded a [PhD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PhD "PhD") in [artificial intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence "Artificial intelligence") in 1978 for research supervised by [Christopher Longuet-Higgins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Longuet-Higgins "Christopher Longuet-Higgins"), who favored the [symbolic AI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbolic_AI "Symbolic AI") approach over the neural network approach.

## Career

After his PhD, Hinton initially worked at the [University of Sussex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Sussex "University of Sussex") and at the [MRC Applied Psychology Unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MRC_Cognition_and_Brain_Sciences_Unit "MRC Cognition and Brain Sciences Unit"). After having difficulty getting funding in Britain, he worked in the US at the [University of California, San Diego](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_San_Diego "University of California, San Diego"), and [Carnegie Mellon University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carnegie_Mellon_University "Carnegie Mellon University"). He was the founding director of the [Gatsby Charitable Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gatsby_Charitable_Foundation "Gatsby Charitable Foundation") Computational Neuroscience Unit at [University College London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_College_London "University College London"). He is currently [University Professor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_Professor "University Professor") [Emeritus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emeritus "Emeritus") in the [Department of Computer Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Toronto_Department_of_Computer_Science "University of Toronto Department of Computer Science") at the [University of Toronto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Toronto "University of Toronto"), where he has been affiliated since 1987.

Upon arrival in Canada, Geoffrey Hinton was appointed at the [Canadian Institute for Advanced Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canadian_Institute_for_Advanced_Research "Canadian Institute for Advanced Research") (CIFAR) in 1987 as a Fellow in CIFAR's first research program, Artificial Intelligence, Robotics & Society. In 2004, Hinton and collaborators successfully proposed the launch of a new program at CIFAR, "Neural Computation and Adaptive Perception" (NCAP), which today is named "Learning in Machines & Brains". Hinton would go on to lead NCAP for ten years. Among the members of the program are [Yoshua Bengio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoshua_Bengio "Yoshua Bengio") and [Yann LeCun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yann_LeCun "Yann LeCun"), with whom Hinton would go on to win the [ACM A.M. Turing Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turing_Award "Turing Award") in 2018. All three Turing winners continue to be members of the CIFAR Learning in Machines & Brains program.

Hinton taught a free online course on Neural Networks on the education platform [Coursera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coursera "Coursera") in 2012. He co-founded DNNresearch Inc. in 2012 with his two graduate students, Alex Krizhevsky and Ilya Sutskever, at the University of Toronto's department of computer science. In March 2013, Google acquired DNNresearch Inc. for $44 million, and Hinton planned to "divide his time between his university research and his work at Google".

In May 2023, Hinton publicly announced his resignation from Google. He explained his decision, saying he wanted to freely speak out about the risks of AI and added that part of him now regrets his life's work.

Notable former PhD students and [postdoctoral researchers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postdoctoral_research "Postdoctoral research") from his group include [Peter Dayan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Dayan "Peter Dayan"), Sam Roweis, [Max Welling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Welling "Max Welling"), [Richard Zemel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Zemel "Richard Zemel"), [Brendan Frey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brendan_Frey "Brendan Frey"), [Radford M. Neal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radford_M._Neal "Radford M. Neal"), [Yee Whye Teh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yee_Whye_Teh "Yee Whye Teh"), [Ruslan Salakhutdinov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruslan_Salakhutdinov "Ruslan Salakhutdinov"), [Ilya Sutskever](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ilya_Sutskever "Ilya Sutskever"), [Yann LeCun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yann_LeCun "Yann LeCun"), [Alex Graves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alex_Graves_(computer_scientist) "Alex Graves (computer scientist)"), [Zoubin Ghahramani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoubin_Ghahramani "Zoubin Ghahramani"), and [Peter Fitzhugh Brown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Fitzhugh_Brown "Peter Fitzhugh Brown").

## Research

Hinton's research concerns the use of neural networks for [machine learning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machine_learning "Machine learning"), [memory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memory "Memory"), [perception](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perception "Perception"), and symbol processing. He has written or co-written more than 200 [peer-reviewed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peer-review "Peer-review") publications.

In the 1980s, Hinton was part of the "Parallel Distributed Processing" group at Carnegie Mellon University, which included notable scientists like [Terrence Sejnowski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terrence_Sejnowski "Terrence Sejnowski"), [Francis Crick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Crick "Francis Crick"), [David Rumelhart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Rumelhart "David Rumelhart"), and [James McClelland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_McClelland_(psychologist) "James McClelland (psychologist)"). This group favoured the [connectionist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connectionist "Connectionist") approach during the [AI winter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AI_winter "AI winter"). Their findings were published in a two-volume set. The connectionist approach adopted by Hinton suggests that capabilities in areas like logic and grammar can be encoded into the parameters of neural networks, and that neural networks can learn them from data. [Symbolists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbolic_artificial_intelligence "Symbolic artificial intelligence") on the other side advocated for explicitly programming [knowledge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knowledge_representation_and_reasoning "Knowledge representation and reasoning") and [rules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rule-based_system "Rule-based system") into AI systems.

In 1985, Hinton co-invented [Boltzmann machines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boltzmann_machine "Boltzmann machine") with David Ackley and Terry Sejnowski. His other contributions to neural network research include [distributed representations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distributed_representation "Distributed representation"), [time delay neural network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_delay_neural_network "Time delay neural network"), [mixtures of experts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mixture_of_experts "Mixture of experts"), [Helmholtz machines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helmholtz_machines "Helmholtz machines") and [product of experts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Product_of_experts "Product of experts"). An accessible introduction to Geoffrey Hinton's research can be found in his articles in *[Scientific American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_American "Scientific American")* in September 1992 and October 1993. In 1995, Hinton and colleagues proposed the wake-sleep algorithm, involving a neural network with separate pathways for recognition and generation, being trained with alternating "wake" and "sleep" phases. In 2007, Hinton coauthored an [unsupervised learning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unsupervised_learning "Unsupervised learning") paper titled *Unsupervised learning of image transformations*. In 2008, he developed the visualization method [t-SNE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T-SNE "T-SNE") with Laurens van der Maaten. While Hinton was a postdoc at UC San Diego, David Rumelhart, Hinton and [Ronald J. Williams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_J._Williams "Ronald J. Williams") applied the [backpropagation algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Backpropagation "Backpropagation") to multi-layer neural networks. Their experiments showed that such networks can learn useful [internal representations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knowledge_representation "Knowledge representation") of data. In a 2018 interview, Hinton said that "David Rumelhart came up with the basic idea of backpropagation, so it's his invention." Although this work was important in popularising backpropagation, it was not the first to suggest the approach. Reverse-mode [automatic differentiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automatic_differentiation "Automatic differentiation"), of which backpropagation is a special case, was proposed by [Seppo Linnainmaa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seppo_Linnainmaa "Seppo Linnainmaa") in 1970, and [Paul Werbos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Werbos "Paul Werbos") proposed to use it to train neural networks in 1974.

In 2017, Hinton co-authored two [open-access](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open-access "Open-access") research papers about [capsule neural networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capsule_neural_network "Capsule neural network"), extending the concept of "capsule" introduced by Hinton in 2011. The architecture aims to better model part-whole relationships within objects in visual data. In 2021, Hinton presented GLOM, a speculative architecture idea also aiming to improve image understanding by modeling part-whole relationships in neural networks. In 2021, Hinton co-authored a widely cited paper proposing a framework for [contrastive learning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contrastive_learning "Contrastive learning") in computer vision. The technique involves pulling together representations of [augmented](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Image_augmentation "Image augmentation") versions of the same image, and pushing apart dissimilar representations.

At the 2022 [Conference on Neural Information Processing Systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conference_on_Neural_Information_Processing_Systems "Conference on Neural Information Processing Systems") (NeurIPS), Hinton introduced a new learning algorithm for neural networks that he calls the "Forward-Forward" algorithm. The idea is to replace the traditional forward-backwards passes of backpropagation with two forward passes, one with positive (i.e. real) data and the other with negative data that could be generated solely by the network. The Forward-Forward algorithm is well-suited for what Hinton calls "mortal computation", where the knowledge learned is not transferable to other systems and thus dies with the hardware, as can be the case for certain [analog computers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analog_computer "Analog computer") used for machine learning.

## Honours and awards

Hinton is a [Fellow of the US Association for the Advancement of Artificial Intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow_of_the_Association_for_the_Advancement_of_Artificial_Intelligence "Fellow of the Association for the Advancement of Artificial Intelligence") (FAAAI) since 1990. He was elected a [Fellow of the Royal Society of Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow_of_the_Royal_Society_of_Canada "Fellow of the Royal Society of Canada") (FRSC) in 1996, and then a [Fellow of the Royal Society of London (FRS) in 1998](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Fellows_of_the_Royal_Society_elected_in_1998 "List of Fellows of the Royal Society elected in 1998"). He was the first winner of the [Rumelhart Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rumelhart_Prize "Rumelhart Prize") in 2001. According to the Royal Society:

> Geoffrey Hinton is distinguished for his work on artificial neural nets, especially how they can be designed to learn without the aid of a human teacher. This may well be the start of autonomous intelligent brain-like machines. He has compared effects of brain damage with effects of losses in such a net, and found striking similarities with human impairment, such as for recognition of names and losses of categorisation. His work includes studies of mental imagery, and inventing puzzles for testing originality and creative intelligence.

In 2001, Hinton was awarded an honorary [Doctor of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Science "Doctor of Science") (DSc) degree from the [University of Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Edinburgh "University of Edinburgh"). He was awarded as [International Honorary Member of the American Academy of Arts and Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Academy_of_Arts_and_Sciences "American Academy of Arts and Sciences") in 2003. Also, in this year he was elected a Fellow of the US [Cognitive Science Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_Science_Society "Cognitive Science Society"). He was the 2005 recipient of the [IJCAI Award for Research Excellence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IJCAI_Award_for_Research_Excellence "IJCAI Award for Research Excellence") lifetime-achievement award. He was awarded the 2011 [Herzberg Canada Gold Medal for Science and Engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerhard_Herzberg_Canada_Gold_Medal_for_Science_and_Engineering "Gerhard Herzberg Canada Gold Medal for Science and Engineering"). In that same year, he also was awarded an honorary DSc degree from the [University of Sussex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Sussex "University of Sussex") In 2012, he received the Canada Council [Killam Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Killam_Prize "Killam Prize") in Engineering. In 2013, he was awarded an honorary doctorate from the [Université de Sherbrooke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Université_de_Sherbrooke "Université de Sherbrooke"). Hinton was elected an Honorary Foreign Member of the Spanish [Royal Academy of Engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Academy_of_Engineering_(Spain) "Royal Academy of Engineering (Spain)") in 2015.

In 2016, Hinton was elected an [International Member of the US National Academy of Engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Academy_of_Engineering "National Academy of Engineering") "for contributions to the theory and practice of artificial neural networks and their application to speech recognition and computer vision". He received the 2016 [IEEE/RSE Wolfson James Clerk Maxwell Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IEEE/RSE_Wolfson_James_Clerk_Maxwell_Award "IEEE/RSE Wolfson James Clerk Maxwell Award"). In 2016, he furthermore won the [BBVA Foundation Frontiers of Knowledge Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBVA_Foundation_Frontiers_of_Knowledge_Award "BBVA Foundation Frontiers of Knowledge Award") in the Information and Communication Technologies category, "for his pioneering and highly influential work" to endow machines with the ability to learn.

Together with [Yann LeCun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yann_LeCun "Yann LeCun") and [Yoshua Bengio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoshua_Bengio "Yoshua Bengio"), Hinton won the 2018 [Turing Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turing_Award "Turing Award") for conceptual and engineering breakthroughs that have made deep neural networks a critical component of computing. Also in 2018, he became a [Companion of the Order of Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Companion_of_the_Order_of_Canada "Companion of the Order of Canada") (CC).
In 2021, he received the [Dickson Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dickson_Prize "Dickson Prize") in Science from the Carnegie Mellon University and in 2022 the [Princess of Asturias Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princess_of_Asturias_Award "Princess of Asturias Award") in the Scientific Research category, along with [Yann LeCun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yann_LeCun "Yann LeCun"), [Yoshua Bengio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoshua_Bengio "Yoshua Bengio"), and [Demis Hassabis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demis_Hassabis "Demis Hassabis"). In the same year, Hinton received an Honorary [DSc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DSc "DSc") degree from the [University of Toronto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Toronto "University of Toronto"). In 2023, he was named an [ACM Fellow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ACM_Fellow "ACM Fellow"), elected an [International Member of the US National Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Academy_of_Sciences "National Academy of Sciences"), and received [Lifeboat Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lifeboat_Foundation "Lifeboat Foundation")'s 2023 Guardian Award along with Ilya Sutskever.

In 2024, he was jointly awarded the [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics") with [John Hopfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Hopfield "John Hopfield") "for foundational discoveries and inventions that enable machine learning with artificial neural networks." His development of the [Boltzmann machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boltzmann_machine "Boltzmann machine") was explicitly mentioned in the citation. When the *[New York Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_Times "New York Times")* reporter Cade Metz asked Hinton to explain in simpler terms how the Boltzmann machine could "pretrain" backpropagation networks, Hinton quipped that [Richard Feynman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Feynman "Richard Feynman") reportedly said: "Listen, buddy, if I could explain it in a couple of minutes, it wouldn't be worth the Nobel Prize." That same year, he received the [VinFuture Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VinFuture_Prize "VinFuture Prize") grand award alongside [Yoshua Bengio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoshua_Bengio "Yoshua Bengio"), [Yann LeCun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yann_LeCun "Yann LeCun"), [Jen-Hsun Huang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jen-Hsun_Huang "Jen-Hsun Huang"), and [Fei-Fei Li](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fei-Fei_Li "Fei-Fei Li") for groundbreaking contributions to [neural networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neural_networks "Neural networks") and [deep learning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deep_learning "Deep learning") algorithms.

German AI researcher [Jürgen Schmidhuber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jürgen_Schmidhuber "Jürgen Schmidhuber") contended that Hinton and others in the field did not appropriately credit existing research, and argued that foundational work by [Paul Werbos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Werbos "Paul Werbos") and [Shun-Ichi Amari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shun-Ichi_Amari "Shun-Ichi Amari") in the 1970s on backpropagation and neural networks was insufficiently acknowledged.

In 2025 he was awarded the [Queen Elizabeth Prize for Engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen_Elizabeth_Prize_for_Engineering "Queen Elizabeth Prize for Engineering") jointly with [Yoshua Bengio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoshua_Bengio "Yoshua Bengio"), [Bill Dally](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bill_Dally "Bill Dally"), [John Hopfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Hopfield "John Hopfield"), [Yann LeCun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yann_LeCun "Yann LeCun"), [Jen-Hsun Huang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jen-Hsun_Huang "Jen-Hsun Huang") and [Fei-Fei Li](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fei-Fei_Li "Fei-Fei Li"). He was also awarded the [King Charles III Coronation Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Charles_III_Coronation_Medal "King Charles III Coronation Medal").
In 2025, he was also named the recipient of the [Sandford Fleming Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sandford_Fleming_Medal "Sandford Fleming Medal"), awarded by the Royal Canadian Institute for Science for excellence in science communication. He received an honorary Doctor of Science degree from [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University "Harvard University") in 2026.

## Views

### Risks of artificial intelligence

| External videos | |
| --- | --- |
| [video icon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Nuvola_apps_kaboodle.svg) [Geoffrey Hinton shares his thoughts on AI's benefits and dangers](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qrvK_KuIeJk), [60 Minutes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/60_Minutes "60 Minutes") YouTube video | |

In 2023, Hinton expressed concerns about the rapid [progress of AI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Progress_of_AI "Progress of AI"). He had previously believed that [artificial general intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_general_intelligence "Artificial general intelligence") (AGI) was "30 to 50 years or even longer away." However, in a March 2023 interview with [CBS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CBS "CBS"), he said that "general-purpose AI" might be fewer than 20 years away and could bring about changes "comparable in scale with the [industrial revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Revolution "Industrial Revolution") or [electricity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrification "Electrification")."

In an interview with *[The New York Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_York_Times "The New York Times")* published on 1 May 2023, Hinton announced his resignation from Google so he could "talk about the dangers of AI without considering how this impacts Google." He noted that a part of him now regrets his life's work.

In early May 2023, Hinton said in an interview with the BBC that AI might soon surpass the information capacity of the human brain. He described some of the risks posed by these chatbots as "quite scary". Hinton explained that chatbots can learn independently and share knowledge, so that whenever one copy acquires new information, it is automatically disseminated to the entire group, allowing AI chatbots to accumulate knowledge far beyond the capacity of any individual. In 2025, he said "My greatest fear is that, in the long run, it'll turn out that these kind of digital beings we're creating are just a better form of intelligence than people. […] We'd no longer be needed. […] If you want to know how it's like not to be the apex intelligence, ask a chicken.

#### Existential risk from AGI

Hinton has expressed concerns about the possibility of an [AI takeover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AI_takeover "AI takeover"), stating that "it's not inconceivable" that AI could "wipe out humanity". Hinton said in 2023 that AI systems capable of [intelligent agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intelligent_agent "Intelligent agent") would be useful for military or economic purposes. He worries that generally intelligent AI systems could "create sub-goals" that are [unaligned](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AI_alignment "AI alignment") with their programmers' interests. He says that AI systems may become [power-seeking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AI_alignment#Power-seeking_and_instrumental_strategies "AI alignment") or prevent themselves from being shut off, not because programmers intended them to, but because those sub-goals are [useful for achieving later goals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instrumental_convergence "Instrumental convergence"). In particular, Hinton says "we have to think hard about how to control" AI systems capable of [self-improvement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intelligence_explosion "Intelligence explosion").

#### Catastrophic misuse

Hinton reports concerns about deliberate misuse of AI by malicious actors, stating that "it is hard to see how you can prevent the bad actors from using [AI] for bad things." In 2017, Hinton called for an international ban on [lethal autonomous weapons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lethal_autonomous_weapon "Lethal autonomous weapon"). In 2025, in an interview, Hinton cited the use of AI by bad actors to create lethal viruses one of the greatest existential threats posed in the short term. "It just requires one crazy guy with a grudge...you can now create new viruses relatively cheaply using AI. And you don't need to be a very skilled molecular biologist to do it."

#### Economic impacts

Hinton was previously optimistic about the economic effects of AI, noting in 2018 that: "The phrase 'artificial general intelligence' carries with it the implication that this sort of single robot is suddenly going to be smarter than you. I don't think it's going to be that. I think more and more of the routine things we do are going to be replaced by AI systems." Hinton had also argued that AGI would not make humans redundant: "[AI in the future is] going to know a lot about what you're probably going to want to do... But it's not going to replace you."

In 2023, however, Hinton became "worried that AI technologies will in time upend the job market" and take away more than just "drudge work". He said in 2024 that the [British government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_government "British government") would have to establish a [universal basic income](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_basic_income "Universal basic income") to deal with the impact of AI on inequality. In Hinton's view, AI will boost productivity and generate more wealth. But unless the government intervenes, it will only make the rich richer and hurt the people who might lose their jobs. "That's going to be very bad for society," he said.

In December 2024, he had become somewhat more pessimistic, saying there was a "10 to 20 per cent chance" that AI would cause human extinction within the next three decades (he had previously suggested a 10% chance, without a timescale). He expressed surprise at the speed with which AI was advancing, and said that most experts expected AI to advance, probably in the next 20 years, to be "smarter than people ... a scary thought. ... So just leaving it to the profit motive of large companies is not going to be sufficient to make sure they develop it safely. The only thing that can force those big companies to do more research on safety is government regulation." Another "godfather of AI", [Yann LeCun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yann_LeCun "Yann LeCun"), disagreed, saying AI "could actually save humanity from extinction".

### Politics

Hinton is a [socialist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialist "Socialist"). He moved from the US to Canada in part due to disillusionment with [Ronald Reagan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Reagan "Ronald Reagan")–era politics and disapproval of military funding of artificial intelligence.

In August 2024, Hinton co-authored a letter with [Yoshua Bengio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoshua_Bengio "Yoshua Bengio"), [Stuart Russell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stuart_J._Russell "Stuart J. Russell"), and [Lawrence Lessig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawrence_Lessig "Lawrence Lessig") in support of [SB 1047](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SB_1047 "SB 1047"), a California AI safety bill that would require companies training models which cost more than US$100 million to perform risk assessments before deployment. They said the legislation was the "bare minimum for effective regulation of this technology."

## Personal life

Hinton's first wife, Rosalind Zalin, died of [ovarian cancer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ovarian_cancer "Ovarian cancer") in 1994; his second wife, Jacqueline "Jackie" Ford, died of [pancreatic cancer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pancreatic_cancer "Pancreatic cancer") in 2018.

Hinton is the great-great-grandson of the mathematician and educator [Mary Everest Boole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Everest_Boole "Mary Everest Boole") and her husband, the logician [George Boole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Boole "George Boole"). George Boole's work eventually became one of the foundations of modern computer science. Another great-great-grandfather of his was the surgeon and author [James Hinton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Hinton_(surgeon) "James Hinton (surgeon)"), who was the father of the mathematician [Charles Howard Hinton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Howard_Hinton "Charles Howard Hinton").

Hinton's father was the [entomologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entomologist "Entomologist") [Howard Hinton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H._E._Hinton "H. E. Hinton"). His middle name comes from another relative, [George Everest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Everest "George Everest"), the [Surveyor General of India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surveyor_General_of_India "Surveyor General of India") after whom the [mountain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Everest "Mount Everest") is named. He is the nephew of the economist [Colin Clark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colin_Clark_(economist) "Colin Clark (economist)"), and nuclear physicist [Joan Hinton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joan_Hinton "Joan Hinton"), one of the two female physicists at the [Manhattan Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manhattan_Project "Manhattan Project"), was his first cousin once removed.

Hinton injured his back at age 19, which makes sitting painful for him. He has dealt with depression throughout his life.
