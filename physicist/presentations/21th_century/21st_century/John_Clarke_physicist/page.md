**John Clarke** (born 10 February 1942) is a British experimental [physicist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physicist "Physicist") and Professor Emeritus at the [University of California, Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Berkeley "University of California, Berkeley"). He is known for his various works on measurement devices based on [superconductivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superconductivity "Superconductivity"). [Steven Girvin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steven_Girvin "Steven Girvin") has called Clarke "the godfather of superconducting electronics."

In the 1980s, Clarke led a research team that included [John M. Martinis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_M._Martinis "John M. Martinis") and [Michel Devoret](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michel_Devoret "Michel Devoret"). Their discoveries in [macroscopic quantum phenomena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macroscopic_quantum_phenomena "Macroscopic quantum phenomena") using the [Josephson effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josephson_effect "Josephson effect") earned them the [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2025_Nobel_Prizes "2025 Nobel Prizes") in 2025.

## Education and career

John Clarke was born on 10 February 1942 in [Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge "Cambridge"), England. He attended [The Perse School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Perse_School "The Perse School"), before embarking on a [Natural Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_Science_Tripos "Natural Science Tripos") degree at [Christ's College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christ's_College,_Cambridge "Christ's College, Cambridge"), [Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge "Cambridge"). He graduated with a [B.A.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B.A. "B.A.") in Physics in 1964, and then studied for a [Ph.D.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ph.D. "Ph.D.") in Physics in the [Royal Society Mond Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Museums_Site "New Museums Site") at the [University of Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Cambridge "University of Cambridge").

In 1965, Clarke became one of the first students to enter the newly founded [Darwin College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darwin_College,_Cambridge "Darwin College, Cambridge"), and was the first president of the Darwin College students' association. While conducting his doctoral work under [Brian Pippard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brian_Pippard "Brian Pippard"), Clarke developed a very sensitive [voltmeter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voltmeter "Voltmeter"), which he later called "SLUG" (Superconducting Low-inductance Undulatory Galvanometer). He obtained his doctorate in 1968. Clarke has said at various times that his work was influenced by Nobel laureate [Brian Josephson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brian_Josephson "Brian Josephson"), who predicted the [Josephson effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josephson_effect "Josephson effect") in 1962 and was also a previous student of Pippard.

After completing his doctorate, Clarke gained a postdoctoral research position at the [University of California, Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Berkeley "University of California, Berkeley"), and subsequently worked at Berkeley for his whole academic career, as Assistant Professor (1969), Associate Professor (1971), and as Professor of Physics (1973–2010). In 1969, Clarke also joined [Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawrence_Berkeley_National_Laboratory "Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory"), eventually retiring as a faculty senior scientist in the Materials Sciences Division in 2010.

Clarke's association with the University of Cambridge continued, after he moved to the United States. In 1972, he was elected a Fellow of Christ's College; in 1989, he was a visiting fellow at [Clare Hall, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clare_Hall,_Cambridge "Clare Hall, Cambridge"), and in 1998 was elected a by-fellow of [Churchill College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Churchill_College,_Cambridge "Churchill College, Cambridge"). Clarke was awarded a [D.Sc.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Science "Doctor of Science") from the University of Cambridge in 2003. He was elected an Honorary Fellow of Christ's College in 1997, and of Darwin College in 2023.

## Research

Clarke's research focuses on [superconductivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superconductivity "Superconductivity") and superconducting electronics, particularly in the development and application of [superconducting quantum interference devices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SQUID "SQUID") (SQUIDs), which are ultrasensitive detectors of [magnetic flux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_flux "Magnetic flux").

In 1985, Clarke, [John M. Martinis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_M._Martinis "John M. Martinis") (his Ph.D. student), and [Michel Devoret](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michel_Devoret "Michel Devoret") (a postdoctoral researcher at the time) demonstrated the quantum behaviour of a [Josephson junction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josephson_effect "Josephson effect"). They showed that at low temperature, a macroscopic electronic state associated with superconductors underwent [quantum tunnelling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_tunnelling "Quantum tunnelling") at zero voltage. The same year, by sending [microwave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microwave "Microwave") pulses of the system, the [resonances showed quantised energy levels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_qubit#Quantized_energy_levels "Phase qubit"). This experiment was the first evidence of [circuit quantum electrodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circuit_quantum_electrodynamics "Circuit quantum electrodynamics"), that would become later the basis for [superconducting quantum computing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superconducting_quantum_computing "Superconducting quantum computing"). The work, which was recognized with the [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics") in 2025, was largely funded by the Office of Basic Energy Sciences in the [United States Department of Energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Energy "United States Department of Energy").

Clarke has also worked in the application of SQUIDs configured as quantum-noise limited amplifiers to search for the [axion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axion "Axion"), a possible component of [dark matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dark_matter "Dark matter").

## Recognition

Clarke obtained an [Alfred P. Sloan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_P._Sloan "Alfred P. Sloan") fellowship (1970) and a [Guggenheim Fellowship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guggenheim_Fellowship "Guggenheim Fellowship") (1977).
