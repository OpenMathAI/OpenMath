**Vladimir Alexandrovich Voevodsky** ([/vɔɪɛˈvɒdski/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English"), [Russian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_language "Russian language"): Влади́мир Алекса́ндрович Воево́дский; 4 June 1966 – 30 September 2017) was a [Russian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russia "Russia")-[American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/America "America") [mathematician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematician "Mathematician"). His work in developing a [homotopy theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homotopy_theory "Homotopy theory") for [algebraic varieties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_varieties "Algebraic varieties") and formulating [motivic cohomology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motivic_cohomology "Motivic cohomology") led to the award of a [Fields Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fields_Medal "Fields Medal") in 2002. He is also known for the proof of the [Milnor conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milnor_conjecture_(K-theory) "Milnor conjecture (K-theory)") and motivic [Bloch–Kato conjectures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bloch–Kato_conjecture "Bloch–Kato conjecture") and for the [univalent foundations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Univalent_foundations "Univalent foundations") of mathematics and [homotopy type theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homotopy_type_theory "Homotopy type theory").

## Early life and education

Vladimir Voevodsky's father, Aleksander Voevodsky, was head of the Laboratory of High Energy Leptons in the Institute for Nuclear Research at the [Russian Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Academy_of_Sciences "Russian Academy of Sciences"). His mother Tatyana was a chemist. Voevodsky attended [Moscow State University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moscow_State_University "Moscow State University") for a while, but was expelled without a diploma for refusing to attend classes and failing academically. He received his [Ph.D.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Philosophy "Doctor of Philosophy") in mathematics from [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University "Harvard University") in 1992 after being recommended without even applying and without a formal college degree, following several independent publications; he was advised there by [David Kazhdan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Kazhdan "David Kazhdan").

While he was a first year undergraduate, he was given a copy of "[Esquisse d'un Programme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Esquisse_d'un_Programme "Esquisse d'un Programme")" (submitted a few months earlier by [Alexander Grothendieck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Grothendieck "Alexander Grothendieck") to [CNRS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CNRS "CNRS")) by his advisor George Shabat. He learned the [French language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language "French language") "with the sole purpose of being able to read this text" and started his research on some of the themes mentioned there.

## Work

Voevodsky's work was in the intersection of [algebraic geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_geometry "Algebraic geometry") with [algebraic topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_topology "Algebraic topology"). Along with [Fabien Morel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fabien_Morel "Fabien Morel"), Voevodsky introduced a [homotopy theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homotopy_theory "Homotopy theory") for [schemes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scheme_(mathematics) "Scheme (mathematics)"). He also formulated what is now believed to be the correct form of [motivic cohomology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motivic_cohomology "Motivic cohomology"), and used this new tool to prove [Milnor's conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milnor's_conjecture "Milnor's conjecture") relating the [Milnor K-theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milnor_K-theory "Milnor K-theory") of a [field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_(mathematics) "Field (mathematics)") to its [étale cohomology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Étale_cohomology "Étale cohomology"). For the above, he received the [Fields Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fields_Medal "Fields Medal") at the 24th [International Congress of Mathematicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Congress_of_Mathematicians "International Congress of Mathematicians") held in [Beijing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beijing "Beijing"), [China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/China "China").

In 1998 he gave a plenary lecture (*A1-Homotopy Theory*) at the International Congress of Mathematicians in [Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berlin "Berlin"). He coauthored (with [Andrei Suslin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrei_Suslin "Andrei Suslin") and [Eric M. Friedlander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eric_M._Friedlander "Eric M. Friedlander")) *Cycles, Transfers and Motivic Homology Theories*, which develops the theory of motivic cohomology in some detail.

From 2002, Voevodsky was a professor at the [Institute for Advanced Study](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_for_Advanced_Study "Institute for Advanced Study") in [Princeton, New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton,_New_Jersey "Princeton, New Jersey").

In January 2009, at an anniversary conference in honor of [Alexander Grothendieck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Grothendieck "Alexander Grothendieck"), held at the [Institut des Hautes Études Scientifiques](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institut_des_Hautes_Études_Scientifiques "Institut des Hautes Études Scientifiques"), Voevodsky announced a proof of the full [Bloch–Kato conjectures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bloch–Kato_conjecture "Bloch–Kato conjecture").

In 2009, he constructed the univalent model of [Martin-Löf type theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin-Löf_type_theory "Martin-Löf type theory") in [simplicial sets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simplicial_set "Simplicial set"). This led to important advances in type theory and in the development of new [univalent foundations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Univalent_foundations "Univalent foundations") of mathematics that Voevodsky worked on in his final years. He worked on a [Rocq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocq "Rocq") (then: *Coq*) library *UniMath* using univalent ideas.

In April 2016, the [University of Gothenburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Gothenburg "University of Gothenburg") awarded an honorary doctorate to Voevodsky.

## Death and legacy

Voevodsky died on 30 September 2017 at his home in [Princeton, New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton,_New_Jersey "Princeton, New Jersey"), aged 51, from an [aneurysm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aneurysm "Aneurysm"). He was survived by his daughters, Diana Yasmine Voevodsky and Natalia Dalia Shalaby.
