**John Edensor Littlewood** (9 June 1885 – 6 September 1977) was a British mathematician. He worked on topics relating to [analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_analysis "Mathematical analysis"), [number theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_theory "Number theory"), and [differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_equation "Differential equation") and had lengthy collaborations with [G. H. Hardy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G._H._Hardy "G. H. Hardy"), [Srinivasa Ramanujan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Srinivasa_Ramanujan "Srinivasa Ramanujan") and [Mary Cartwright](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Cartwright "Mary Cartwright").

## Biography

Littlewood was born on the 9th of June 1885 in [Rochester, Kent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rochester,_Kent "Rochester, Kent"), the eldest son of Edward Thornton Littlewood and Sylvia Maud (née Ackland). In 1892, his father accepted the headmastership of a school in [Wynberg, Cape Town](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wynberg,_Cape_Town "Wynberg, Cape Town"), in South Africa, taking his family there. Littlewood returned to Britain in 1900 to attend [St Paul's School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Paul's_School,_London "St Paul's School, London") in London, studying under [Francis Sowerby Macaulay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Sowerby_Macaulay "Francis Sowerby Macaulay"), an influential [algebraic geometer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_geometry "Algebraic geometry").

In 1903, Littlewood entered the [University of Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Cambridge "University of Cambridge"), studying in [Trinity College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College,_Cambridge "Trinity College, Cambridge"). He spent his first two years preparing for the [Tripos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tripos "Tripos") examinations which qualify undergraduates for a bachelor's degree where he emerged in 1905 as [Senior Wrangler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Senior_Wrangler_(University_of_Cambridge) "Senior Wrangler (University of Cambridge)") bracketed with [James Mercer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Mercer_(mathematician) "James Mercer (mathematician)") (Mercer had already graduated from the [University of Manchester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Manchester "University of Manchester") before attending Cambridge). In 1906, after completing the second part of the Tripos, he started his research under [Ernest Barnes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_Barnes "Ernest Barnes"). One of the problems that Barnes suggested to Littlewood was to prove the [Riemann hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann_hypothesis "Riemann hypothesis"), an assignment at which he did not succeed. He was elected a Fellow of Trinity College in 1908. From October 1907 to June 1910, he worked as a [Richardson Lecturer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richardson_Chair_of_Applied_Mathematics "Richardson Chair of Applied Mathematics") in the [School of Mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/School_of_Mathematics,_University_of_Manchester "School of Mathematics, University of Manchester") at the University of Manchester.
He was elected to the membership of [Manchester Literary and Philosophical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manchester_Literary_and_Philosophical_Society "Manchester Literary and Philosophical Society") on 14 January 1908.

He returned to Cambridge in October 1910, where he remained for the rest of his career. He was appointed [Rouse Ball Professor of Mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rouse_Ball_Professor_of_Mathematics "Rouse Ball Professor of Mathematics") in 1928, retiring in 1950. He was elected a [Fellow of the Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow_of_the_Royal_Society "Fellow of the Royal Society") in 1916, awarded the [Royal Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Medal "Royal Medal") in 1929, the [Sylvester Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sylvester_Medal "Sylvester Medal") in 1943, and the [Copley Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copley_Medal "Copley Medal") in 1958. He was president of the [London Mathematical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Mathematical_Society "London Mathematical Society") from 1941 to 1943 and was awarded the [De Morgan Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Morgan_Medal "De Morgan Medal") in 1938 and the [Senior Berwick Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berwick_Prizes "Berwick Prizes") in 1960.

Littlewood died on 6 September 1977.

## Work

Most of Littlewood's work was in the field of [mathematical analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_analysis "Mathematical analysis"). He began research under the supervision of [Ernest William Barnes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_William_Barnes "Ernest William Barnes"), who suggested that he attempt to prove the [Riemann hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann_hypothesis "Riemann hypothesis"): Littlewood showed that if the Riemann hypothesis is true, then the [prime number theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_number_theorem "Prime number theorem") follows and obtained the error term. This work won him his Trinity fellowship. However, the link between the Riemann hypothesis and the prime number theorem had been known before in Continental Europe, and Littlewood wrote later in his book, *A Mathematician's Miscellany* that his rediscovery of the result did not shed a positive light on the isolated nature of British mathematics at the time.

### Collaboration with G. H. Hardy

Littlewood collaborated for many years with [G. H. Hardy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G._H._Hardy "G. H. Hardy"). Together they devised the [first Hardy–Littlewood conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Hardy–Littlewood_conjecture "First Hardy–Littlewood conjecture"), a strong form of the [twin prime conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twin_prime_conjecture "Twin prime conjecture"), and the [second Hardy–Littlewood conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Hardy–Littlewood_conjecture "Second Hardy–Littlewood conjecture").

#### Ramanujan

He also, with Hardy, identified the work of the Indian mathematician [Srinivasa Ramanujan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Srinivasa_Ramanujan "Srinivasa Ramanujan") as that of a genius and supported him in travelling from India to work at Cambridge. A self-taught mathematician, Ramanujan later became a [Fellow of the Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow_of_the_Royal_Society "Fellow of the Royal Society"), Fellow of [Trinity College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College,_Cambridge "Trinity College, Cambridge"), and widely recognised as on a par with other geniuses such as [Euler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler "Euler") and [Jacobi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Gustav_Jacob_Jacobi "Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi").

### Collaboration with Mary Cartwright

In the late 1930s, as the prospect of war loomed, the [Department of Scientific and Industrial Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Department_of_Scientific_and_Industrial_Research_(United_Kingdom) "Department of Scientific and Industrial Research (United Kingdom)") sought the interest of pure mathematicians in the properties of [non linear differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_equations "Differential equations") that were needed by radio engineers and scientists. The problems appealed to Littlewood and [Mary Cartwright](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Cartwright "Mary Cartwright"), and they worked on them independently during the next 20 years.

The problems that Littlewood and Cartwright worked on concerned [differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_equations "Differential equations") arising out of early research on [radar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radar "Radar"): their work foreshadowed the modern theory of dynamical systems. [Littlewood's 4/3 inequality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Littlewood's_4/3_inequality "Littlewood's 4/3 inequality") on bilinear forms was a forerunner of the later [Grothendieck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grothendieck "Grothendieck") [tensor norm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tensor_norm "Tensor norm") theory.

## Military service WWI – ballistics work

During the [Great War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_World_War "First World War"), Littlewood served in the [Royal Garrison Artillery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Garrison_Artillery "Royal Garrison Artillery") as a second lieutenant. He made highly significant contributions in the field of ballistics.

## Later life

He continued to write papers into his eighties, particularly in analytical areas of what would become the theory of [dynamical systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamical_systems "Dynamical systems").

Littlewood is also remembered for his book of reminiscences, *[A Mathematician's Miscellany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Mathematician's_Miscellany "A Mathematician's Miscellany")* (new edition published in 1986).

Among his PhD students were [Sarvadaman Chowla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarvadaman_Chowla "Sarvadaman Chowla"), [Harold Davenport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Davenport "Harold Davenport"), and [Donald C. Spencer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_C._Spencer "Donald C. Spencer"). Spencer reported that in 1941 when he (Spencer) was about to get on the boat that would take him home to the United States, Littlewood reminded him: "*n*, *n* alpha, *n* beta!" (referring to [Littlewood's conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Littlewood's_conjecture "Littlewood's conjecture")).

Littlewood's collaborative work, carried out by correspondence, covered fields in [Diophantine approximation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diophantine_approximation "Diophantine approximation") and [Waring's problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waring's_problem "Waring's problem"), in particular. In his other work, he collaborated with [Raymond Paley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raymond_Paley "Raymond Paley") on [Littlewood–Paley theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Littlewood–Paley_theory "Littlewood–Paley theory") in [Fourier theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourier_transform "Fourier transform"), and with [Cyril Offord](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyril_Offord "Cyril Offord") in combinatorial work on random sums, in developments that opened up fields that are still intensively studied.

In a 1947 lecture, the Danish mathematician [Harald Bohr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harald_Bohr "Harald Bohr") said, "To illustrate to what extent Hardy and Littlewood in the course of the years came to be considered as the leaders of recent English mathematical research, I may report what an excellent colleague once jokingly said: 'Nowadays, there are only three really great English mathematicians: Hardy, Littlewood, and Hardy–Littlewood.' "

The German mathematician [Edmund Landau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmund_Landau "Edmund Landau") supposed that Littlewood was a pseudonym that Hardy used for his lesser work and "so doubted the existence of Littlewood that he made a special trip to Great Britain to see the man with his own eyes". He visited Cambridge where he saw much of Hardy but nothing of Littlewood and so considered his conjecture to be proven. A similar story was told about [Norbert Wiener](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norbert_Wiener "Norbert Wiener"), who vehemently denied it in his autobiography.

He coined [Littlewood's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Littlewood's_law "Littlewood's law"), which states that individuals can expect "miracles" to happen to them at the rate of about one per month.
