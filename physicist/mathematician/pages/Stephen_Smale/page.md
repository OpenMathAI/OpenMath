**Stephen Smale** (born July 15, 1930) is an American mathematician, known for his research in [topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topology "Topology"), [dynamical systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamical_system "Dynamical system") and [mathematical economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_economics "Mathematical economics"). He was awarded the [Fields Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fields_Medal "Fields Medal") in 1966 and spent more than three decades on the mathematics faculty of the [University of California, Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Berkeley "University of California, Berkeley") (1960–1961 and 1964–1995), where he currently is a professor emeritus, with research interests in [algorithms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algorithms "Algorithms"), [numerical analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numerical_analysis "Numerical analysis") and [global analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_analysis "Global analysis").

## Education and career

Smale was born in [Flint, Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flint,_Michigan "Flint, Michigan") and entered the [University of Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Michigan "University of Michigan") in 1948. Initially, he was placed into an honors [calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calculus "Calculus") sequence taught by [Bob Thrall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_M._Thrall "Robert M. Thrall") and earned himself A's. However, his [sophomore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sophomore_year "Sophomore year") and junior years were marred with mediocre grades, mostly Bs, Cs and even an F in [nuclear physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_physics "Nuclear physics"). Smale obtained his Bachelor of Science degree in 1952. Despite his grades, Smale was accepted as a graduate student at the University of Michigan's mathematics department. Yet again, Smale performed poorly in his first years, earning a C average as a graduate student. When the department chair, [Hildebrandt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theophil_Henry_Hildebrandt "Theophil Henry Hildebrandt"), threatened to kick Smale out, he began to take his studies more seriously. Smale finally earned his [PhD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PhD "PhD") in 1957, under [Raoul Bott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raoul_Bott "Raoul Bott"), beginning his career as an instructor at the [University of Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Chicago "University of Chicago").

Early in his career, Smale was involved in controversy over remarks he made regarding his work habits while proving the higher-dimensional Poincaré conjecture. He said that his best work had been done "on the beaches of Rio." He has been politically active in various movements in the past, such as the [Free Speech movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_Speech_movement "Free Speech movement") and member of the [Fair Play for Cuba Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fair_Play_for_Cuba_Committee "Fair Play for Cuba Committee"). In 1966, having travelled to Moscow under an [NSF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Science_Foundation "National Science Foundation") grant to accept the Fields Medal, he held a press conference there to denounce the [American position in Vietnam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnam_War "Vietnam War"), [Soviet intervention in Hungary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungary–Soviet_Union_relations "Hungary–Soviet Union relations") and Soviet maltreatment of intellectuals. After his return to the US, he was unable to renew the grant. At one time he was [subpoenaed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subpoena "Subpoena") by the [House Un-American Activities Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_Un-American_Activities_Committee "House Un-American Activities Committee").

In 1960, Smale received a [Sloan Research Fellowship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sloan_Research_Fellowship "Sloan Research Fellowship") and was appointed to the [Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Berkeley "University of California, Berkeley") mathematics faculty, moving to a professorship at [Columbia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia_University "Columbia University") the following year. In 1964 he returned to a professorship at Berkeley, where he has spent the main part of his career. He became a professor emeritus at Berkeley in 1995 and took up a post as professor at the [City University of Hong Kong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_University_of_Hong_Kong "City University of Hong Kong"). He also amassed over the years one of the finest private mineral collections in existence. Many of Smale's mineral specimens can be seen in the book *The Smale Collection: Beauty in Natural Crystals*.

From 2003 to 2012, Smale was a professor at the [Toyota Technological Institute at Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toyota_Technological_Institute_at_Chicago "Toyota Technological Institute at Chicago"); starting August 1, 2009, he became a Distinguished University Professor at the [City University of Hong Kong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_University_of_Hong_Kong "City University of Hong Kong").

In 1988, Smale was the recipient of the [Chauvenet Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chauvenet_Prize "Chauvenet Prize") of the [MAA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_Association_of_America "Mathematical Association of America"). In 2007, Smale was awarded the [Wolf Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolf_Prize "Wolf Prize") in mathematics.

## Research

Smale proved that the [oriented diffeomorphism group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffeomorphism_group "Diffeomorphism group") of the two-dimensional sphere has the same [homotopy type](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homotopy_type "Homotopy type") as the [special orthogonal group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_orthogonal_group "Special orthogonal group") of 3 × 3 matrices. Smale's theorem has been reproved and extended a few times, notably to higher dimensions in the form of the [Smale conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smale_conjecture "Smale conjecture"), as well as to other topological types.

In another early work, he studied the [immersions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immersion_(mathematics) "Immersion (mathematics)") of the two-dimensional sphere into Euclidean space. By relating immersion theory to the [algebraic topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_topology "Algebraic topology") of [Stiefel manifolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stiefel_manifold "Stiefel manifold"), he was able to fully clarify when two immersions can be deformed into one another through a family of immersions. Directly from his results it followed that the standard immersion of the sphere into three-dimensional space can be deformed (through immersions) into its negation, which is now known as [sphere eversion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sphere_eversion "Sphere eversion"). He also extended his results to higher-dimensional spheres, and his doctoral student [Morris Hirsch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morris_Hirsch "Morris Hirsch") extended his work to immersions of general [smooth manifolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_manifold "Smooth manifold"). Along with [John Nash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Forbes_Nash "John Forbes Nash")'s work on [isometric immersions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isometric_immersion "Isometric immersion"), the Hirsch–Smale immersion theory was highly influential in [Mikhael Gromov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikhael_Gromov_(mathematician) "Mikhael Gromov (mathematician)")'s early work on development of the [h-principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H-principle "H-principle"), which abstracted and applied their ideas to contexts other than that of immersions.

In the study of [dynamical systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamical_system "Dynamical system"), Smale introduced what is now known as a [Morse–Smale system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morse–Smale_system "Morse–Smale system"). For these dynamical systems, Smale was able to prove [Morse inequalities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morse_inequality "Morse inequality") relating the [cohomology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cohomology "Cohomology") of the underlying space to the dimensions of the [(un)stable manifolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stable_manifold "Stable manifold"). Part of the significance of these results is from Smale's theorem asserting that the [gradient flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gradient_flow "Gradient flow") of any [Morse function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morse_function "Morse function") can be arbitrarily well approximated by a Morse–Smale system without closed orbits. Using these tools, Smale was able to construct *self-indexing* Morse functions, where the value of the function equals its [Morse index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morse_index "Morse index") at any critical point. Using these self-indexing Morse functions as a key tool, Smale resolved the [generalized Poincaré conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalized_Poincaré_conjecture "Generalized Poincaré conjecture") in every dimension greater than four. Building on these works, he also established the more powerful [h-cobordism theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H-cobordism "H-cobordism") the following year, together with the full classification of [simply-connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simply-connected "Simply-connected") smooth five-dimensional manifolds.

Smale also introduced the [horseshoe map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horseshoe_map "Horseshoe map"), inspiring much subsequent research. He also outlined a research program carried out by many others. Smale is also known for injecting [Morse theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morse_theory "Morse theory") into mathematical [economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economics "Economics"), as well as recent explorations of various theories of [computation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computation "Computation").

In 1998 he compiled a list of 18 problems in [mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics "Mathematics") to be solved in the 21st century, known as [Smale's problems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smale's_problems "Smale's problems"). This list was compiled in the spirit of [Hilbert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Hilbert "David Hilbert")'s [famous list of problems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilbert's_problems "Hilbert's problems") produced in 1900. In fact, Smale's list contains some of the original Hilbert problems, including the [Riemann hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann_hypothesis "Riemann hypothesis") and the second half of [Hilbert's sixteenth problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilbert's_sixteenth_problem "Hilbert's sixteenth problem"), both of which are still unsolved. Other famous problems on his list include the [Poincaré conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poincaré_conjecture "Poincaré conjecture") (now a theorem, proved by [Grigori Perelman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grigori_Perelman "Grigori Perelman")), the [P = NP problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P_=_NP_problem "P = NP problem"), and the [Navier–Stokes equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navier–Stokes_existence_and_smoothness "Navier–Stokes existence and smoothness"), all of which have been designated [Millennium Prize Problems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millennium_Prize_Problems "Millennium Prize Problems") by the [Clay Mathematics Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clay_Mathematics_Institute "Clay Mathematics Institute").
