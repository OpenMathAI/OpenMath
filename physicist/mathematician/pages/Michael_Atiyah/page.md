**Sir Michael Francis Atiyah** ([/əˈtiːə/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English"); 22 April 1929 – 11 January 2019) was a British-Lebanese [mathematician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematician "Mathematician") specialising in [geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometry "Geometry"). His contributions include the [Atiyah–Singer index theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atiyah–Singer_index_theorem "Atiyah–Singer index theorem") and co-founding [topological K-theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_K-theory "Topological K-theory"). He was awarded the [Fields Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fields_Medal "Fields Medal") in 1966 and the [Abel Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abel_Prize "Abel Prize") in 2004.

## Early life and education

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/df/TrinityCollegeCamGreatCourt.jpg/250px-TrinityCollegeCamGreatCourt.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:TrinityCollegeCamGreatCourt.jpg)

[Great Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_Great_Court "Trinity Great Court") of [Trinity College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College,_Cambridge "Trinity College, Cambridge"), where Atiyah was a student and later [Master](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master_of_Trinity_College,_Cambridge "Master of Trinity College, Cambridge")

Atiyah was born on 22 April 1929 in [Hampstead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hampstead "Hampstead"), [London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London "London"), England, the son of Jean (née Levens) and [Edward Atiyah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Atiyah "Edward Atiyah"). His mother was Scottish and his father was a Lebanese [Orthodox Christian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Christianity_in_Lebanon "Eastern Orthodox Christianity in Lebanon"). He had two brothers, [Patrick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patrick_Atiyah "Patrick Atiyah") (deceased) and Joe, and a sister, Selma (deceased). Atiyah went to primary school at the Diocesan school in [Khartoum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khartoum "Khartoum"), Sudan (1934–1941), and to secondary school at [Victoria College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victoria_College,_Alexandria "Victoria College, Alexandria") in [Cairo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cairo "Cairo") and [Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandria "Alexandria") (1941–1945); the school was also attended by [European nobility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_nobility "European nobility") displaced by the [Second World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_World_War "Second World War") and some future leaders of Arab nations. He returned to England and [Manchester Grammar School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manchester_Grammar_School "Manchester Grammar School") for his [HSC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higher_School_Certificate_(UK) "Higher School Certificate (UK)") studies (1945–1947) and did his [national service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Service_in_the_United_Kingdom "National Service in the United Kingdom") with the [Royal Electrical and Mechanical Engineers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Electrical_and_Mechanical_Engineers "Royal Electrical and Mechanical Engineers") (1947–1949). His [undergraduate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Undergraduate "Undergraduate") and [postgraduate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postgraduate "Postgraduate") studies took place at [Trinity College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College,_Cambridge "Trinity College, Cambridge") (1949–1955). He was a [doctoral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctoral "Doctoral") student of [William V. D. Hodge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_V._D._Hodge "William V. D. Hodge") and was awarded a doctorate in 1955 for a thesis entitled *Some Applications of Topological Methods in Algebraic Geometry*.

During his time at Cambridge, he was president of [The Archimedeans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Archimedeans "The Archimedeans").

## Career and research

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/28/Fuld_Hall%2C_Institute_for_Advanced_Study%2C_Princeton%2C_NJ.jpg/250px-Fuld_Hall%2C_Institute_for_Advanced_Study%2C_Princeton%2C_NJ.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Fuld_Hall,_Institute_for_Advanced_Study,_Princeton,_NJ.jpg)

The [Institute for Advanced Study](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_for_Advanced_Study "Institute for Advanced Study") in Princeton, where Atiyah was professor from 1969 to 1972

Atiyah spent the academic year 1955–1956 at the [Institute for Advanced Study, Princeton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_for_Advanced_Study,_Princeton "Institute for Advanced Study, Princeton"), then returned to [Cambridge University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_University "Cambridge University"), where he was a research fellow and assistant [lecturer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lecturer "Lecturer") (1957–1958), then a university [lecturer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lecturer "Lecturer") and tutorial [fellow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow "Fellow") at [Pembroke College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pembroke_College,_Cambridge "Pembroke College, Cambridge") (1958–1961). In 1961, he moved to the [University of Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Oxford "University of Oxford"), where he was a [reader](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reader_(academic_rank) "Reader (academic rank)") and [professorial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Professor "Professor") fellow at [St Catherine's College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Catherine's_College,_Oxford "St Catherine's College, Oxford") (1961–1963). He became [Savilian Professor of Geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Savilian_Professor_of_Geometry "Savilian Professor of Geometry") and a professorial fellow of [New College, Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_College,_Oxford "New College, Oxford"), from 1963 to 1969. He took up a three-year professorship at the Institute for Advanced Study in [Princeton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton,_New_Jersey "Princeton, New Jersey") after which he returned to Oxford as a [Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society "Royal Society") Research Professor and professorial fellow of St Catherine's College. He was president of the [London Mathematical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Mathematical_Society "London Mathematical Society") from 1974 to 1976.

> I started out by changing local currency into foreign currency everywhere I travelled as a child and ended up making money. That's when my father realised that I would be a mathematician some day.

— Michael Atiyah

Atiyah was president of the [Pugwash Conferences on Science and World Affairs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pugwash_Conferences_on_Science_and_World_Affairs "Pugwash Conferences on Science and World Affairs") from 1997 to 2002. He also contributed to the foundation of the [InterAcademy Panel on International Issues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/InterAcademy_Panel_on_International_Issues "InterAcademy Panel on International Issues"), the Association of European Academies (ALLEA), and the [European Mathematical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Mathematical_Society "European Mathematical Society") (EMS).

Within the United Kingdom, he was involved in the creation of the [Isaac Newton Institute for Mathematical Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Newton_Institute_for_Mathematical_Sciences "Isaac Newton Institute for Mathematical Sciences") in Cambridge and was its first director (1990–1996). He was [President of the Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_of_the_Royal_Society "President of the Royal Society") (1990–1995), [Master of Trinity College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master_of_Trinity_College,_Cambridge "Master of Trinity College, Cambridge") (1990–1997), [Chancellor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chancellor_(education) "Chancellor (education)") of the [University of Leicester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Leicester "University of Leicester") (1995–2005), and president of the [Royal Society of Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society_of_Edinburgh "Royal Society of Edinburgh") (2005–2008). From 1997 until his death in 2019 he was an honorary professor in the [University of Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Edinburgh "University of Edinburgh"). He was a Trustee of the [James Clerk Maxwell Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Clerk_Maxwell_Foundation "James Clerk Maxwell Foundation").

Atiyah's mathematical collaborators included [Raoul Bott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raoul_Bott "Raoul Bott"), [Friedrich Hirzebruch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Hirzebruch "Friedrich Hirzebruch") and [Isadore Singer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isadore_Singer "Isadore Singer"), and his students included [Graeme Segal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graeme_Segal "Graeme Segal"), [Nigel Hitchin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nigel_Hitchin "Nigel Hitchin"), [Simon Donaldson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simon_Donaldson "Simon Donaldson"), and [Edward Witten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Witten "Edward Witten"). Together with Hirzebruch, he laid the foundations for [topological K-theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_K-theory "Topological K-theory"), an important tool in [algebraic topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_topology "Algebraic topology"), which, informally speaking, describes ways in which spaces can be twisted. His best known result, the [Atiyah–Singer index theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atiyah–Singer_index_theorem "Atiyah–Singer index theorem"), was proved with Singer in 1963 and is used in counting the number of independent solutions to [differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_equation "Differential equation"). Some of his more recent work was inspired by [theoretical physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theoretical_physics "Theoretical physics"), in particular [instantons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instanton "Instanton") and [monopoles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monopole_(mathematics) "Monopole (mathematics)"), which are responsible for some corrections in [quantum field theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_field_theory "Quantum field theory"). He was awarded the [Fields Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fields_Medal "Fields Medal") in 1966 and the [Abel Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abel_Prize "Abel Prize") in 2004.

The seven volumes of Atiyah's collected papers include most of his work, except for his commutative algebra textbook; the first five volumes are divided thematically and the sixth and seventh arranged by date.

### Algebraic geometry (1952–1958)

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/89/Twisted_cubic_curve.png/250px-Twisted_cubic_curve.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Twisted_cubic_curve.png)

A [twisted cubic curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twisted_cubic_curve "Twisted cubic curve"), the subject of Atiyah's first paper

Atiyah's early papers on algebraic geometry (and some general papers) are reprinted in the first volume of his collected works.

As an undergraduate Atiyah was interested in classical projective geometry, and wrote his first paper: a short note on [twisted cubics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twisted_cubics "Twisted cubics"). He started research under [W. V. D. Hodge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W._V._D._Hodge "W. V. D. Hodge") and won the [Smith's prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smith's_prize "Smith's prize") for 1954 for a [sheaf-theoretic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheaf_(mathematics) "Sheaf (mathematics)") approach to [ruled surfaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruled_surface "Ruled surface"), which encouraged Atiyah to continue in mathematics, rather than switch to his other interests—architecture and archaeology.
His PhD thesis with Hodge was on a sheaf-theoretic approach to [Solomon Lefschetz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solomon_Lefschetz "Solomon Lefschetz")'s theory of integrals of the second kind on algebraic varieties, and resulted in an invitation to visit the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton for a year. While in Princeton he classified [vector bundles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_bundle "Vector bundle") on an [elliptic curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptic_curve "Elliptic curve") (extending [Alexander Grothendieck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Grothendieck "Alexander Grothendieck")'s classification of vector bundles on a genus 0 curve), by showing that any vector bundle is a sum of (essentially unique) indecomposable vector bundles, and then showing that the space of indecomposable vector bundles of given degree and positive dimension can be identified with the elliptic curve. He also studied double points on surfaces, giving the first example of a [flop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flop_(algebraic_geometry) "Flop (algebraic geometry)"), a special birational transformation of [3-folds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3-fold "3-fold") that was later heavily used in [Shigefumi Mori](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shigefumi_Mori "Shigefumi Mori")'s work on [minimal models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimal_model_(birational_geometry) "Minimal model (birational geometry)") for 3-folds. Atiyah's flop can also be used to show that the universal marked family of [K3 surfaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K3_surface "K3 surface") is not [Hausdorff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hausdorff_space "Hausdorff space").

### K-theory (1959–1974)

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d9/M%C3%B6bius_strip.jpg/250px-M%C3%B6bius_strip.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Möbius_strip.jpg)

A [Möbius band](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Möbius_band "Möbius band") is the simplest non-trivial example of a [vector bundle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_bundle "Vector bundle").

Atiyah's works on [K-theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K-theory "K-theory"), including his book on K-theory are reprinted in volume 2 of his collected works.

The simplest nontrivial example of a vector bundle is the [Möbius band](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Möbius_band "Möbius band") (pictured on the right): a strip of paper with a twist in it, which represents a rank 1 vector bundle over a circle (the circle in question being the centerline of the Möbius band). K-theory is a tool for working with higher-dimensional analogues of this example, or in other words for describing higher-dimensional twistings: elements of the K-group of a space are represented by vector bundles over it, so the Möbius band represents an element of the K-group of a circle.

Topological K-theory was discovered by Atiyah and [Friedrich Hirzebruch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Hirzebruch "Friedrich Hirzebruch") who were inspired by Grothendieck's proof of the [Grothendieck–Riemann–Roch theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grothendieck–Riemann–Roch_theorem "Grothendieck–Riemann–Roch theorem") and Bott's work on the [periodicity theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bott_periodicity_theorem "Bott periodicity theorem"). This paper only discussed the zeroth K-group; they shortly after extended it to K-groups of all degrees, giving the first (nontrivial) example of a [generalized cohomology theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalized_cohomology_theory "Generalized cohomology theory").

Several results showed that the newly introduced K-theory was in some ways more powerful than ordinary cohomology theory. Atiyah and Todd used K-theory to improve the lower bounds found using ordinary cohomology by Borel and Serre for the [James number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_number?action=edit&redlink=1 "James number"), describing when a map from a complex [Stiefel manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stiefel_manifold "Stiefel manifold") to a sphere has a cross section. ([Adams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._Frank_Adams "J. Frank Adams") and Grant-Walker later showed that the bound found by Atiyah and Todd was best possible.) Atiyah and Hirzebruch used K-theory to explain some relations between [Steenrod operations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steenrod_operation "Steenrod operation") and [Todd classes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Todd_class "Todd class") that Hirzebruch had noticed a few years before. The original solution of the [Hopf invariant one problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hopf_invariant_one_problem "Hopf invariant one problem") operations by J. F. Adams was very long and complicated, using secondary cohomology operations. Atiyah showed how primary operations in K-theory could be used to give a short solution taking only a few lines, and in joint work with Adams also proved analogues of the result at odd primes.

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/09/Atiyah-Hirzebruch.jpeg/250px-Atiyah-Hirzebruch.jpeg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Atiyah-Hirzebruch.jpeg)

> "Algebra is the offer made by the devil to the mathematician. The devil says: `I will give you this powerful machine, it will answer any question you like. All you need to do is give me your soul: give up geometry and you will have this marvellous machine."

He introduced the [J-group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J-group "J-group") *J*(*X*) of a finite complex *X*, defined as the group of stable fiber homotopy equivalence classes of [sphere bundles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sphere_bundle "Sphere bundle"); this was later studied in detail by [J. F. Adams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._F._Adams "J. F. Adams") in a series of papers, leading to the [Adams conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adams_conjecture "Adams conjecture").

With Hirzebruch he extended the [Grothendieck–Riemann–Roch theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grothendieck–Riemann–Roch_theorem "Grothendieck–Riemann–Roch theorem") to complex analytic embeddings, and in a related paper they showed that the [Hodge conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hodge_conjecture "Hodge conjecture") for integral cohomology is false. The Hodge conjecture for rational cohomology is, as of 2008, a major unsolved problem.

The [Bott periodicity theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bott_periodicity_theorem "Bott periodicity theorem") was a central theme in Atiyah's work on K-theory, and he repeatedly returned to it, reworking the proof several times to understand it better. With Bott he worked out an elementary proof, and gave another version of it in his book. With Bott and [Shapiro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Shapiro "Alexander Shapiro") he analysed the relation of Bott periodicity to the periodicity of [Clifford algebras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clifford_algebras "Clifford algebras"); although this paper did not have a proof of the periodicity theorem, a proof along similar lines was shortly afterwards found by R. Wood. He found a proof of several generalizations using [elliptic operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptic_operator "Elliptic operator"); this new proof used an idea that he used to give a particularly short and easy proof of Bott's original periodicity theorem.

### Index theory (1963–1984)

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f2/Isadore_Singer_1977.jpeg/250px-Isadore_Singer_1977.jpeg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Isadore_Singer_1977.jpeg)

[Isadore Singer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isadore_Singer "Isadore Singer") (in 1977), who worked with Atiyah on index theory

Atiyah's work on index theory is reprinted in volumes 3 and 4 of his collected works.

The index of a differential operator is closely related to the number of independent solutions (more precisely, it is the differences of the numbers of independent solutions of the differential operator and its adjoint). There are many hard and fundamental problems in mathematics that can easily be reduced to the problem of finding the number of independent solutions of some differential operator, so if one has some means of finding the index of a differential operator these problems can often be solved. This is what the Atiyah–Singer index theorem does: it gives a formula for the index of certain differential operators, in terms of topological invariants that look quite complicated but are in practice usually straightforward to calculate.

Several deep theorems, such as the [Hirzebruch–Riemann–Roch theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hirzebruch–Riemann–Roch_theorem "Hirzebruch–Riemann–Roch theorem"), are special cases of the Atiyah–Singer index theorem. In fact the index theorem gave a more powerful result, because its proof applied to all compact complex manifolds, while Hirzebruch's proof only worked for projective manifolds. There were also many new applications: a typical one is calculating the dimensions of the moduli spaces of instantons. The index theorem can also be run "in reverse": the index is obviously an integer, so the formula for it must also give an integer, which sometimes gives subtle integrality conditions on invariants of manifolds. A typical example of this is [Rochlin's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rochlin's_theorem "Rochlin's theorem"), which follows from the index theorem.

> The most useful piece of advice I would give to a mathematics student is always to suspect an impressive sounding Theorem if it does not have a special case which is *both* simple *and* non-trivial.

— Michael Atiyah

The index problem for [elliptic differential operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptic_differential_operator "Elliptic differential operator") was posed in 1959 by [Gel'fand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Israil_Gelfand "Israil Gelfand"). He noticed the homotopy invariance of the index, and asked for a formula for it by means of [topological invariants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_invariant "Topological invariant"). Some of the motivating examples included the [Riemann–Roch theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann–Roch_theorem "Riemann–Roch theorem") and its generalization the [Hirzebruch–Riemann–Roch theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hirzebruch–Riemann–Roch_theorem "Hirzebruch–Riemann–Roch theorem"), and the [Hirzebruch signature theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hirzebruch_signature_theorem "Hirzebruch signature theorem"). [Hirzebruch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Hirzebruch "Friedrich Hirzebruch") and [Borel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armand_Borel "Armand Borel") had proved the integrality of the [Â genus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Â_genus "Â genus") of a spin manifold, and Atiyah suggested that this integrality could be explained if it were the index of the [Dirac operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirac_operator "Dirac operator") (which was rediscovered by Atiyah and Singer in 1961).

The first announcement of the Atiyah–Singer theorem was their 1963 paper. The proof sketched in this announcement was inspired by Hirzebruch's proof of the [Hirzebruch–Riemann–Roch theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hirzebruch–Riemann–Roch_theorem "Hirzebruch–Riemann–Roch theorem") and was never published by them, though it is described in the book by Palais. Their first published proof was more similar to Grothendieck's proof of the [Grothendieck–Riemann–Roch theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grothendieck–Riemann–Roch_theorem "Grothendieck–Riemann–Roch theorem"), replacing the [cobordism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cobordism "Cobordism") theory of the first proof with [K-theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K-theory "K-theory"), and they used this approach to give proofs of various generalizations in a sequence of papers from 1968 to 1971.

Instead of just one elliptic operator, one can consider a family of elliptic operators parameterized by some space *Y*. In this case the index is an element of the K theory of *Y*, rather than an integer. If the operators in the family are real, then the index lies in the real K theory of *Y*. This gives a little extra information, as the map from the real K theory of *Y* to the [complex K-theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_K-theory "Complex K-theory") is not always injective.

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/1f/Graeme_Segal.jpeg/250px-Graeme_Segal.jpeg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Graeme_Segal.jpeg)

Atiyah's former student [Graeme Segal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graeme_Segal "Graeme Segal") (in 1982), who worked with Atiyah on [equivariant K-theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K-theory "K-theory")

With Bott, Atiyah found an analogue of the [Lefschetz fixed-point formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lefschetz_fixed-point_formula "Lefschetz fixed-point formula") for elliptic operators, giving the Lefschetz number of an endomorphism of an [elliptic complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptic_complex "Elliptic complex") in terms of a sum over the fixed points of the endomorphism. As special cases their formula included the [Weyl character formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weyl_character_formula "Weyl character formula"), and several new results about elliptic curves with complex multiplication, some of which were initially disbelieved by experts.
Atiyah and Segal combined this fixed point theorem with the index theorem as follows.
If there is a compact [group action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_(mathematics) "Group action (mathematics)") of a group *G* on the compact manifold *X*, commuting with the elliptic operator, then one can replace ordinary K-theory in the index theorem with [equivariant K-theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equivariant_algebraic_K-theory "Equivariant algebraic K-theory").
For trivial groups *G* this gives the index theorem, and for a finite group *G* acting with isolated fixed points it gives the Atiyah–Bott fixed point theorem. In general it gives the index as a sum over fixed point submanifolds of the group *G*.

Atiyah solved a problem asked independently by [Hörmander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lars_Hörmander "Lars Hörmander") and Gel'fand, about whether complex powers of analytic functions define [distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_(mathematics) "Distribution (mathematics)"). Atiyah used [Hironaka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heisuke_Hironaka "Heisuke Hironaka")'s resolution of singularities to answer this affirmatively. An ingenious and elementary solution was found at about the same time by [J. Bernstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._Bernstein "J. Bernstein"), and discussed by Atiyah.

As an application of the equivariant index theorem, Atiyah and Hirzebruch showed that manifolds with effective circle actions have vanishing [Â-genus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Â-genus "Â-genus"). (Lichnerowicz showed that if a manifold has a metric of positive scalar curvature then the Â-genus vanishes.)

With [Elmer Rees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elmer_Rees "Elmer Rees"), Atiyah studied the problem of the relation between topological and holomorphic vector bundles on projective space. They solved the simplest unknown case, by showing that all rank 2 vector bundles over projective 3-space have a holomorphic structure. [Horrocks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geoffrey_Horrocks_(mathematician) "Geoffrey Horrocks (mathematician)") had previously found some non-trivial examples of such vector bundles, which were later used by Atiyah in his study of instantons on the 4-sphere.

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c8/Raoul_Bott_1986.jpeg/250px-Raoul_Bott_1986.jpeg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Raoul_Bott_1986.jpeg)

[Raoul Bott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raoul_Bott "Raoul Bott"), who worked with Atiyah on fixed point formulas and several other topics

Atiyah, Bott and [Vijay K. Patodi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vijay_Kumar_Patodi "Vijay Kumar Patodi") gave a new proof of the index theorem using the [heat equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_equation "Heat equation").

If the [manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manifold "Manifold") is allowed to have boundary, then some restrictions must be put on the domain of the elliptic operator in order to ensure a finite index. These conditions can be local (like demanding that the sections in the domain vanish at the boundary) or more complicated global conditions (like requiring that the sections in the domain solve some differential equation). The local case was worked out by Atiyah and Bott, but they showed that many interesting operators (e.g., the [signature operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signature_operator "Signature operator")) do not admit local boundary conditions. To handle these operators, Atiyah, Patodi and Singer introduced global boundary conditions equivalent to attaching a cylinder to the manifold along the boundary and then restricting the domain to those sections that are square integrable along the cylinder, and also introduced the [Atiyah–Patodi–Singer eta invariant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atiyah–Patodi–Singer_eta_invariant "Atiyah–Patodi–Singer eta invariant"). This resulted in a series of papers on spectral asymmetry, which were later unexpectedly used in [theoretical physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theoretical_physics "Theoretical physics"), in particular in Witten's work on anomalies.

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7b/Schlierenfoto_Mach_1-2_Pfeilfl%C3%BCgel_-_NASA.jpg/250px-Schlierenfoto_Mach_1-2_Pfeilfl%C3%BCgel_-_NASA.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Schlierenfoto_Mach_1-2_Pfeilflügel_-_NASA.jpg)

The lacunas discussed by Petrovsky, Atiyah, Bott and Gårding are similar to the spaces between shockwaves of a supersonic object.

The fundamental solutions of linear [hyperbolic partial differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_partial_differential_equation "Hyperbolic partial differential equation") often have [Petrovsky lacunas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petrovsky_lacuna "Petrovsky lacuna"): regions where they vanish identically. These were studied in 1945 by [I. G. Petrovsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I._G._Petrovsky "I. G. Petrovsky"), who found topological conditions describing which regions were lacunas.
In collaboration with Bott and [Lars Gårding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lars_Gårding "Lars Gårding"), Atiyah wrote three papers updating and generalizing Petrovsky's work.

Atiyah showed how to extend the index theorem to some non-compact manifolds, acted on by a discrete group with compact quotient. The kernel of the elliptic operator is in general infinite-dimensional in this case, but it is possible to get a finite index using the dimension of a module over a [von Neumann algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Von_Neumann_algebra "Von Neumann algebra"); this index is in general real rather than integer valued. This version is called the *L2 index theorem,* and was used by Atiyah and Schmid to give a geometric construction, using square integrable harmonic spinors, of Harish-Chandra's [discrete series representations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_series_representation "Discrete series representation") of [semisimple Lie groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semisimple_Lie_group "Semisimple Lie group"). In the course of this work they found a more elementary proof of Harish-Chandra's fundamental theorem on the local integrability of characters of Lie groups.

With H. Donnelly and I. Singer, he extended Hirzebruch's formula (relating the signature defect at cusps of Hilbert modular surfaces to values of L-functions) from real quadratic fields to all totally real fields.

### Gauge theory (1977–1985)

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/bf/Camposcargas.svg/250px-Camposcargas.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Camposcargas.svg)

On the left, two nearby monopoles of the same polarity repel each other, and on the right two nearby monopoles of opposite polarity form a [dipole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dipole "Dipole"). These are abelian monopoles; the non-abelian ones studied by Atiyah are more complicated.

Many of his papers on gauge theory and related topics are reprinted in volume 5 of his collected works. A common theme of these papers is the study of moduli spaces of solutions to certain [non-linear partial differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-linear_partial_differential_equation "Non-linear partial differential equation"), in particular the equations for instantons and monopoles. This often involves finding a subtle correspondence between solutions of two seemingly quite different equations. An early example of this which Atiyah used repeatedly is the [Penrose transform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penrose_transform "Penrose transform"), which can sometimes convert solutions of a non-linear equation over some real manifold into solutions of some linear holomorphic equations over a different complex manifold.

In a series of papers with several authors, Atiyah classified all instantons on 4-dimensional Euclidean space. It is more convenient to classify instantons on a sphere as this is compact, and this is essentially equivalent to classifying instantons on Euclidean space as this is conformally equivalent to a sphere and the equations for instantons are conformally invariant. With Hitchin and Singer he calculated the dimension of the moduli space of irreducible self-dual connections (instantons) for any principal bundle over a compact 4-dimensional Riemannian manifold (the [Atiyah–Hitchin–Singer theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atiyah–Hitchin–Singer_theorem "Atiyah–Hitchin–Singer theorem")). For example, the dimension of the space of SU2 instantons of rank *k*>0 is 8*k*−3. To do this they used the Atiyah–Singer index theorem to calculate the dimension of the tangent space of the moduli space at a point; the tangent space is essentially the space of solutions of an elliptic differential operator, given by the linearization of the non-linear Yang–Mills equations. These moduli spaces were later used by Donaldson to construct his [invariants of 4-manifolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donaldson_invariant "Donaldson invariant").
Atiyah and Ward used the Penrose correspondence to reduce the classification of all instantons on the 4-sphere to a problem in algebraic geometry. With Hitchin he used ideas of Horrocks to solve this problem, giving the [ADHM construction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ADHM_construction "ADHM construction") of all instantons on a sphere; Manin and Drinfeld found the same construction at the same time, leading to a joint paper by all four authors. Atiyah reformulated this construction using [quaternions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quaternion "Quaternion") and wrote up a leisurely account of this classification of instantons on Euclidean space as a book.

> The mathematical problems that have been solved or techniques that have arisen out of physics in the past have been the lifeblood of mathematics.

— Michael Atiyah

Atiyah's work on instanton moduli spaces was used in Donaldson's work on [Donaldson theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donaldson_theory "Donaldson theory"). Donaldson showed that the moduli space of (degree 1) instantons over a compact simply connected [4-manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/4-manifold "4-manifold") with positive definite intersection form can be compactified to give a cobordism between the manifold and a sum of copies of complex projective space. He deduced from this that the intersection form must be a sum of one-dimensional ones, which led to several spectacular applications to smooth 4-manifolds, such as the existence of non-equivalent [smooth structures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_structure "Smooth structure") on 4-dimensional Euclidean space. Donaldson went on to use the other moduli spaces studied by Atiyah to define [Donaldson invariants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donaldson_invariant "Donaldson invariant"), which revolutionized the study of smooth 4-manifolds, and showed that they were more subtle than smooth manifolds in any other dimension, and also quite different from topological 4-manifolds. Atiyah described some of these results in a survey talk.

[Green's functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green's_function "Green's function") for linear partial differential equations can often be found by using the [Fourier transform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourier_transform "Fourier transform") to convert this into an algebraic problem. Atiyah used a non-linear version of this idea. He used the Penrose transform to convert the Green's function for the conformally invariant Laplacian into a complex analytic object, which turned out to be essentially the diagonal embedding of the Penrose twistor space into its square. This allowed him to find an explicit formula for the conformally invariant Green's function on a 4-manifold.

In his paper with Jones, he studied the topology of the moduli space of SU(2) instantons over a 4-sphere. They showed that the natural map from this moduli space to the space of all connections induces epimorphisms of [homology groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homology_group "Homology group") in a certain range of dimensions, and suggested that it might induce isomorphisms of homology groups in the same range of dimensions. This became known as the [Atiyah–Jones conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atiyah–Jones_conjecture "Atiyah–Jones conjecture"), and was later proved by several mathematicians.

Harder and [M. S. Narasimhan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M._S._Narasimhan "M. S. Narasimhan") described the cohomology of the [moduli spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moduli_space "Moduli space") of [stable vector bundles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stable_vector_bundle "Stable vector bundle") over [Riemann surfaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann_surface "Riemann surface") by counting the number of points of the moduli spaces over finite fields, and then using the Weil conjectures to recover the cohomology over the complex numbers.
Atiyah and [R. Bott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R._Bott "R. Bott") used [Morse theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morse_theory "Morse theory") and the [Yang–Mills equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yang–Mills_equation "Yang–Mills equation") over a [Riemann surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann_surface "Riemann surface") to reproduce and extending the results of Harder and Narasimhan.

An old result due to [Schur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Issai_Schur "Issai Schur") and Horn states that the set of possible diagonal vectors of an Hermitian matrix with given eigenvalues is the convex hull of all the permutations of the eigenvalues. Atiyah proved a generalization of this that applies to all compact [symplectic manifolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symplectic_manifold "Symplectic manifold") acted on by a torus, showing that the image of the manifold under the moment map is a convex polyhedron, and with Pressley gave a related generalization to infinite-dimensional loop groups.

Duistermaat and Heckman found a striking formula, saying that the push-forward of the [Liouville measure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liouville_measure "Liouville measure") of a [moment map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moment_map "Moment map") for a torus action is given exactly by the stationary phase approximation (which is in general just an asymptotic expansion rather than exact). Atiyah and Bott showed that this could be deduced from a more general formula in [equivariant cohomology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equivariant_cohomology "Equivariant cohomology"), which was a consequence of well-known [localization theorems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Localization_formula_for_equivariant_cohomology "Localization formula for equivariant cohomology"). Atiyah showed that the moment map was closely related to [geometric invariant theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric_invariant_theory "Geometric invariant theory"), and this idea was later developed much further by his student [F. Kirwan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F._Kirwan "F. Kirwan"). Witten shortly after applied the [Duistermaat–Heckman formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duistermaat–Heckman_formula "Duistermaat–Heckman formula") to loop spaces and showed that this formally gave the Atiyah–Singer index theorem for the Dirac operator; this idea was lectured on by Atiyah.

With Hitchin he worked on [magnetic monopoles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_monopole "Magnetic monopole"), and studied their scattering using an idea of [Nick Manton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nick_Manton "Nick Manton"). His book with Hitchin gives a detailed description of their work on [magnetic monopoles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_monopoles "Magnetic monopoles"). The main theme of the book is a study of a moduli space of [magnetic monopoles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_monopoles "Magnetic monopoles"); this has a natural Riemannian metric, and a key point is that this metric is complete and [hyperkähler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperkähler "Hyperkähler"). The metric is then used to study the scattering of two monopoles, using a suggestion of N. Manton that the geodesic flow on the moduli space is the low energy approximation to the scattering. For example, they show that a head-on collision between two monopoles results in 90-degree scattering, with the direction of scattering depending on the relative phases of the two monopoles. He also studied monopoles on hyperbolic space.

Atiyah showed that instantons in 4 dimensions can be identified with instantons in 2 dimensions, which are much easier to handle. There is of course a catch: in going from 4 to 2 dimensions the structure group of the gauge theory changes from a finite-dimensional group to an infinite-dimensional loop group. This gives another example where the moduli spaces of solutions of two apparently unrelated nonlinear partial differential equations turn out to be essentially the same.

Atiyah and Singer found that anomalies in quantum field theory could be interpreted in terms of index theory of the Dirac operator; this idea later became widely used by physicists.

### Later work (1986–2019)

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/d/dd/Edward_Witten_at_Harvard.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail_unscaled)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Edward_Witten_at_Harvard.jpg)

[Edward Witten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Witten "Edward Witten"), whose work on invariants of manifolds and [topological quantum field theories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_quantum_field_theories "Topological quantum field theories") was influenced by Atiyah

Many of the papers in the 6th volume of his collected works are surveys, obituaries, and general talks. Atiyah continued to publish subsequently, including several surveys, a popular book, and another paper with [Segal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graeme_Segal "Graeme Segal") on [twisted K-theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twisted_K-theory "Twisted K-theory").

One paper is a detailed study of the [Dedekind eta function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dedekind_eta_function "Dedekind eta function") from the point of view of topology and the index theorem.

Several of his papers from around this time study the connections between [quantum field theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_field_theory "Quantum field theory"), [knots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knot_theory "Knot theory"), and [Donaldson theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donaldson_theory "Donaldson theory"). He introduced the concept of a [topological quantum field theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_quantum_field_theory "Topological quantum field theory"), inspired by Witten's work and Segal's definition of a conformal field theory. His book "The Geometry and Physics of Knots" describes the new [knot invariants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knot_invariant "Knot invariant") found by [Vaughan Jones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaughan_Jones "Vaughan Jones") and [Edward Witten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Witten "Edward Witten") in terms of topological quantum field theories, and his paper with L. Jeffrey explains Witten's Lagrangian giving the [Donaldson invariants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donaldson_invariant "Donaldson invariant").

He studied [skyrmions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skyrmion "Skyrmion") with Nick Manton, finding a relation with [magnetic monopoles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_monopoles "Magnetic monopoles") and [instantons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instanton "Instanton"), and giving a conjecture for the structure of the [moduli space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moduli_space "Moduli space") of two [skyrmions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skyrmions "Skyrmions") as a certain [subquotient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subquotient "Subquotient") of complex [projective 3-space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_3-space "Projective 3-space").

Several papers were inspired by a question of [Jonathan Robbins](https://research-information.bristol.ac.uk/en/persons/jonathan-m-robbins(60101278-f877-4e14-946f-262f3b95d5ae).html) (called the [Berry–Robbins problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berry–Robbins_problem "Berry–Robbins problem")), who asked if there is a map from the configuration space of *n* points in 3-space to the flag manifold of the unitary group. Atiyah gave an affirmative answer to this question, but felt his solution was too computational and studied a conjecture that would give a more natural solution. He also related the question to [Nahm's equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nahm's_equation "Nahm's equation"), and introduced the [Atiyah conjecture on configurations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atiyah_conjecture_on_configurations "Atiyah conjecture on configurations").

> But for most practical purposes, you just use the classical groups. The exceptional Lie groups are just there to show you that the theory is a bit bigger; it is pretty rare that they ever turn up.

— Michael Atiyah

With [Juan Maldacena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juan_Maldacena "Juan Maldacena") and [Cumrun Vafa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cumrun_Vafa "Cumrun Vafa"), and [E. Witten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E._Witten "E. Witten") he described the dynamics of [M-theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M-theory "M-theory") on [manifolds with G2 holonomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joyce_manifold "Joyce manifold"). These papers seem to be the first time that Atiyah worked on exceptional Lie groups.

In his papers with [M. Hopkins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_J._Hopkins "Michael J. Hopkins") and G. Segal he returned to his earlier interest of K-theory, describing some twisted forms of K-theory with applications in [theoretical physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theoretical_physics "Theoretical physics").

In October 2016, he claimed a short proof of the non-existence of [complex structures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_manifold "Complex manifold") on the 6-sphere. His proof, like many predecessors, is considered flawed by the mathematical community, even after the proof was rewritten in a revised form.

At the 2018 [Heidelberg Laureate Forum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klaus_Tschira_Foundation "Klaus Tschira Foundation"), he claimed to have solved the [Riemann hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann_hypothesis "Riemann hypothesis"), [Hilbert's eighth problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilbert's_eighth_problem "Hilbert's eighth problem"), [by contradiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proof_by_contradiction "Proof by contradiction") using the [fine-structure constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fine-structure_constant "Fine-structure constant"). Again, the proof did not hold up and the hypothesis remains one of the six unsolved [Millennium Prize Problems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millennium_Prize_Problems "Millennium Prize Problems") in mathematics, as of 2025.

## Awards and honours

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/73/Royal_Society_20040420.jpg/250px-Royal_Society_20040420.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Royal_Society_20040420.jpg)

The premises of the [Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society "Royal Society"), where Atiyah was president from 1990 to 1995

In 1966, when he was thirty-seven years old, he was awarded the [Fields Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fields_Medal "Fields Medal"), for his work in developing K-theory, a generalized [Lefschetz fixed-point theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lefschetz_fixed-point_theorem "Lefschetz fixed-point theorem") and the Atiyah–Singer theorem, for which he also won the [Abel Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abel_Prize "Abel Prize") jointly with [Isadore Singer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isadore_Singer "Isadore Singer") in 2004.
Among other prizes he has received are the [Royal Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Medal "Royal Medal") of the [Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society "Royal Society") in 1968, the [De Morgan Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Morgan_Medal "De Morgan Medal") of the [London Mathematical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Mathematical_Society "London Mathematical Society") in 1980, the [Antonio Feltrinelli Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonio_Feltrinelli_Prize "Antonio Feltrinelli Prize") from the [Accademia Nazionale dei Lincei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accademia_Nazionale_dei_Lincei "Accademia Nazionale dei Lincei") in 1981, the [King Faisal International Prize for Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Faisal_International_Prize_for_Science "King Faisal International Prize for Science") in 1987, the [Copley Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copley_Medal "Copley Medal") of the Royal Society in 1988, the [Benjamin Franklin Medal for Distinguished Achievement in the Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benjamin_Franklin_Medal_for_Distinguished_Achievement_in_the_Sciences "Benjamin Franklin Medal for Distinguished Achievement in the Sciences") of the [American Philosophical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Philosophical_Society "American Philosophical Society") in 1993, the Jawaharlal Nehru Birth Centenary Medal
of the [Indian National Science Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_National_Science_Academy "Indian National Science Academy") in 1993, the [President's Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President's_Medal_of_the_IOP "President's Medal of the IOP") from the [Institute of Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_of_Physics "Institute of Physics") in 2008, the [Grande Médaille](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grande_Médaille "Grande Médaille") of the [French Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Academy_of_Sciences "French Academy of Sciences") in 2010 and the Grand Officier of the [French Légion d'honneur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legion_of_Honour "Legion of Honour") in 2011.

He was elected a foreign member of the [National Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_National_Academy_of_Sciences "United States National Academy of Sciences"), the [American Academy of Arts and Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Academy_of_Arts_and_Sciences "American Academy of Arts and Sciences") (1969), the [Académie des "Chinese Academy of Sciences"), the [Australian Academy of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_Academy_of_Science "Australian Academy of Science"), the [Russian Academy of Atiyah was made a [Knight Bachelor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knight_Bachelor "Knight Bachelor") in 1983 and made a member of the [Order of Merit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_Merit_(Commonwealth) "Order of Merit (Commonwealth)") in 1992.

The Michael Atiyah building at the [University of Leicester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Leicester "University of Leicester")
and the Michael Atiyah Chair in Mathematical Sciences at the [American University of Beirut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_University_of_Beirut "American University of Beirut") were named after him.

## Personal life

Atiyah married Lily Brown on 30 July 1955, with whom he had three sons, John, David and Robin. Atiyah's eldest son John died on 24 June 2002 while on a walking holiday in the [Pyrenees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyrenees "Pyrenees") with his wife Maj-Lis.

Lily Atiyah died on 13 March 2018 at the age of 90 while Sir Michael Atiyah died less than a year later on 11 January 2019, aged 89.
